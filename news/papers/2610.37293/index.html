Foundations of quantum mechanics · Experiment

Experimental evidence for quantum Brownian motion in electron-metal decoherence

Wayne Cheng-Wei Huang; Zilin Chen; Herman Batelaan

arXiv:2610.37293v1 ↗PDF ↗Submitted 27 October 2026

Question
Twenty years ago Anglin, Paz and Zurek proposed that an electron passing close to a metal loses coherence through quantum Brownian motion: its image charge dissipates energy in the metal, and by the fluctuation–dissipation theorem the same process randomizes the electron’s phase. Interferometer experiments near silicon found decoherence two orders of magnitude below the prediction, and those near gold were at the edge of detectability. Can electron–metal decoherence be measured and compared quantitatively with the theory?
Result
Yes, using a diffractometer instead of an interferometer. Diffracted 1.67 keV electrons enter a gold-plated microchannel at milliradian grazing incidence and bounce off the wall at sub-nanometre distance, which raises the interaction strength \(\Delta t/y^3\) to about \(10^7\) ps/μm³. All diffraction orders broaden equally and fringe visibility falls to 30–60%. The measured decoherence rate peaks at 182 μm⁻² at a tilt of 4.6 mrad, close to the classical-trajectory prediction of 216 μm⁻², and a standing-wave model reproduces the turnover at small tilt with a fitted gold resistivity of \(37\pm5\) nΩ m.

The paper in detail

In the Anglin–Paz–Zurek (APZ) picture, an electron at height \(y_0\) above a metal of resistivity \(\rho\) drags its image charge, whose Joule losses slow the electron and whose fluctuations diffuse its momentum. For an interaction time \(\Delta t\), \[\gamma_{\rm dec}^{(\rm APZ)}=\frac{e^2k_BT\rho}{16\pi\hbar^2}\,\frac{\Delta t}{y_0^3},\] and coherence between positions separated by \(\Delta x\) decays as \(e^{-\gamma_{\rm dec}\Delta x^2}\). The silicon experiments that disagreed with the theory violated Boyer’s perturbative criterion \(\rho\ll y/(4\pi\varepsilon_0v_0)\); a clean test needs a good metal and nanometre distances, where patch potentials and roughness ruin interferometer alignment.

An interferometer recombines separated beams and measures coherence between them, the off-diagonal blocks of the position density matrix, so it saturates once \(\gamma_{\rm dec}\Delta x^2\gtrsim1\). In a diffractometer the orders never overlap at the screen, and only coherence within each order, the width of the diagonal blocks, shapes the pattern. The authors’ simulation gives \(\mathcal V_{\rm intra}=1/[1+(\gamma_{\rm dec}/\gamma_0)^{2.3}]\) with \(\gamma_0=144\) μm⁻², equivalent to an interferometer with \(\Delta x_{\rm eff}\approx55\) nm, so strong decoherence remains measurable. Equal broadening of all orders, with unchanged peak positions and height ratios, separates decoherence from lensing or dephasing that act differently on each order.

Electrons from a thermionic gun pass two collimating slits and a nanograting with 100 nm period (transverse coherence 380 nm), then a 1.5 mm long, 1.7 μm tall microchannel between gold-coated glass plates, and travel 247 mm to a quadrupole lens and detector. Tilting the channel between 0 and 20 mrad sets how steeply electrons hit the wall and hence how long they spend near it; with at most one electron in the apparatus at a time, the patterns show peak broadening and visibility loss that vary with tilt. Fitting each pattern gives a decoherence rate that rises to 182 μm⁻² at 4.6 mrad and then falls on both sides.

A straight-line bounce off a step potential with turning point \(a_c\) gives \(\gamma\propto1/(a_c^2\sin\theta)\), which explains the size and the decrease at large tilt but not the turnover. Near the wall, incident and reflected waves form a standing wave with a node inside the metal, and averaging the \(y^{-3}\) rate over its density adds an oscillating term that suppresses the rate once \(\lambda_{\rm dB}/(4\sin\theta)\) exceeds the sampled height range, giving a turnover near 5 mrad. The fit gives \(a_c=5.4\) Å and \(\rho_{\rm gold}=37\pm5\) nΩ m, above bulk gold as expected for sputtered films, and \(\rho\ll a_c/(4\pi\varepsilon_0v_0)=2.0\times10^{-7}\) Ω m confirms the perturbative regime. The same parameters predict an energy loss \[E_{\rm loss}\approx\frac{e^2\rho v_0}{16\pi a_c^2}\,\frac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta},\] a few eV, which would need sub-0.1% beam stability and 10 meV resolution to detect.

Transferable methods

  1. Integrating a local decoherence rate along a trajectory

    APZ give a decoherence rate at fixed height. For an electron that approaches and bounces off the wall, the paper integrates the local rate along the classical path, and at the smallest heights replaces the path by the quantum standing-wave density, which explains the measured tilt dependence.

    The local rate scales as \(e^2k_BT\rho/(16\pi\hbar^2y^3)\). Along \(y(t)=h_0-v_0t\sin\theta\) down to the turning point \(a_c\) and back, \[\gamma^{(\rm CM)}_{\rm dec}=\frac{2e^2k_BT\rho}{16\pi\hbar^2}\int_0^{t_f}\frac{dt}{y(t)^3}\approx\frac{e^2k_BT\rho}{16\pi\hbar^2a_c^2v_0\sin\theta},\] using \(\int_{a_c}^{h_0}dy/y^3\approx1/(2a_c^2)\) for \(h_0\gg a_c\). The weight \(y^{-3}\) falls to 5% of its maximum at \(h_c=a_c/0.05^{1/3}\), so only the last nanometre contributes, and the rate falls as \(1/\sin\theta\).

    Editorial check: with \(T=300\) K, \(\rho=37\) nΩ m, \(a_c=5.4\) Å and \(v_0=\sqrt{2E_0/m_e}=2.42\times10^7\) m/s, the formula gives 216 μm⁻² at \(\theta=4.6\) mrad, as quoted. The Fresnel reflectivity of a 21 eV step at 5 mrad comes out at 0.84, and \(\lambda_{\rm dB}=0.30\) Å with \(h_c=1.47\) nm gives \(\theta_c=\arcsin[\lambda_{\rm dB}/(4h_c)]=5.1\) mrad. The energy-loss formula gives 0.75–5.8 eV over 1.7–13 mrad at the central resistivity; the quoted 0.67–6.5 eV corresponds to including the ±5 nΩ m range (we obtain 0.65–6.5 eV).

    How to reuse it

    For any position-dependent decoherence or heating rate near a surface, integrate it over the actual trajectory or, when the particle reflects, over the standing-wave density near the turning point. A steep \(y^{-3}\) weight means grazing incidence trades interaction time against minimum distance, and that the short-distance cut-off, here the turning point, controls the answer.

  2. Intra-beam coherence as a short-baseline decoherence detector

    An interferometer measures coherence between widely separated paths and is blind once decoherence is strong. A diffractometer measures coherence within each diffracted beam, an effective separation of tens of nanometres, and so can quantify much stronger decoherence; several orders act as internal references.

    In Caldeira–Leggett decoherence the position density matrix evolves as \(\rho(x,x^\prime)\to\rho(x,x^\prime)\,e^{-\gamma_{\rm dec}(x-x^\prime)^2}\). An interferometer with path separation \(\Delta x\) sees visibility \[\mathcal V_{\rm inter}=e^{-\gamma_{\rm dec}\Delta x^2},\] already lost at \(\gamma_{\rm dec}\sim0.01\) μm⁻² for \(\Delta x=10\) μm. In a diffractometer only coherence across the transverse width of each order matters, and the simulated response \(\mathcal V_{\rm intra}=1/[1+(\gamma_{\rm dec}/\gamma_0)^{2.3}]\), \(\gamma_0=144\) μm⁻², stays sensitive up to a few hundred μm⁻².

    Editorial estimate: \(1/\Delta x_{\rm eff}^2\) with \(\Delta x_{\rm eff}=55\) nm is about 330 μm⁻², the same order as \(\gamma_0\), so the effective-separation picture is self-consistent. Because decoherence broadens every order by the same amount while leaving peak positions and height ratios unchanged, correlated features across orders distinguish it from lensing or patch-potential dephasing, which act differently on each order.

    How to reuse it

    Match the detector’s baseline to the strength of the decoherence: long-baseline interferometers for weak effects, intra-beam or near-field measurements for strong ones. Using several diffraction orders as internal references is a general way to separate genuine loss of coherence from deterministic beam distortions.

Context & limitations

Why it matters. This is the first measurement of controlled electron–metal decoherence in the regime the original proposal assumed, where Boyer’s perturbative criterion holds, and the agreement supports quantum Brownian motion as the mechanism. Detecting coherence within each diffracted beam corresponds to a path separation of about 55 nm, which opens the nanometre regime relevant to on-chip electron accelerators, electron–photon sources and, possibly, the resolution limit of electron microscopes.

Limitations. The decoherence rate is extracted by fitting a path-integral simulation, the model has two fitted parameters (the turning point \(a_c\) and the resistivity), and the error bars include only the uncertainty in propagation distance. A single bounce and a step potential are assumed, and spurious broadening from patch potentials or lensing is argued against rather than measured independently. The predicted energy loss of roughly 0.7–6.5 eV, which would test the dissipation side of Caldeira–Leggett theory, was not measured.
