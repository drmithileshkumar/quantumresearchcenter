Problem-aware sampling for bias and variance reduction in randomised Hamiltonian simulation

Brian Coyle; Jonathan H. Fetherolf; Antonio Márquez Romero; Stefano Scali; Giuseppe Buonaiuto; Josh Kirsopp

arXiv:2610.31515v1 ↗PDF ↗Submitted 22 October 2026

Question
qDRIFT approximates \(e^{-iHT}\) by random Pauli rotations sampled in proportion to \(|c_j|\), ignoring the initial state and the observable. Its estimators carry bias and variance that cost many steps and trajectories. When the goal is a single \(\langle O(T)\rangle\) from a known initial state, can the sampling distribution be tuned to the problem while keeping qDRIFT’s guarantees?
Result
Yes, with StateDRIFT. To fourth order in \(dt\), bias and variance depend on the distribution only through a few \(t=0\) quantities built from \(|\psi\rangle\), \(O\) and the Hamiltonian terms. This gives closed-form variance-optimal rules, an interpolating family that provably improves both bias and variance at one step for sign-definite inputs, and an optimized family for deeper circuits. For molecules of 6 to 18 qubits the dynamics resolves in 1.7–26 times fewer steps and 1.2–11 times fewer two-qubit gates; for BeH\(_2\) the gate count falls from 11,201 to 983. Mixing with 20% of the vanilla distribution bounds every reweighting by a factor of 5.

The paper in detail

With \(H=\sum_jc_jP_j\), each qDRIFT step samples \(j\) with probability \(q_j\) and applies \(e^{-i\theta_jP_j}\), \(\theta_j=c_jdt/q_j\). With \(M\) trajectories the error is \(B_N^2+V_N/M\), and only the bias does not shrink with \(M\). One step is exact: \(\hat O_1(j)=\langle O\rangle+\sin^2\theta_j\,\gamma_j+\tfrac12\sin2\theta_j\,\beta_j\), with \(\gamma_j=\langle P_jOP_j\rangle-\langle O\rangle\) and \(\beta_j=-i\langle[O,P_j]\rangle\). In the toy \(H=3Z_0Z_1+X_0+X_1\), vanilla qDRIFT spends 60% of samples on \(Z_0Z_1\), which commutes with the magnetization and has \(\gamma=0\). The state-aware rule moves weight to the transverse terms and cuts the root-mean-square error by 2–7 times.

For \(N\) steps, \(B_N=N(b_{\gamma,2}-\tfrac12\varphi_{H,H})dt^2+O(dt^3)\), with \(b_{\gamma,2}=\sum_jc_j^2\gamma_j/q_j\) and \(\varphi_{H,H}=d^2\langle O\rangle/dt^2|_0\). The variance starts at \(Nv_{\beta^2,2}dt^2\). Cross-step terms carry higher powers of \(N\) but still fall as \(1/N\) at fixed \(T\). If the state, observable and terms are real in some basis, as for real-orbital chemistry with Hartree–Fock references, then \(\beta_j=0\) and odd orders vanish. When the \(\gamma_j\) have mixed signs, a distribution can make \(b_{\gamma,2}=\tfrac12\varphi_{H,H}\) and cancel the \(dt^2\) bias.

Densities of the form \(\sum_jc_j^2f_j/q_j\) are minimized by \(q_j\propto|c_j|\sqrt{f_j}\), a Neyman allocation. This gives \(q_j\propto|c_j|\sqrt{|\gamma_j|}\) when \(\beta_j=0\), \(q_j\propto|c_j||\beta_j|\) otherwise, and the family \(q_j\propto|c_j|\sqrt{|\gamma_j|+\alpha\beta_j^2}\) between them. Along this family the bias density grows and the variance density shrinks, so for sign-definite \(\gamma\) some \(\alpha\) beats vanilla in both. Deeper circuits use per-term weights on five densities, optimized for mean-squared error with neither bias nor variance more than 5% worse than vanilla. Earlier state-aware rules are special cases with \(O=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\), for which \(\gamma_j\le0\) and the bias cannot be cancelled.

For random product states under five lattice models of 6–18 qubits, most single-step instances improve both bias and variance. For quenches between molecular geometries, from H\(_3\) to CH\(_4\), the steps needed to resolve the change in a weighted occupation fall 1.7–26 times. A validity ratio \(R^{(4)}_{\rm val}>2\) separated wins from losses in every case tested. For deployment, a Hartree–Fock surrogate misjudges correlated LiH and does worse than vanilla, while an MPS with \(\chi=4\) suffices. Matchgate shadows need only the 3-RDM for every distribution-dependent input and the 5-RDM for one fixed term, with \(O(n^5\log n/\varepsilon^2)\) snapshots.

Transferable methods

  1. Neyman allocation for importance-sampled channels

    The leading bias and variance are sums \(\sum_jc_j^2f_j/q_j\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives the minimizer in closed form, and the same argument applies to any weighted sum of such densities, which yields a one-parameter family trading bias against variance.

    For \(f_j\ge0\) and \(\sum_jq_j=1\), \[\Bigl(\sum_j|c_j|\sqrt{f_j}\Bigr)^2=\Bigl(\sum_j\tfrac{|c_j|\sqrt{f_j}}{\sqrt{q_j}}\sqrt{q_j}\Bigr)^2\le\sum_j\tfrac{c_j^2f_j}{q_j}\sum_jq_j,\] with equality iff \(q_j\propto|c_j|\sqrt{f_j}\). With \(f_j=|\gamma_j|\) of one sign, the single-step variance \(v_\gamma=\operatorname{Var}_q[(c_j/q_j)^2\gamma_j]\) vanishes at the same \(q\), because the random variable becomes constant.

    The bias \((b_{\gamma,2}-\tfrac12\varphi_{H,H})dt^2\) is minimized at that \(q\) but cancels only if \(\tfrac12\varphi_{H,H}\) lies beyond \(b_\gamma^\star=\pm(\sum_j|c_j|\sqrt{|\gamma_j|})^2\). Mixed signs allow exact cancellation, which is how the staggered magnetization of the XY chain reaches \(|B_1|\approx10^{-4}\) of vanilla.

    Editorial check: for the toy model with \(|\psi_a\rangle\), \(O=Z_0+Z_1\) and \(T=0.2\), \(\gamma=(0,-1.732,-1)\). The 20%-mixed rule \(q=(0.12,0.49,0.39)\) lowers the exact single-step bias from \(-0.498\) to \(-0.339\) and the variance from 0.251 to 0.0072. The root-mean-square error at \(M=1\) falls from 0.707 to 0.349, within the 2–7 times range of Fig. 2.

    How to reuse it

    Any randomized compiler or importance-sampled estimator (randomized measurements, LCU sampling, Pauli-path sums) whose cost is \(\sum_jw_j^2f_j/q_j\) can be tuned with \(q_j\propto|w_j|\sqrt{f_j}\), provided \(f_j\) is cheap to estimate in advance. Mix with the baseline so that no probability falls below \(\tau\) times its baseline value, which bounds every reweighting by \(1/\tau\).

  2. Majorana degree counting fixes the RDM order

    To estimate the distribution inputs on a fermionic state, count how many Majorana operators the nested commutators can contain. This fixes which reduced density matrix is needed and therefore the matchgate-shadow cost.

    Majorana monomials \(\zeta_{\mathcal I}\) and \(\zeta_{\mathcal I'}\) with \(|\mathcal I'|\) even commute unless \(|\mathcal I\cap\mathcal I'|\) is odd, in which case \([\zeta_{\mathcal I},\zeta_{\mathcal I'}]=\pm2\zeta_{\mathcal S}\) with \(\mathcal S\) the symmetric difference and \(|\mathcal S|\le|\mathcal I|+|\mathcal I'|-2\). Hamiltonian terms have degree 2 or 4, so each commutator raises the degree by at most 2.

    A one-body \(O\) has degree 2. Conjugation by \(P_j\) flips the sign of pairs with exactly one index in \(\mathcal I_j\), so \(\gamma_j=-2i\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal I_j,\nu\notin\mathcal I_j}A_{\mu\nu}\langle\zeta_\mu\zeta_\nu\rangle\) needs only the 1-RDM. \(\beta_j\) needs the 2-RDM and \(\varphi_{j,b}\) the 3-RDM. The fixed term \(\langle[H,[H,[H,[H,O]]]]\rangle\) has degree up to 10 and needs the 5-RDM.

    Matchgate shadows estimate a degree-\(2k\) correlator with single-snapshot variance at most \(\binom{2n}{2k}/\binom nk\approx(2n)^k/(2k-1)!!\), polynomial in \(n\) at fixed \(k\), whereas local Pauli shadows scale as \(3^w\) in the Pauli weight.

    How to reuse it

    Before measuring nested-commutator quantities on fermionic states (Trotter error bounds, Krylov moments, perturbative corrections), count Majorana degrees to find the highest RDM order needed. One matchgate-shadow dataset then serves every such quantity, provided the Hamiltonian has at most quartic fermionic terms.

Context & limitations

Why it matters. It is an ancilla-free change to randomized simulation that targets the quantity actually estimated. Its inputs can come from a bond-dimension-4 matrix-product state surrogate (for LiH) or from matchgate shadows with polynomially many snapshots, without full classical simulation.

Limitations. The expansion is fourth order. Its truncation bound scales as \((\lambda T)^3\) and is loose, so the method targets short times: water fails at \(T=1\), as the validity metric predicts. Most benchmarks use exact inputs on classically simulable sizes. A Hartree–Fock surrogate performs worse than vanilla qDRIFT for LiH. The distribution is fixed at \(t=0\), and gate savings depend on the cost model.
