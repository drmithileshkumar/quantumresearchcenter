17.1 The Principles Reviewed

The course began by refusing to read quantum theory off a table of experimental outcomes. It is time to collect what was assumed instead, what each assumption did, and what the world would look like without it.

Predict first. Which single principle of the course is responsible for the appearance of complex numbers in quantum theory? Which is responsible for intrinsic randomness?

Compare your reasoning

Complex numbers come from local tomography, which excludes the disc of real quantum theory, together with reversible interaction, which excludes higher-dimensional balls. Intrinsic randomness comes from continuous reversibility, which forces pure states between perfectly distinguishable ones. Neither feature was assumed.

17.1.2 Experimental tests of the principles

Principles are not justified by experiments, but they are exposed to them. Each has an observable consequence that a foil does not share.

Local tomography separates complex from real quantum theory. Marc-Olivier Renou and collaborators showed in 2021 that in a network with two independent sources, real quantum theory cannot reproduce certain correlations that complex quantum theory predicts; experiments with photons and superconducting qubits reported in 2022 observed the complex-theory correlations. The Born rule has been tested through the absence of higher-order interference: a triple-slit experiment by Urbasi Sinha and collaborators in 2010 bounded deviations from the pairwise interference structure that the Born rule implies. Bell tests (Chapter 13) and contextuality tests (Chapter 12) confirm consequences that exclude local and noncontextual hidden-variable foils. Each such experiment tests a principle against a definite alternative, which is the role experiments play in a principle theory.

Problem 17.1. For each foil, name a principle it violates: classical probability theory, real quantum theory, boxworld, the square bit as a single system.

Worked solution

Classical probability theory violates continuous reversibility. Real quantum theory violates local tomography. Boxworld violates continuous reversibility, since its reversible transformations are only local operations and permutations. The square bit violates continuous reversibility as a single system, since its symmetries are the eight discrete symmetries of a square.

17.2 Other Reconstructions

The route of this course is one of several. Comparing them shows which features are robust: every successful reconstruction needs something like local tomography, something like reversibility or purification, and a way to fix the qubit.

17.2.1 Purification and information

Lucien Hardy’s 2001 derivation used five axioms: probabilities as limiting frequencies, a simplicity axiom selecting the smallest \(K\), the subspace axiom, the composite-system rules \(K_{AB}=K_AK_B\) and \(N_{AB}=N_AN_B\), and continuity of reversible transformations. Chapter 5 followed his counting; the course replaced simplicity by an argument from reversible interaction.

Giulio Chiribella, Giacomo Mauro D’Ariano, and Paolo Perinotti derived quantum theory in 2011 from purification together with five informational axioms: causality, local discriminability (equivalent to local tomography), perfect distinguishability, ideal compression, and pure conditioning. Their framework treats processes as circuits and makes information-processing tasks the source of the axioms. Borivoje Dakić and Časlav Brukner argued from an information capacity of one bit per elementary system, local tomography, and continuous reversibility. Rob Clifton, Jeffrey Bub, and Hans Halvorson characterized quantum theory within the algebraic framework by the impossibility of signaling, of broadcasting unknown states, and of unconditionally secure bit commitment.

17.2.2 Quantum logic and other routes

An older program begins not with probabilities but with propositions: the yes–no tests of a system and the relations of compatibility, implication, and negation among them. Garrett Birkhoff and John von Neumann proposed it in 1936. Constantin Piron showed that, under certain lattice-theoretic conditions, the propositions are the closed subspaces of a Hilbert space over a field with an involution, and Maria Pia Solèr proved in 1995 that, if the space is infinite-dimensional and has an infinite orthonormal sequence, the field is the real numbers, the complex numbers, or the quaternions. Gleason’s theorem (Chapter 12) then supplies the probabilities. This route assumes no probabilities at the start, at the price of abstract axioms whose physical motivation is debated.

Other routes start elsewhere: from Jordan algebras, classified by Pascual Jordan, von Neumann, and Eugene Wigner in 1934, and connected to self-dual homogeneous cones; from symmetry and consistency of probability calculus, as in the work of Philip Goyal, Kevin Knuth, and John Skilling; or, assuming unitary quantum mechanics, from decision theory or symmetries of entanglement, as in the arguments of David Deutsch and David Wallace and of Wojciech Zurek for the Born rule. The last group derives the Born rule from the rest of quantum theory rather than the theory from principles.

Problem 17.2. Hardy’s simplicity axiom and this course’s reversible-interaction argument both lead to \(r=2\). What is the advantage of the second?

Worked solution

Simplicity is a methodological preference with no direct empirical content: a world with \(r=3\) would not violate it observably in any single experiment. Reversible interaction between two bits is a physical property that a foil violates, so the derivation rests on a claim about the world rather than about economy of description.

17.3 Open Questions

A derivation from principles changes which questions remain open, without closing all of them.

17.3.1 The measurement problem

The framework takes definite outcomes as primitive, and the principles then determine the theory of their statistics. The theory, applied to the measuring apparatus itself, describes an entangled superposition of outcomes. Chapter 15 showed that decoherence makes such superpositions locally indistinguishable from mixtures, but not that one outcome occurs. The reconstruction thus sharpens the measurement problem into a precise question: how can a theory whose primitives are outcomes also describe the devices that produce them? Interpretations answer differently, and some, such as objective-collapse models, make predictions that experiments are beginning to test.

The principles constrain the answers. Any interpretation must reproduce intrinsic randomness for the theory’s states (Chapter 6), contextuality for any deeper variables (Chapter 12), and nonlocality for deeper variables of composites (Chapter 13). These are not features of particular interpretations; they are consequences of the principles.

17.3.2 Beyond finite dimensions and beyond quantum theory

No reconstruction of infinite-dimensional quantum theory, or of quantum field theory, from operational principles is as complete as the finite-dimensional ones. Chapter 16 described the usual route through symmetry, but a principle-based derivation of field theory, including its inequivalent representations, remains open. So does the relation between quantum theory and gravity, where the assumption of a fixed background of space and time, used implicitly in every laboratory description, may fail.

Finally, the framework of Chapters 1–4 is larger than quantum theory. It contains every foil met in the course and many more. This makes it a tool for testing quantum theory: any proposed deviation, such as higher-order interference, real amplitudes, or stronger correlations, can be formulated as a theory in the framework, its violated principle identified, and an experiment designed to look for it. Quantum theory is the unique theory satisfying a short list of principles. Whether nature satisfies them exactly is, in the end, an empirical question.

Problem 17.3. The 2022 network experiments observed correlations that real quantum theory cannot reproduce. Does this prove local tomography?

Worked solution

No. It excludes one specific foil, real quantum theory with the standard rule for composing independent sources, which violates local tomography. Other theories that violate local tomography in different ways are not tested by this experiment. As in every principle theory, experiments refute definite alternatives; the principle remains a general claim that survives each test it could have failed.

4. Worked problem §

Drop continuous reversibility and keep the other principles. Which theories become possible, and what would a world described by them be like?

Hint 1

Classical probability theory satisfies the others.

Hint 2

What could not happen in such a world?

Worked solution

Classical probability theory, among others, becomes possible. In such a world every pure state would assign definite outcomes to sharp tests, randomness would always be ignorance, and a finite system could not change continuously and reversibly between distinguishable states. There would be no interference and no entanglement.

5. Worked problem §

Rafael Sorkin defined the third-order interference term \(I_3=P_{ABC}-P_{AB}-P_{AC}-P_{BC}+P_A+P_B+P_C\), where \(P_S\) is the probability at a detector with the slits in \(S\) open. Show that \(I_3=0\) when \(P_S=|\sum_{s\in S}\psi_s|^2\).

Hint 1

Expand \(|a+b+c|^2\).

Hint 2

Each cross term appears once in \(P_{ABC}\) and once in one pair term.

Worked solution

\(P_{ABC}=|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+2\mathrm{Re}(ab^*+ac^*+bc^*)\). The pair terms sum to \(2(|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2)+2\mathrm{Re}(ab^*+ac^*+bc^*)\). So \(I_3=(\Sigma-2\Sigma+\Sigma)+(\text{cross}-\text{cross})=0\). The Born rule allows only pairwise interference, which triple-slit experiments test.

7. Worked problem §

Solèr’s theorem leaves three possible fields: real, complex, and quaternionic. Which principles of this course exclude the real and quaternionic options?

Hint 1

Recall Chapter 7.

Hint 2

Consider composite systems.

Worked solution

The real case is excluded by local tomography, since real quantum composites have more parameters than local statistics fix. The quaternionic case is excluded by counting (\(K=6\) for a bit) and by the failure of a well-behaved tensor product for composites. Both exclusions come from how systems combine.

8. Misconception §

A student says: “Since quantum theory has been derived from principles, it is now proven to be true.” Correct the statement.

Hint 1

What is the logical form of the derivation?

Hint 2

Recall Section 17.3.2.

Worked solution

The derivation shows that if the principles hold, the theory is quantum theory. Whether the principles hold is an empirical question, and each has foils that experiments could favor. The derivation makes quantum theory understandable and exposes it to sharper tests; it does not make it necessarily true.

9. Extension §

Describe two experimental outcomes that would refute a principle of the course, and say which principle each refutes.

Hint 1

Consider interference patterns and correlations.

Hint 2

Each foil predicts something quantum theory forbids.

Worked solution

A nonzero third-order interference term \(I_3\) would refute the Born rule, and with it the derivation that produces it, including the shape of the state space. Correlations between separated systems with CHSH value above \(2\sqrt2\) would refute the Hilbert-space structure of composites derived from local tomography and continuous reversibility. Observing composite states not determined by local statistics would refute local tomography directly.

10. Extension §

List the features that any interpretation of quantum theory must reproduce, according to the course, and explain why they are not features of a particular interpretation.

Hint 1

Chapters 6, 12, and 13.

Hint 2

They follow from the principles.

Worked solution

Intrinsic randomness for the theory’s pure states (Chapter 6), contextuality of any deeper variables (Chapter 12), and nonlocality of any deeper variables for composites (Chapter 13). They follow from the principles and from theorems about all hidden-variable completions, so every interpretation that reproduces quantum predictions must accommodate them, whatever it says about the measurement problem.
