Reading guide · preparation and approach

Starting point: Chapters 8–10: Hilbert space, symmetry, and observables.

Anchor example: A particle on a line, translated in space and boosted in velocity.

Principle at work: Galilean symmetry represented by continuous unitary groups.

Foil: Finite-dimensional theories, which cannot satisfy the canonical commutation relation.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

16.1 Why Finite Dimensions Are Not Enough

Everything so far has assumed finiteness: finitely many distinguishable states, finitely many parameters. A particle moving on a line violates both. This chapter identifies exactly where the finite-dimensional derivation stops, and what replaces it: symmetry, now of space and time rather than of an abstract state space.

Predict first. Could position and momentum of a particle be represented by \(N\times N\) matrices \(x\) and \(p\) satisfying \(xp-px=i\hbar I\), for some large but finite \(N\)?

Compare your reasoning

No. The trace of any commutator of finite matrices is zero, because \(\tr(xp)=\tr(px)\), while the trace of \(i\hbar I\) is \(i\hbar N\ne0\). The canonical commutation relation requires infinite-dimensional spaces and operators that are not bounded.

16.1.1 The trace argument

For finite matrices, \(\tr(AB)=\tr(BA)\), so every commutator has trace zero. The relation \([x,p]=i\hbar I\) has trace \(i\hbar N\) on the right, so it has no finite-dimensional solution. Even in infinite dimensions it has no solution by bounded operators, those with a finite largest stretching factor: Aurel Wintner and Helmut Wielandt proved in the 1940s that the commutator of two bounded operators is never a nonzero multiple of the identity.

Why should the relation hold at all? In the usual presentation it is postulated, by analogy with classical Poisson brackets. Section 16.3 derives it from the requirement that position measurements transform correctly under translations and boosts. First we need the tools that infinite dimensions require.

16.1.2 Unbounded operators

Position and momentum have unbounded sets of possible values, and their operators are unbounded. On the space \(L^2(\mathbb R)\) of square-integrable wave functions, position acts as \((x\psi)(x)=x\,\psi(x)\) and momentum as \((p\psi)(x)=-i\hbar\,\psi'(x)\). Neither is defined on every square-integrable function: \(x\psi\) may fail to be square-integrable, and \(\psi\) may fail to be differentiable. Each is defined on a dense subspace, its domain.

Domains matter. An operator can satisfy \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A\phi}\psi\) on its domain, being symmetric, without being self-adjoint, and only self-adjoint operators have a spectral decomposition and generate unitary groups. The momentum operator on a half-line, for instance, is symmetric but has no self-adjoint version, reflecting that a particle confined to a half-line cannot be translated freely. In finite dimensions the distinction does not arise, which is one reason the course has postponed infinite dimensions until now.

Problem 16.1. Show directly that the commutator of any two \(2\times2\) matrices has trace zero.

Worked solution

\(\tr(AB)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}\) and \(\tr(BA)=\sum_{i,j}B_{ij}A_{ji}\); renaming \(i\leftrightarrow j\) shows the sums are equal. So \(\tr(AB-BA)=0\), while \(\tr(i\hbar I)=2i\hbar\).

16.2 Spectra and Groups

Two finite-dimensional results need upgrading: the spectral decomposition of observables, and the theorem that continuous one-parameter groups have generators.

16.2.1 Continuous spectra

Position has no eigenvectors in \(L^2(\mathbb R)\): a function concentrated at a single point is not square-integrable. Instead of projections onto eigenvectors, position comes with projections onto intervals: \(P(\Delta)\) multiplies \(\psi\) by the indicator function of the interval \(\Delta\). The probability that a position measurement finds the particle in \(\Delta\) is \(\tr(\rho P(\Delta))\), and for a pure state \(\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx\).

The spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators says that every self-adjoint \(A\), bounded or not, has a unique projection-valued measure \(P\) with \(A=\int a\,dP(a)\). This replaces the finite sum \(\sum_ka_kP_k\) of Chapter 10. The Born rule takes the same form, \(\tr(\rho P(\Delta))\), and Gleason’s theorem extends to separable infinite-dimensional spaces for countably additive probability assignments. What fails is finiteness itself: there is no finite fiducial set, and \(K\) and \(N\) are infinite.

16.2.2 Stone’s theorem

Stone’s theorem (1932). Every strongly continuous one-parameter group of unitary operators on a Hilbert space has the form \(U(t)=e^{-iHt}\) for a unique self-adjoint operator \(H\), possibly unbounded. Conversely, every self-adjoint operator generates such a group.

This is the infinite-dimensional version of the theorem in Chapter 9, and its proof requires the spectral theorem. Applied to spatial translations, \((T(a)\psi)(x)=\psi(x-a)\), it gives a generator, and a Taylor expansion identifies it: \(T(a)=e^{-iap/\hbar}\) with \(p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\). Momentum is the generator of translations, just as the Hamiltonian is the generator of time translations. The connection is structural, not an analogy with classical mechanics.

Problem 16.2. Expand \(e^{-iap/\hbar}\psi(x)\) with \(p=-i\hbar\,d/dx\) as a power series and identify the result for an analytic \(\psi\).

Worked solution

\(-iap/\hbar=-a\,d/dx\), so \(e^{-a\,d/dx}\psi(x)=\sum_n\frac{(-a)^n}{n!}\psi^{(n)}(x)\), which is the Taylor series of \(\psi(x-a)\). The exponential of the momentum operator translates the wave function by \(a\).

16.3 Position and Momentum

With these tools, the canonical commutation relation can be derived from symmetry rather than postulated. The principle at work replaces continuous reversibility with a specific group: the Galilean symmetries of space.

16.3.1 Translations, boosts, and commutation

A position observable for a particle on a line is a projection-valued measure \(P(\Delta)\). Translating the particle by \(a\) should translate its position distribution: \(T(a)^\dagger P(\Delta)T(a)=P(\Delta-a)\), or the equivalent with the opposite sign convention. George Mackey showed in 1949, in his imprimitivity theorem, that any such covariant pair, irreducible, is unitarily equivalent to the standard one on \(L^2(\mathbb R)\): positions multiply, translations shift. With \(V(b)=e^{ibx/\hbar}\), which boosts momentum by \(b\), the covariance relation becomes the Weyl relation

\[T(a)\,V(b)=e^{-iab/\hbar}\,V(b)\,T(a),\]

and differentiating in \(a\) and \(b\) at zero gives \([x,p]=i\hbar\) on a suitable domain. The commutation relation is the infinitesimal form of the statement that translations shift positions. Valentine Bargmann’s analysis of the Galilean group (1954) adds that mass appears as a parameter of its projective representations, and that states of different total mass cannot be superposed: another superselection rule, like the one of Chapter 14.

16.3.2 The Stone–von Neumann theorem

Stone–von Neumann theorem (1930s). Every irreducible representation of the Weyl relations for finitely many degrees of freedom by strongly continuous unitary groups is unitarily equivalent to the standard representation on \(L^2(\mathbb R^n)\).

So there is essentially one quantum theory of a finite number of particles, once the symmetry of space is imposed: the Schrödinger representation. For infinitely many degrees of freedom, as in quantum field theory, the theorem fails and inequivalent representations exist, which is the source of phenomena such as superselection sectors and spontaneous symmetry breaking.

The reconstruction programs of this course assume finiteness, and no equally complete principle-based derivation of infinite-dimensional quantum theory from operational principles alone exists. The usual route is the one described here: accept the finite-dimensional structure, then fix the infinite-dimensional representation by symmetry. Another is to treat infinite-dimensional systems as limits of finite ones, which works for many purposes but requires care with domains and limits.

Problem 16.3. Verify \([x,p]\psi=i\hbar\psi\) for a differentiable \(\psi\) in the domain of both operators.

Worked solution

\(xp\psi=-i\hbar\,x\psi'\) and \(px\psi=-i\hbar(x\psi)'=-i\hbar(\psi+x\psi')\). Subtracting, \((xp-px)\psi=i\hbar\psi\).

For \(3\times3\) matrices, what are the traces of \([x,p]\) and of \(i\hbar I\)?

Hint 1

\(\tr(xp)=\tr(px)\).

Hint 2

\(\tr I=3\).

Worked solution

\(0\) and \(3i\hbar\), so \([x,p]=i\hbar I\) is impossible.

2. Quick check §

A particle has wave function \(\psi(x)=1\) on \([0,1]\) and zero elsewhere. What is the probability that a position measurement finds it in \([0,0.25]\)?

Hint 1

\(\int_\Delta|\psi|^2dx\).

Hint 2

The density is uniform.

Worked solution

\(\int_0^{0.25}1\,dx=0.25\).

3. Quick check §

Which observable generates spatial translations, and which generates time translations?

Hint 1

Stone’s theorem gives a generator for each continuous group.

Hint 2

Compare with Chapter 9.

Worked solution

Momentum generates spatial translations, \(T(a)=e^{-iap/\hbar}\); the Hamiltonian generates time translations, \(U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}\).

4. Worked problem §

For the Gaussian \(\psi(x)\propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}\), the position spread is \(\Delta x=\sigma\) and the momentum spread is \(\Delta p=\hbar/(2\sigma)\). (a) Check that the Robertson bound is attained. (b) What happens to \(\psi\) under \(T(a)\)?

Hint 1

Robertson gives \(\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2\).

Hint 2

\(T(a)\psi(x)=\psi(x-a)\).

Worked solution

(a) \(\Delta x\,\Delta p=\sigma\cdot\hbar/(2\sigma)=\hbar/2\), the minimum allowed by \([x,p]=i\hbar\). (b) It becomes \(e^{-(x-a)^2/(4\sigma^2)}\): the same shape centered at \(a\), with the same spreads. Translations shift positions and leave momenta unchanged.

Revisit the relevant explanation

5. Worked problem §

Verify the Weyl relation \(T(a)V(b)=e^{-iab/\hbar}V(b)T(a)\) on a wave function, with \(T(a)\psi(x)=\psi(x-a)\) and \(V(b)\psi(x)=e^{ibx/\hbar}\psi(x)\).

Hint 1

Apply both sides to \(\psi\).

Hint 2

\(T(a)\) shifts the argument of the phase too.

Worked solution

\(T(a)V(b)\psi(x)=e^{ib(x-a)/\hbar}\psi(x-a)\) and \(V(b)T(a)\psi(x)=e^{ibx/\hbar}\psi(x-a)\). Their ratio is \(e^{-iab/\hbar}\), as claimed. The relation states compactly that translations and momentum boosts do not commute.

6. Worked problem §

Show that \(\psi(x)=1/(1+|x|)\) is square-integrable but \(x\psi\) is not. What does this say about the domain of the position operator?

Hint 1

\(\int_{-\infty}^\infty(1+|x|)^{-2}dx=2\).

Hint 2

\(|x\psi(x)|\to1\) as \(|x|\to\infty\).

Worked solution

\(\int\psi^2dx=2\int_0^\infty(1+x)^{-2}dx=2\), finite. But \(x\psi(x)\to\pm1\) at large \(|x|\), so \(\int|x\psi|^2dx\) diverges. The position operator cannot act on every state: it is defined on a dense domain, the hallmark of an unbounded operator.

Revisit the relevant explanation

7. Worked problem §

Truncate the harmonic-oscillator lowering operator to \(N\times N\), \(a=\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\sqrt n\,\ketbra{n-1}{n}\). Compute \([a,a^\dagger]\) and explain how it reconciles with the trace argument, given \([x,p]=i\hbar[a,a^\dagger]\).

Hint 1

\(aa^\dagger\) and \(a^\dagger a\) are diagonal.

Hint 2

Look at the last diagonal entry.

Worked solution

\([a,a^\dagger]=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,\dots,1,-(N-1))\). It equals the identity except in the last entry, which makes the trace zero. The truncated \([x,p]\) agrees with \(i\hbar\) on low-lying states but fails at the cutoff. Finite approximations are useful only for states far from the truncation.

8. Misconception §

A student says: “Infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics is just the limit \(N\to\infty\) of the finite-dimensional theory.” Give three things that change.

Hint 1

Think of domains, spectra, and representations.

Hint 2

Recall the Stone–von Neumann theorem.

Worked solution

Operators can be unbounded and defined only on domains, with symmetric operators that are not self-adjoint; spectra can be continuous, with no normalizable eigenvectors; and for infinitely many degrees of freedom the canonical commutation relations have inequivalent representations. Each needs new mathematics, not just a limit.

Revisit the relevant explanation

9. Extension §

Why does the Stone–von Neumann theorem fail for infinitely many degrees of freedom, and why does this matter in quantum field theory?

Hint 1

Uniqueness relies on finitely many pairs of Weyl operators.

Hint 2

Different representations can describe different phases or vacua.

Worked solution

With infinitely many pairs, one can build representations that differ in infinitely many modes at once, and no unitary relates them; uniqueness fails. In field theory, different vacua, such as those of spontaneously broken symmetries or of interacting versus free fields, live in inequivalent representations, so the choice of representation is part of the physics.

10. Extension §

Which finite-dimensional results of the course survive in separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, and which reconstruction step fails?

Hint 1

Consider Gleason, Wigner, and the Born rule.

Hint 2

Which principle assumes finiteness?

Worked solution

Wigner’s theorem holds, and Gleason’s theorem holds for countably additive assignments, so the Born rule \(\tr(\rho P)\) and unitary or antiunitary symmetries survive. The reconstruction from operational principles fails because it relies on finiteness: there is no finite fiducial set, and the counting arguments of Chapters 5 and 8 do not apply. Infinite-dimensional systems are fixed instead by symmetry, as Section 16.3 shows.
