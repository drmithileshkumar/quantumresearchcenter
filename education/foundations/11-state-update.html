How does a state change when a measurement reveals an outcome?

Reading guide · preparation and approach

Starting point: Chapter 10: projective measurements, compatibility, and commutation.

Anchor example: Three Stern–Gerlach devices in sequence: z, then x, then z.

Principle at work: Repeatable measurements that disturb the state as little as their outcome requires.

Foil: Measurement as passive reading of pre-existing values.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

11.1 Instruments

A measurement produces an outcome, and the system often survives it. What state should be assigned afterward? Textbooks postulate the answer, the projection rule. This chapter shows that outcome probabilities alone do not determine it, and derives the rule from two conditions on ideal measurements.

Predict first. Three Stern–Gerlach devices act in sequence on a spin-½ particle: along \(z\), keep the upper beam; along \(x\), keep the upper beam; along \(z\) again. What fraction of the particles leaving the first device emerge from the lower port of the third? What if the middle device is removed?

Compare your reasoning

With the middle device, one half pass it, and of those one half go down at the third device: \(1/4\) of the particles that left the first. Without it, none go down. The middle measurement changed the state; it did not just read a value. Section 11.3 derives this from the update rule.

11.1.1 Outcomes and post-measurement states

A complete description of a measurement that leaves the system intact must say, for each outcome \(k\), both its probability and the state that follows. Bundle these together as a map \(\mathcal I_k\) that takes the initial state \(\rho\) to the unnormalized state \(\mathcal I_k(\rho)\), whose trace is the probability of outcome \(k\) and whose normalized form is the state after outcome \(k\). The collection \(\{\mathcal I_k\}\) is an instrument. Each \(\mathcal I_k\) respects mixing and so is linear, and the sum \(\sum_k\mathcal I_k\), the effect of measuring and forgetting the result, preserves trace.

An instrument determines its measurement: the effect \(E_k\) is the unique operator with \(\tr(\rho E_k)=\tr\mathcal I_k(\rho)\) for all \(\rho\). The converse fails, as the next subsection shows.

11.1.2 Probabilities do not fix the update

Consider two devices that both measure \(Z\) on a qubit with projections \(P_0=\ketbra00\) and \(P_1=\ketbra11\). The first leaves the system in \(\ket0\) after outcome \(0\) and in \(\ket1\) after outcome \(1\). The second does the same, then flips the qubit with \(X\). Both have identical outcome probabilities for every input, yet they leave different states. A third device could discard the system and prepare a fresh \(\ket+\) whatever the outcome.

So the update is not a property of the observable. It depends on how the measurement is physically carried out, which the effects do not record. A general rule must therefore refer to a special class of measurements. The projection rule describes the measurements that disturb least.

Problem 11.1. For the second device above (measure \(Z\), then apply \(X\)), what is the probability that an immediate repetition of the same device gives the same outcome?

Worked solution

After outcome \(0\) the qubit is in \(\ket1\), so a repetition gives outcome \(1\) with certainty, and vice versa. The probability of the same outcome is \(0\): the device is not repeatable, although it measures the same observable as the first device.

11.2 The Lüders Rule

Two conditions characterize an ideal measurement of a projective observable. Together they force a unique update rule.

11.2.1 Repeatability

A measurement is repeatable if an immediate second application gives the same outcome with certainty. For a projective measurement \(\{P_k\}\), this says that the state after outcome \(k\) gives probability \(1\) to \(P_k\), so it lies in the range of \(P_k\): \(P_k\rho_kP_k=\rho_k\). Repeatability is the operational meaning of the phrase “the measurement found the value \(a_k\)”: the property is now possessed in the sense that testing it again confirms it.

Repeatability alone does not fix the state. After outcome \(0\) of a measurement of \(Z\otimes I\) on two qubits, any state in the range of \(\ketbra00\otimes I\) is repeatable, including states that have scrambled the second qubit entirely.

11.2.2 Minimal disturbance

Add a second condition: the measurement disturbs nothing it does not have to. Precisely, every effect that is compatible with the measured observable and is certain before the measurement remains certain after it. Gerhard Lüders proposed the resulting rule in 1951, and Fedor Herbut showed in 1969 that such minimal requirements characterize it:

\[\rho\ \longmapsto\ \frac{P_k\rho P_k}{\tr(\rho P_k)}\qquad\text{after outcome }k.\]

For a pure state this is the familiar projection: \(\ket\psi\mapsto P_k\ket\psi/\|P_k\ket\psi\|\). The rule preserves every coherence within the eigenspace of the outcome and removes those between eigenspaces. In the two-qubit example it leaves the second qubit exactly as it was, which is what minimal disturbance demands. When each eigenspace is one-dimensional, the post-measurement state is simply the eigenvector, whatever the initial state.

The Lüders rule is a description of ideal measurements, not a law that every measurement obeys. Real devices realize other instruments, and the general form of an instrument is obtained in Chapter 15 from purification. The rule is also not a dynamical process inside the theory: it updates the description given the recorded outcome. Whether something physical “collapses” is the measurement problem, which Chapter 15 states and the principles do not settle.

Problem 11.2. A measurement of \(Z\otimes I\) is applied to \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}-\ket{11})/2\). Find the probability of outcome \(0\) and the Lüders post-measurement state.

Worked solution

Projecting onto first-qubit \(0\) gives \((\ket{00}+\ket{01})/2\), of squared norm \(1/2\). The post-measurement state is \(\ket0\otimes(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). The second qubit is in \(\ket+\), the coherence within the outcome-\(0\) subspace having been kept.

11.3 Information and Disturbance

With an update rule in hand, sequences of measurements become calculable, and a general limit appears: learning about a quantum state costs disturbance.

11.3.1 Sequences of measurements

Return to the three Stern–Gerlach devices. After the first device the spin is in \(\ket0\). The \(x\)-device keeps the outcome \(\ket+\) with probability \(|\braket+0|^2=1/2\), and by the Lüders rule the state becomes \(\ket+\). The third device then finds \(\ket1\) with probability \(|\braket1+|^2=1/2\). Overall, a quarter of the particles leaving the first device exit the lower port of the third. Without the middle device none do.

The order of measurements matters. Measuring \(Z\) and then \(X\) gives a different joint distribution of outcomes from measuring \(X\) and then \(Z\), for most initial states. The noncommutativity of Chapter 10 shows up here as the dependence of sequential statistics on order.

11.3.2 No information without disturbance

Suppose a measurement leaves every state of some set unchanged. It then cannot help distinguish them, for the following reason. If two nonorthogonal pure states \(\ket\psi\) and \(\ket\phi\) are both left undisturbed by an instrument, consider its dilation as a unitary interaction with a probe (constructed in Chapter 15): \(\ket\psi\ket0\mapsto\ket\psi\ket{a}\) and \(\ket\phi\ket0\mapsto\ket\phi\ket{b}\). Unitaries preserve inner products, so \(\braket\phi\psi=\braket\phi\psi\braket ba\). Since \(\braket\phi\psi\ne0\), \(\braket ba=1\): the probe states are identical and carry no information about which state was present.

Gaining any information about nonorthogonal states therefore requires disturbing at least one of them. This is the principle behind quantum key distribution, and it is a consequence of the derived structure, not an extra postulate. It has a counterpart in classical theory only for mixed states; a classical pure state can be read without any disturbance at all.

Problem 11.3. In the three-device sequence, replace the middle \(x\)-device by one along a direction at angle \(\theta\) from \(z\) in the \(xz\)-plane, again keeping its upper port. What fraction leaves the lower port of the final \(z\)-device?

Worked solution

The middle device passes \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\) of the particles and leaves them in the state at angle \(\theta\). The final device sends a fraction \(\sin^2(\theta/2)\) of those down. The total is \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\sin^2(\theta/2)=\tfrac14\sin^2\theta\), largest, \(1/4\), at \(\theta=\pi/2\).

1. Quick check §

A measurement of \(Z\) is applied to \(\ket+\). Give the probability of outcome \(0\) and the Lüders post-measurement state.

Hint 1

\(|\braket0+|^2\).

Hint 2

Project and normalize.

Worked solution

Probability \(\tfrac12\); the state becomes \(\ket0\).

2. Quick check §

A device measures \(Z\) and then prepares \(\ket+\) regardless of the outcome. Does it measure the same observable as an ideal \(Z\) measurement? Is it repeatable?

Hint 1

Compare outcome probabilities.

Hint 2

What does a second application give?

Worked solution

It has the same outcome probabilities, so it measures \(Z\). It is not repeatable: after it, \(\ket+\) gives each outcome with probability \(\tfrac12\). The observable does not determine the update.

3. Quick check §

In the three-device experiment, what fraction leaves the lower port of the final \(z\)-device if the middle \(x\)-device is removed?

Hint 1

The state after the first device is \(\ket0\).

Hint 2

\(\braket10=0\).

Worked solution

None. The first device leaves \(\ket0\), which the final device passes upward with certainty.

4. Worked problem §

A two-qubit parity measurement has projections \(P_{\text{even}}=\ketbra{00}{00}+\ketbra{11}{11}\) and \(P_{\text{odd}}=I-P_{\text{even}}\). Apply it to \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{11})/\sqrt3\). Find the probabilities and the Lüders states.

Hint 1

Project onto each subspace.

Hint 2

Keep coherence within the subspace.

Worked solution

Even: \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt3\), probability \(\tfrac23\), state \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). Odd: \(\ket{01}/\sqrt3\), probability \(\tfrac13\), state \(\ket{01}\). The even outcome preserves the superposition of \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket{11}\), producing an entangled state: minimal disturbance does not mean no change.

Revisit the relevant explanation

5. Worked problem §

On \(\ket0\), compare the joint outcome distributions of (a) measuring \(Z\) then \(X\), and (b) measuring \(X\) then \(Z\), with Lüders updates.

Hint 1

After \(Z\), the state is \(\ket0\).

Hint 2

After \(X\), the state is \(\ket\pm\).

Worked solution

(a) \(Z\) gives \(0\) with certainty, then \(X\) gives \(\pm\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each: outcomes \((0,+)\) and \((0,-)\), each \(\tfrac12\). (b) \(X\) gives \(\pm\) with probability \(\tfrac12\); from \(\ket\pm\), \(Z\) gives each value with probability \(\tfrac12\): four outcomes, each \(\tfrac14\). The order matters, reflecting that \(X\) and \(Z\) do not commute.

Revisit the relevant explanation

6. Worked problem §

A gentle measurement of \(Z\) has Kraus operators \(K_\pm=\sqrt{\tfrac12(I\pm\eta Z)}\). Apply it to \(\ket+\). For outcome \(+\), find the post-measurement Bloch vector and interpret the trade-off between information and disturbance.

Hint 1

\(K_+=\mathrm{diag}\big(\sqrt{(1+\eta)/2},\sqrt{(1-\eta)/2}\big)\).

Hint 2

Normalize \(K_+\ket+\) and compute \(x\) and \(z\).

Worked solution

\(K_+\ket+\propto(\sqrt{1+\eta},\sqrt{1-\eta})\), so the post-measurement state is pure with \(z=\eta\) and \(x=\sqrt{1-\eta^2}\). For \(\eta=0\) nothing is learned and nothing is disturbed; for \(\eta=1\) the outcome is sharp and the \(x\)-component is destroyed. The more the measurement reveals about \(Z\), the more it disturbs the state’s coherence.

7. Worked problem §

Between a \(z\)-device keeping the upper port and a final device along \(-z\), insert \(n-1\) devices at angles \(k\pi/n\), \(k=1,\dots,n-1\), each keeping its upper port. Find the fraction passing all of them for \(n=10\) and \(n=100\).

Hint 1

Each step passes \(\cos^2(\pi/2n)\).

Hint 2

There are \(n\) steps.

Worked solution

Each step passes \(\cos^2(\pi/2n)\), so the total is \(\cos^{2n}(\pi/2n)\): about \(0.78\) for \(n=10\) and \(0.98\) for \(n=100\). Frequent measurements can drag a state from \(\ket0\) to \(\ket1\) almost without loss, a version of the quantum Zeno effect, made possible by the Lüders update.

8. Misconception §

A student says: “The Lüders rule proves that measurement physically collapses the wave function.” What does the rule describe, and what does it leave open?

Hint 1

From what assumptions was the rule derived?

Hint 2

Recall the measurement problem.

Worked solution

The rule describes how the state assigned to a system should be updated after an ideal measurement with a recorded outcome, derived from repeatability and minimal disturbance. It is not a dynamical law within the theory. Whether something physical collapses, or the update reflects new information, or branches persist, is the measurement problem, which the principles do not settle.

Revisit the relevant explanation

9. Extension §

Give two different instruments for the observable \(Z\otimes I\) that are both repeatable but differ in their effect on the second qubit, and identify the one satisfying minimal disturbance.

Hint 1

After outcome \(a\), any state in the range of \(\ketbra aa\otimes I\) is repeatable.

Hint 2

Try applying a unitary to the second qubit.

Worked solution

Instrument 1: \(\rho\mapsto(P_a\otimes I)\rho(P_a\otimes I)\). Instrument 2: the same followed by \(X\) on the second qubit. Both leave the first qubit in \(\ket a\), so both are repeatable. Instrument 2 changes an initially certain property of the second qubit, such as \(Z\otimes I\) and \(I\otimes Z\) both definite in \(\ket{00}\), while instrument 1 does not. Minimal disturbance selects instrument 1, the Lüders rule.

Revisit the relevant explanation

10. Extension §

A probe interacts unitarily with a qubit prepared in \(\ket0\) or \(\ket+\), leaving the qubit undisturbed and the probe in \(\ket a\) or \(\ket b\). Show that \(\braket ba=1\), and conclude that the probe gains no information.

Hint 1

Unitaries preserve inner products.

Hint 2

\(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\ne0\).

Worked solution

Inner products before and after must agree: \(\braket0+=\braket0+\braket ba\). Dividing by \(1/\sqrt2\) gives \(\braket ba=1\), so the probe states are identical and carry no information. To learn anything about which of two nonorthogonal states was present, at least one must be disturbed.
