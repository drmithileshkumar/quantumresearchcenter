Reading guide · preparation and approach

Starting point: Chapter 6: the state space of a two-outcome system is a ball of some dimension.

Anchor example: Two qubits that interact reversibly and continuously.

Principle at work: Local tomography together with continuous reversible interaction between two bits.

Foil: Real quantum theory (the disc) and quaternionic quantum theory (the five-dimensional ball).

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

7.1 Which Ball?

Chapter 6 reduced the simplest system to a ball of unknown dimension \(d\). Single systems cannot fix \(d\): every ball is a consistent bit. This chapter shows that composition does, and then translates the resulting ball into the language of complex matrices, where the Born rule appears as a theorem.

Predict first. In quantum theory with real amplitudes, can two different states of a pair of systems give identical statistics for every measurement on either system and for every correlation between such measurements?

Compare your reasoning

Yes. Section 7.1 constructs two such states that a joint measurement distinguishes perfectly, although no local measurements and correlations can tell them apart at all. Real quantum theory violates local tomography, and that is why the disc is not our bit.

7.1.1 Counting parameters for two bits

A ball of dimension \(d\) has \(K=d+1\). Under local tomography a pair of such bits has \(K_{AB}=(d+1)^2\), and with \(N_{AB}=4\) the counting rule of Chapter 5 requires \(K=N^r\) for both: \(d+1=2^r\) and \((d+1)^2=4^r\). So \(d\) must be \(1,3,7,15,\dots\). The disc (\(d=2\)) and the quaternionic ball (\(d=5\)) are already excluded by counting, and the classical segment (\(d=1\)) by continuity.

The disc fails in an instructive way. In quantum theory with real amplitudes, states are real symmetric matrices of unit trace with nonnegative eigenvalues, and a single bit has the observables built from

\[I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Products of these give nine real symmetric \(4\times4\) matrices for a pair, but real symmetric \(4\times4\) matrices form a ten-dimensional space. The tenth direction is the matrix \(Y\otimes Y\), with entries \(-1,1,1,-1\) on the antidiagonal and zeros elsewhere; it is real, although \(Y\) itself is not. The states \(\rho_\pm=\tfrac14(I\otimes I\pm Y\otimes Y)\) have eigenvalues \(0,0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12\) and so are valid. Every local observable and every product of local observables has the same expectation in both, yet the global observable \(Y\otimes Y\) has expectation \(+1\) in one and \(-1\) in the other. The two states are perfectly distinguishable by a joint measurement and completely indistinguishable by local ones.

7.1.2 Why three dimensions

Counting leaves \(d=3,7,15,\dots\). The remaining step uses continuous reversibility for the pair. Two bits that never interact are of little use: the principle requires reversible transformations that carry product pure states to every pure state of the pair, including correlated ones, continuously. Lluís Masanes, Markus Müller, Remigiusz Augusiak, and David Pérez-García proved in 2013 that if bits are balls of dimension \(d\), composites are locally tomographic, and some continuous reversible transformation of a pair is not merely a product of local ones, then \(d=3\), and the theory of the pair is quantum theory. The proof analyzes which generators of motion on the \((d+1)^2\)-dimensional space can preserve the set of states, and lies beyond this course.

For \(d=3\) such interactions exist; we shall write one down in Chapter 9. For larger \(d\) none do. So the bit of our theory is the three-dimensional ball, \(K=4\), and the exponent of Chapter 5 is \(r=2\). From now on we call this system a qubit and its state space the Bloch ball.

Problem 7.1. Verify that \(\rho_+=\tfrac14(I\otimes I+Y\otimes Y)\) gives expectation zero for \(X\otimes Z\) and for \(Z\otimes I\), and expectation \(1\) for \(Y\otimes Y\).

Worked solution

Expectations are \(\tr(\rho_+M)\). Products of distinct Pauli products have zero trace, and \(\tr(M^2)=4\) for each, so \(\tr(\rho_+(X\otimes Z))=\tr(\rho_+(Z\otimes I))=0\), while \(\tr(\rho_+(Y\otimes Y))=\tfrac14\tr(I)=1\). The same local values hold for \(\rho_-\), which gives \(-1\) for \(Y\otimes Y\).

7.2 From the Ball to Matrices

A ball is a geometric object; quantum mechanics speaks of vectors and matrices. The two descriptions are the same, and seeing why explains where complex numbers come from.

7.2.1 Density matrices for a qubit

Write a state of the Bloch ball as a vector \(\mathbf r=(x,y,z)\) with \(|\mathbf r|\le1\). Introduce the three matrices \(X\), \(Z\) of Section 7.1 and

\[Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\ i&0\end{pmatrix},\]

and assign to \(\mathbf r\) the matrix

\[\rho=\tfrac12\big(I+xX+yY+zZ\big)=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1+z&x-iy\\x+iy&1-z\end{pmatrix}.\]

This matrix is Hermitian (equal to its conjugate transpose) and has trace \(1\). Its eigenvalues are \((1\pm|\mathbf r|)/2\), which are nonnegative exactly when \(|\mathbf r|\le1\). So the Bloch ball is the same thing as the set of \(2\times2\) Hermitian matrices with unit trace and nonnegative eigenvalues: the density matrices of a qubit. Hermitian \(2\times2\) matrices form a real vector space of dimension \(4\), matching \(K=4\), and mixing states corresponds to averaging matrices.

Pure states, \(|\mathbf r|=1\), are the density matrices with eigenvalues \(1\) and \(0\), that is, projections onto a single direction in \(\mathbb C^2\). Such a projection can be written \(\rho=\psi\psi^\dagger\), with \(\psi\) a unit column vector, determined up to a phase factor. We write \(\psi\) as \(\ket\psi\) and its conjugate transpose as \(\bra\psi\), so \(\rho=\ketbra\psi\psi\). The point \(\mathbf r=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)\) corresponds to \(\ket\psi=\big(\cos\tfrac\theta2,\ e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\big)^T\). State vectors are not postulated; they are a convenient way to write pure points of the ball.

7.2.2 Why complex numbers appear

Real symmetric \(2\times2\) matrices of unit trace form a two-dimensional family: those with \(y=0\). They describe the disc. A three-dimensional ball needs a third independent direction, and among \(2\times2\) Hermitian matrices that direction is supplied only by an imaginary off-diagonal entry, the matrix \(Y\). Complex numbers enter because the bit is three-dimensional, and the bit is three-dimensional because of local tomography and reversible interaction.

The reversible transformations of the ball are its rotations. Each rotation is realized as \(\rho\mapsto U\rho U^\dagger\) for a \(2\times2\) unitary matrix \(U\) of determinant \(1\), with \(U\) and \(-U\) giving the same rotation. For instance, \(U=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\alpha/2},e^{i\alpha/2})\) rotates the ball by angle \(\alpha\) about the \(z\)-axis. Unitary matrices thus appear as the representation of rotations of the Bloch ball. Reflections of the ball are not in \(G_0\), and Chapter 9 shows they correspond to antiunitary maps.

Problem 7.2. Find the density matrix and a state vector for the pure state \(\mathbf r=(0,1,0)\).

Worked solution

\(\rho=\tfrac12(I+Y)=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\ i&1\end{pmatrix}\). With \(\theta=\pi/2\), \(\phi=\pi/2\), \(\ket\psi=(1,i)^T/\sqrt2\), and indeed \(\ketbra\psi\psi=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\ i&1\end{pmatrix}\). No real vector gives this matrix.

7.3 The Probability Rule

The Born rule is usually stated as a postulate. Here it is what effects on the Bloch ball look like when written in matrix language.

7.3.1 Effects on the Bloch ball

By Chapter 3, an effect is an affine functional \(e(\mathbf r)=a+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf r\) with values between \(0\) and \(1\) on the ball. Its extreme values on the ball are \(a\pm|\mathbf b|\), so the condition is

\[0\le a-|\mathbf b|,\qquad a+|\mathbf b|\le1 .\]

We assume, as the last principle of Chapter 8 will state generally, that every such functional is realized by some measurement. Associate with \(e\) the Hermitian matrix \(E=aI+b_xX+b_yY+b_zZ\). Its eigenvalues are \(a\pm|\mathbf b|\), so the conditions say exactly that \(E\) has eigenvalues between \(0\) and \(1\), written \(0\le E\le I\). The unit effect is \(I\), and a measurement is a list of such matrices summing to \(I\).

7.3.2 The Born rule for a qubit

Products of distinct Pauli matrices have zero trace, and each squares to \(I\). Hence

\[\tr(\rho E)=\tfrac12\tr\big((I+\mathbf r\cdot\vec\sigma)(aI+\mathbf b\cdot\vec\sigma)\big)=\tfrac12(2a+2\,\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf b)=a+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf r=e(\mathbf r),\]

where \(\vec\sigma=(X,Y,Z)\). The probability of an outcome is \(\tr(\rho E)\). For a pure state \(\rho=\ketbra\psi\psi\) and a sharp effect \(E=\ketbra\phi\phi\), this is \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\): the squared magnitude of an inner product. We have derived the Born rule for a qubit from the shape of its state space and the linearity of probabilities.

The rule is quadratic in the vectors \(\ket\psi\) and linear in the state \(\rho\), as Chapter 2 required. The sharp effect that tests for the pure state \(\mathbf m\) is \(E=\tfrac12(I+\mathbf m\cdot\vec\sigma)\), with \(a=\tfrac12\) and \(\mathbf b=\mathbf m/2\). On the pure state \(\mathbf n\) at angle \(\theta\) from \(\mathbf m\) it gives \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)=\cos^2(\theta/2)\). The Stern–Gerlach statistics of Chapter 1 are thus a consequence of the principles, not their foundation.

Problem 7.3. Compute \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\) for \(\ket\psi=(1,0)^T\) and \(\ket\phi=(\cos\tfrac\theta2,\sin\tfrac\theta2)^T\), and check that it agrees with \(\tfrac12(1+\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf m)\) for the corresponding Bloch vectors.

Worked solution

\(\braket\phi\psi=\cos\tfrac\theta2\), so the probability is \(\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\). The Bloch vectors are \((0,0,1)\) and \((\sin\theta,0,\cos\theta)\), whose dot product is \(\cos\theta\), and \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)=\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\).

1. Quick check §

Find the density matrix of the state \(\mathbf r=(0.6,0,0.8)\). Is it pure? Give a state vector.

Hint 1

\(\rho=\tfrac12(I+xX+yY+zZ)\).

Hint 2

Pure states have \(|\mathbf r|=1\).

Worked solution

\(\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix}\). Since \(|\mathbf r|=1\) it is pure, and \(\ket\psi=(3,1)^T/\sqrt{10}\), because \(\ketbra\psi\psi=\tfrac1{10}\begin{pmatrix}9&3\\3&1\end{pmatrix}\).

2. Quick check §

What are the eigenvalues of the density matrix of a state with \(|\mathbf r|=0.5\)?

Hint 1

The eigenvalues are \((1\pm|\mathbf r|)/2\).

Hint 2

They must be nonnegative and sum to one.

Worked solution

\(0.75\) and \(0.25\). The state is mixed, a quarter of the way from the center to the surface in terms of eigenvalues.

3. Quick check §

Is \(E=0.5I+0.3Z\) an effect? What probability does it give on \(\ket0\) and on \(\ket1\)?

Hint 1

Its eigenvalues are \(a\pm|\mathbf b|\).

Hint 2

Use \(\tr(\rho E)\).

Worked solution

Its eigenvalues are \(0.8\) and \(0.2\), both in \([0,1]\), so it is an effect, a noisy test for \(\ket0\). It gives \(0.8\) on \(\ket0\) and \(0.2\) on \(\ket1\).

4. Worked problem §

For \(\rho_\pm=\tfrac14(I\otimes I\pm Y\otimes Y)\): (a) confirm both are valid states; (b) show that they give the same expectation for every product of observables from \(\{I,X,Z\}\); (c) explain why, in complex quantum theory, local tomography still distinguishes them.

Hint 1

\(Y\otimes Y\) is real, with eigenvalues \(\pm1\).

Hint 2

In complex quantum theory, \(Y\) is a local observable.

Worked solution

(a) \(Y\otimes Y\) has eigenvalues \(\pm1\), each twice, so \(\rho_\pm\) have eigenvalues \(0,0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12\): valid. (b) Distinct Pauli products are orthogonal under the trace, so every product from \(\{I,X,Z\}\), other than \(I\otimes I\), has zero expectation in both. (c) In complex quantum theory each party can measure \(Y\), and the correlation \(\langle Y\otimes Y\rangle=\pm1\) is a product of local outcomes. The states differ in a local correlation, as local tomography requires.

Revisit the relevant explanation

5. Worked problem §

Show that \(U=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\alpha/2},e^{i\alpha/2})\) rotates the Bloch vector by \(\alpha\) about the \(z\)-axis.

Hint 1

\(U\rho U^\dagger\) multiplies the off-diagonal entry \(\tfrac12(x-iy)\) by a phase.

Hint 2

Compare \((x-iy)e^{-i\alpha}\) with a rotated vector.

Worked solution

The diagonal of \(\rho\) is unchanged, so \(z\) is fixed. The entry \(\tfrac12(x-iy)\) becomes \(\tfrac12(x-iy)e^{-i\alpha}\). Writing \(x'-iy'=(x-iy)(\cos\alpha-i\sin\alpha)\) gives \(x'=x\cos\alpha-y\sin\alpha\) and \(y'=x\sin\alpha+y\cos\alpha\): a rotation by \(\alpha\) about \(z\).

6. Worked problem §

Compute the probability that \(\ket\psi=(\cos\tfrac\pi8,\sin\tfrac\pi8)^T\) passes a test for \(\ket+\) in two ways: from \(|\braket+\psi|^2\), and from the Bloch vectors.

Hint 1

\(\braket+\psi=(\cos\tfrac\pi8+\sin\tfrac\pi8)/\sqrt2\).

Hint 2

\(\ket\psi\) has Bloch angle \(\theta=\pi/4\) in the \(xz\)-plane.

Worked solution

\(|\braket+\psi|^2=\tfrac12(\cos\tfrac\pi8+\sin\tfrac\pi8)^2=\tfrac12(1+\sin\tfrac\pi4)\approx0.854\). The Bloch vectors \((\sin\tfrac\pi4,0,\cos\tfrac\pi4)\) and \((1,0,0)\) are at angle \(\pi/4\), giving \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\tfrac\pi4)\approx0.854\). The Born rule and the geometry of the ball agree, as Section 7.3 proved.

7. Worked problem §

Counting alone allows a bit that is a ball of dimension \(7\). (a) What are \(K\) for one such bit and for a pair? (b) Do these satisfy \(K=N^r\)? (c) Which result excludes \(d=7\)?

Hint 1

\(K=d+1\).

Hint 2

\(8=2^3\).

Worked solution

(a) \(K=8\) for one bit and \(64\) for a pair. (b) Yes, with \(r=3\): \(8=2^3\) and \(64=4^3\). (c) The theorem of Masanes, Müller, Augusiak, and Pérez-García: with local tomography, balls of dimension \(d\ne3\) admit no continuous reversible interaction between two bits. Counting is not enough; reversible interaction selects \(d=3\).

8. Misconception §

A student says: “Complex numbers in quantum mechanics are just convenient bookkeeping; real amplitudes would give the same predictions.” What is wrong?

Hint 1

What state space does a real qubit have?

Hint 2

Recall the network experiments of Chapter 17.

Worked solution

Real \(2\times2\) density matrices describe only the disc, so a real qubit lacks the states with \(y\ne0\). One can simulate a complex qubit with an extra real qubit, but then composite systems behave differently, and Renou and collaborators showed that real quantum theory with independent sources cannot reproduce certain network correlations of complex quantum theory, which experiments observed in 2022. Complex numbers are physically significant, and the course traces them to local tomography.

9. Extension §

A \(2\times2\) Hermitian matrix with quaternion entries has real diagonal entries and an off-diagonal quaternion. (a) Count its real parameters and find the dimension of the corresponding ball of states. (b) What does this give for \(K\)?

Hint 1

A quaternion has four real components.

Hint 2

Subtract one for unit trace.

Worked solution

(a) Two real diagonal entries and four real components off the diagonal: \(6\) parameters. Unit trace leaves \(5\), so the state space is a \(5\)-dimensional ball. (b) \(K=6\), not a power of \(2\), so counting excludes it; its composites also lack a well-defined tensor product over the quaternions.

10. Extension §

Show that \(\ket\psi\) and \(e^{i\phi}\ket\psi\) have the same density matrix, while \((1,e^{i\phi})^T/\sqrt2\) has Bloch vector \((\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0)\). Interpret global and relative phase geometrically.

Hint 1

\(\ketbra\psi\psi\) is unchanged by a common phase.

Hint 2

Read off \(x\) and \(y\) from the off-diagonal entry.

Worked solution

\((e^{i\phi}\ket\psi)(e^{i\phi}\ket\psi)^\dagger=\ketbra\psi\psi\), so a global phase is not a property of the state. For \((1,e^{i\phi})^T/\sqrt2\) the off-diagonal entry \(\rho_{10}=e^{i\phi}/2=\tfrac12(x+iy)\), so \(x=\cos\phi\), \(y=\sin\phi\), \(z=0\). A relative phase is the azimuthal angle on the Bloch sphere: a physical property, measurable by interference.
