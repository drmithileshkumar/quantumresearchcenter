Reading guide · preparation and approach

Starting point: Chapter 7: the qubit, its density matrices, and the probability rule.

Anchor example: A spin-1 particle with three perfectly distinguishable states.

Principle at work: Equivalence of subspaces, and the availability of all measurements consistent with the state space.

Foil: Theories with restricted measurements, and Hardy’s classical branch K = N.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

8.1 Equivalence of Subspaces

We now know the qubit completely. A spin-1 particle, an atom with many levels, or a pair of qubits has more than two distinguishable states. This chapter shows how the qubit fixes the description of every finite system, using two further principles.

Predict first. A system has three perfectly distinguishable states. Consider only preparations that never give the third outcome of the distinguishing measurement. What should the set of those states look like, if the theory treats all systems carrying one bit alike?

Compare your reasoning

A Bloch ball. Such states carry exactly one bit, and the equivalence of subspaces says they form a system indistinguishable from a qubit. This holds for every pair of distinguishable states, and it is enough to reconstruct the whole state space.

8.1.1 Faces of the state space

Let \(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_N\) be perfectly distinguishable pure states with distinguishing effects \(e_1,\dots,e_N\). Choose a subset, say the first \(k\). The states that give probability zero to all of \(e_{k+1},\dots,e_N\) form a convex subset that is closed under decomposition: if a mixture lies in it, so does each component, since a mixture of states can give probability zero only if every component does. Such a subset is called a face. Physically it is the set of states that pass a filter blocking the last \(N-k\) outcomes with certainty.

Equivalence of subspaces. Every face generated by \(k\) perfectly distinguishable pure states, with the effects and reversible transformations that act within it, is equivalent to the state space of a system with \(N=k\). In particular, all systems with the same \(N\) are equivalent.

The principle says that a system confined to \(k\) of its alternatives behaves like any other system with \(k\) alternatives; it retains no trace of being part of something larger. Hardy called it the subspace axiom. It is what allowed Chapter 5 to treat \(K\) as a function of \(N\) alone. A foil would be a theory in which, say, the two-outcome faces of a three-level system were squares while its genuine bits were balls: logically possible, but a theory in which an encoding’s behavior depends on what else the carrier could have done.

8.1.2 Every two-outcome face is a qubit

Applied to pairs, the principle says that any two perfectly distinguishable pure states, together with all states that pass the corresponding filter, form a Bloch ball. A spin-1 particle has three distinguishable states, often labeled \(+1\), \(0\), \(-1\). Each of the three pairs spans a qubit face: a ball whose poles are the two chosen states and whose equator consists of pure states that give each of the two outcomes with probability one half.

The faces overlap only in mixtures of their shared states, and together they do not exhaust the state space. A pure state can give all three outcomes with nonzero probability, so it lies in none of the two-outcome faces. Reconstructing the full space requires understanding how the faces fit together, which is the subject of the next section.

Problem 8.1. In a system with \(N=4\), how many two-outcome faces are generated by a fixed set of four distinguishable pure states? How many three-outcome faces?

Worked solution

Choosing \(2\) of \(4\) gives \(6\) qubit faces, and choosing \(3\) of \(4\) gives \(4\) faces equivalent to a system with \(N=3\).

8.2 Hilbert Space

With the qubit and the equivalence of subspaces in hand, the description of a system with \(N\) distinguishable states is fixed. We count its parameters, state the reconstruction theorem, and see Hilbert space emerge.

8.2.1 The rule K = N²

Hardy observed that the faces give a direct count. A state is fixed by \(N\) probabilities for the distinguishing measurement, plus, for each pair of distinguishable states, whatever further information the qubit face of that pair requires. A qubit has \(K=4\): two parameters are the probabilities of its two outcomes, already counted, and two are new. With \(N(N-1)/2\) pairs,

\[K(N)=N+2\cdot\frac{N(N-1)}{2}=N^2 .\]

The same result follows from Chapter 5: the counting rule \(K=N^r\) with the qubit value \(K(2)=4\) forces \(r=2\). The two derivations agree, as they must, and both rely on local tomography: the first through the qubit, the second through the composition rule.

8.2.2 Density operators and pure states as rays

Theorem (Masanes and Müller, 2011). In a theory satisfying the principles of this course, the states of a system with \(N\) perfectly distinguishable states are the \(N\times N\) complex Hermitian matrices with unit trace and nonnegative eigenvalues, the density operators on \(\mathbb C^N\). The reversible transformations are \(\rho\mapsto U\rho U^\dagger\) with \(U\) unitary.

The proof extends the qubit by induction on \(N\) using the faces, and it uses the classification of transitive group actions; we cite it. Its consistency with what we know is easy to check. Hermitian \(N\times N\) matrices have \(N\) real diagonal entries and \(N(N-1)/2\) complex off-diagonal entries, so \(N^2\) real parameters. The \(2\times2\) block for any pair of basis states, together with the corresponding diagonal entries, is exactly a Bloch ball, as the equivalence of subspaces requires.

The pure states are the projections \(\ketbra\psi\psi\) onto unit vectors \(\ket\psi\in\mathbb C^N\), with vectors that differ by a phase factor giving the same state. A pure state is thus a ray in a complex Hilbert space. Writing \(\ket\psi=\sum_jc_j\ket j\) in the distinguishable basis displays superposition: the \(|c_j|^2\) are the outcome probabilities, while the relative phases of the \(c_j\) carry the information in the qubit faces. The transition probability between pure states is \(\tr(\ketbra\phi\phi\,\ketbra\psi\psi)=|\braket\phi\psi|^2\). Every element of the usual Hilbert-space formalism for states is now derived.

Problem 8.2. For a three-level system, compare the pure state \(\ket\psi=(1,1,1)^T/\sqrt3\) with the maximally mixed state \(I/3\). Which measurement distinguishes them, and with what probabilities?

Worked solution

Both give probability \(1/3\) for each basis outcome. The test \(\{\ketbra\psi\psi,\ I-\ketbra\psi\psi\}\) gives the first outcome with probability \(1\) on \(\ket\psi\) and \(\tr(\ketbra\psi\psi/3)=1/3\) on \(I/3\). The off-diagonal entries of \(\ketbra\psi\psi\), all equal to \(1/3\), carry the difference.

8.3 All Measurements

States are now fixed. The last principle fixes the measurements, completing the general form of the Born rule.

8.3.1 Effects as positive operators

All measurements allowed. Every linear functional that takes values between \(0\) and \(1\) on all states is an effect of some measurement, and every list of effects summing to the unit effect is a measurement.

Without it, a theory could have a rich state space but few measurements, so that states differing in principle could never be told apart. The principle says that the only limits on measurement are those imposed by the states themselves.

Linear functionals on Hermitian matrices can all be written as \(\rho\mapsto\tr(\rho E)\) for a unique Hermitian \(E\). The condition \(0\le\tr(\rho E)\le1\) for all density operators holds exactly when \(0\le\braket\psi{E\psi}\le1\) for all unit vectors, that is, when \(E\) has eigenvalues between \(0\) and \(1\). So the effects are the operators with \(0\le E\le I\), the unit effect is \(I\), and the probability of an outcome is

\[p=\tr(\rho E).\]

This is the Born rule in its general form, now derived for every finite system.

8.3.2 General measurements

A measurement is a list of effects \(E_1,\dots,E_m\) with \(\sum_kE_k=I\), often called a positive-operator-valued measure, or POVM. When each \(E_k\) is a projection and they are mutually orthogonal, the measurement is projective; projective measurements onto a basis distinguish its states perfectly. General measurements can have more outcomes than \(N\), and their effects need not be projections.

A standard example on a qubit is the trine measurement. Take three unit vectors \(\mathbf n_0,\mathbf n_1,\mathbf n_2\) in a plane of the Bloch ball at \(120^\circ\) to each other, so that \(\mathbf n_0+\mathbf n_1+\mathbf n_2=0\), and set \(E_k=\tfrac13(I+\mathbf n_k\cdot\vec\sigma)\). Each \(E_k\) has eigenvalues \(2/3\) and \(0\), and the three sum to \(I\). The state \(\mathbf n_0\) gives outcome \(0\) with probability \(\tfrac13(1+1)=\tfrac23\) and each other outcome with probability \(\tfrac13(1-\tfrac12)=\tfrac16\). The trine is a valid measurement because the principle makes every such list available; it cannot be written as a projective measurement on the qubit alone, although Chapter 15 shows how to realize it as a projective measurement on a larger system.

Problem 8.3. Check that the trine effects sum to \(I\), and find the probability of each outcome for the maximally mixed state.

Worked solution

\(\sum_kE_k=I+\tfrac13(\mathbf n_0+\mathbf n_1+\mathbf n_2)\cdot\vec\sigma=I\). For \(\rho=I/2\), \(\tr(\rho E_k)=\tfrac12\tr E_k=\tfrac13\) for each outcome.

1. Quick check §

What is \(K\) for systems with \(N=3\) and \(N=4\), and how many parameters does a normalized state have?

Hint 1

\(K=N^2\).

Hint 2

Subtract one for normalization.

Worked solution

\(K=9\) and \(16\); normalized states have \(8\) and \(15\) parameters.

2. Quick check §

Is \(E=\mathrm{diag}(0.5,0.5,1)\) an effect on a three-level system? Is it a projection?

Hint 1

Check the eigenvalues.

Hint 2

A projection has eigenvalues \(0\) or \(1\).

Worked solution

Its eigenvalues lie in \([0,1]\), so it is an effect. It is not a projection, because of the eigenvalue \(0.5\): it describes a test that passes level \(3\) for sure and the others with probability one half.

3. Quick check §

Compute the transition probability between \((1,1,0)^T/\sqrt2\) and \((1,0,1)^T/\sqrt2\).

Hint 1

\(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\).

Hint 2

The inner product is \(1/2\).

Worked solution

\(\braket\phi\psi=\tfrac12\), so the probability is \(\tfrac14\).

4. Worked problem §

For a spin-1 system with basis \(\ket{+1},\ket0,\ket{-1}\), describe the face of states that give probability zero for outcome \(-1\). Show that it is a Bloch ball.

Hint 1

Zero probability for a basis outcome forces a zero diagonal entry.

Hint 2

A positive matrix with a zero diagonal entry has zeros in that row and column.

Worked solution

Probability zero for \(-1\) means \(\rho_{-1,-1}=0\), and positivity then forces the whole row and column of \(-1\) to vanish. The remaining \(2\times2\) block on \(\ket{+1},\ket0\) is an arbitrary qubit density matrix, so the face is a Bloch ball, as the equivalence of subspaces requires.

Revisit the relevant explanation

5. Worked problem §

Use Hardy’s face count to compute \(K\) for \(N=3\) and \(N=4\), and prove \(K(N)=N^2\) in general.

Hint 1

\(N\) probabilities plus \(2\) new parameters per pair.

Hint 2

There are \(N(N-1)/2\) pairs.

Worked solution

\(K(3)=3+2\cdot3=9\) and \(K(4)=4+2\cdot6=16\). In general \(K(N)=N+2\cdot\tfrac{N(N-1)}2=N^2\). The \(2\) per pair correspond to the real and imaginary parts of an off-diagonal density-matrix entry.

Revisit the relevant explanation

6. Worked problem §

The three trine states of a qubit are the pure states \(\mathbf n_k\) at \(120^\circ\) in a plane. With equal priors, a party measures the trine POVM \(E_k=\tfrac13(I+\mathbf n_k\cdot\vec\sigma)\) and guesses \(k\). Find the success probability.

Hint 1

\(\tr(\rho_kE_k)=\tfrac13(1+\mathbf n_k\cdot\mathbf n_k)\).

Hint 2

Average over \(k\).

Worked solution

Each state gives the matching outcome with probability \(\tfrac13(1+1)=\tfrac23\), so the success probability is \(\tfrac23\). This is the optimal value for the trine with equal priors; a two-outcome projective measurement cannot even produce three guesses.

Revisit the relevant explanation

7. Worked problem §

For a three-level system, let \(\ket\psi=(1,e^{i\phi},0)^T/\sqrt2\). Find the probability of passing a test for \((1,1,0)^T/\sqrt2\), and interpret it using the qubit face.

Hint 1

Compute the inner product.

Hint 2

The state lies in the face of the first two levels.

Worked solution

The inner product is \((1+e^{i\phi})/2\), so the probability is \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\phi)\). Both states lie in the qubit face of levels 1 and 2, and the result is the Bloch-sphere formula for two equatorial states at angle \(\phi\). Relative phases in a qubit face produce interference.

8. Misconception §

A student says: “A general measurement is just a projective measurement with added noise.” Is the trine a counterexample?

Hint 1

Can the trine be obtained by randomly choosing projective measurements and relabeling outcomes?

Hint 2

Each trine effect has rank one.

Worked solution

Yes. The trine’s effects are rank-one and it is an extremal measurement: it cannot be written as a random choice among projective measurements followed by classical processing of their outcomes. It is genuinely more general, though, by Naimark’s theorem (Chapter 15), it is a projective measurement on a larger system.

9. Extension §

A modified qubit theory has the Bloch ball as its state space but allows only the measurement \(\{\tfrac12(I+Z),\tfrac12(I-Z)\}\) and its noisy versions. (a) Which states can it distinguish? (b) What would an observer conclude about the state space from measurements alone?

Hint 1

Effects depend only on \(z\).

Hint 2

States with the same \(z\) give identical statistics.

Worked solution

(a) Only states with different \(z\), and perfectly only \(z=\pm1\). (b) States with the same \(z\) can never be told apart, so operationally they are the same state; the effective state space collapses to the segment \(-1\le z\le1\), a classical bit. The principle that all measurements are allowed ensures that states and effects match, so that every difference in the state space is observable.

Revisit the relevant explanation

10. Extension §

Suppose the two-outcome faces of every system were discs instead of balls. Redo Hardy’s face count and identify the theory.

Hint 1

A disc has \(K=3\), so one new parameter per pair.

Hint 2

Compare with the parameter count of real symmetric matrices.

Worked solution

\(K(N)=N+1\cdot\tfrac{N(N-1)}2=\tfrac{N(N+1)}2\), the count of real symmetric \(N\times N\) matrices: real quantum theory. Its faces are discs, its parameter count is not multiplicative, and it violates local tomography, as Chapters 5 and 7 found.
