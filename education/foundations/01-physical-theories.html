Reading guide · preparation and approach

Starting point: None. This chapter fixes the language used in the rest of the course.

Anchor example: The same table of Stern–Gerlach outcomes, explained by two incompatible theories.

Principle at work: Framework: a theory must predict the statistics of repeated, identically prepared experiments.

Foil: Any finite table of outcomes, which many theories fit equally well.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

1.1 Principles and Construction

Most courses begin quantum mechanics with a list. States are unit vectors in a complex Hilbert space. Observables are self-adjoint operators. Probabilities are squared magnitudes of inner products. Isolated systems evolve unitarily. Composite systems are described by tensor products. Each item is stated, and the course proceeds to use it. The list works: no prediction derived from it has failed. But it does not say why the world should be described this way rather than some other way. This course asks that question and answers it with derivations.

Predict first. A beam of silver atoms passes through a Stern–Gerlach magnet oriented along \(z\); the upper beam then enters a second magnet tilted by an angle \(\theta\). Over many runs, the fraction of atoms deflected up by the second magnet is close to \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\) for every angle tried. Does this table of results establish that the state of the atom’s spin is a vector in a two-dimensional complex space?

Compare your reasoning

No. In 1966 John Bell constructed a model in which each atom carries a hidden direction that fixes every outcome in advance, and which reproduces the single-spin statistics exactly. The table is equally consistent with that model and with quantum theory; the two disagree only about other experiments, such as correlated pairs. Data from one family of experiments do not select a framework.

1.1.1 Why outcomes underdetermine theories

Three separate obstacles stand between a table of outcomes and a theory. The first is finiteness. Any experiment produces finitely many records, and infinitely many rules agree with any finite list. A lookup table that simply stores every observed outcome “explains” the data perfectly and predicts nothing. Choosing between candidate rules always involves something beyond the data: simplicity, symmetry, or consistency with other domains.

The second obstacle is that descriptions of experiments are already theoretical. To say that a magnet is “tilted by \(\theta\)” or that a beam is “spin up along \(z\)” we must already know what spin directions are and how rotations act on them. The data arrive shaped by the concepts we brought to them, so they cannot independently justify those concepts.

The third obstacle is universality. A physical theory claims to apply to every system of a given kind, in every arrangement, including arrangements nobody has built. Stern–Gerlach statistics concern one kind of system in one family of arrangements. Bell’s model shows concretely that agreement on that family leaves the theory open: the model reproduces every single-spin measurement, yet it predicts different correlations for pairs of spins, and experiments on pairs favor quantum theory. The experiments that motivate quantum mechanics in textbooks therefore make it plausible, not necessary.

1.1.2 Principle theories and constructive theories

In a 1919 article Albert Einstein distinguished two kinds of physical theory. A constructive theory builds complex phenomena from simpler assumed parts, as the kinetic theory of gases builds pressure and temperature from molecules in motion. A principle theory starts from general empirical regularities, raised to principles, which every process must satisfy. Thermodynamics is the standard example: from the impossibility of perpetual motion machines of the first and second kinds it derives constraints on every engine, whatever it is made of. Special relativity derives the Lorentz transformations from the equivalence of inertial frames and the invariance of the speed of light.

A principle theory earns its authority differently from a fit to data. Its principles are few and general. Each one can be stated without the formalism it will produce, so one can ask whether it is reasonable before seeing the consequences. Each one has empirical content: a violation would be observable. And the structure of the theory follows by mathematics, so that accepting the principles means accepting the theory.

Since 2001 several research programs have shown that finite-dimensional quantum theory can be presented as a principle theory. Lucien Hardy derived it from five “reasonable axioms”; Rob Clifton, Jeffrey Bub, and Hans Halvorson characterized it by information-theoretic constraints; Lluís Masanes and Markus Müller, and independently Giulio Chiribella, Giacomo Mauro D’Ariano, and Paolo Perinotti, gave complete derivations from operational principles. This course follows mainly the route of Masanes and Müller, supplemented by Hardy’s parameter counting and by the purification principle of Chiribella, D’Ariano, and Perinotti.

Problem 1.1. A student argues that because the predictions of quantum mechanics have never failed, its postulates are justified, so a derivation from principles adds nothing. What does a derivation add?

Worked solution

Success supports the predictions that were tested, not the claim that no other theory could have succeeded on the same tests. A derivation shows that any theory satisfying the principles must have the quantum structure. It also locates each piece of the formalism: we learn which principle produces complex numbers, which produces the tensor product, and which experiments would reveal a failure of each.

1.2 The Operational Language

To compare theories we need a language in which all of them can be stated. It must not presuppose quantum theory, classical mechanics, or any picture of what systems are made of. The operational language achieves this by speaking only about what is done in a laboratory and what is recorded.

1.2.1 Preparations, transformations, and measurements

A preparation is a recipe that produces a system: heat an oven of silver, select atoms in a narrow beam, block one output of a magnet. A transformation is a recipe that acts on a system and returns a system, possibly of a different type: let it fly through a magnetic field for a fixed time, or combine it with another system. A measurement is a recipe that acts on a system and produces one of a set of classical records, its outcomes: a spot in the upper or lower region of a screen, a click in one of two detectors.

An experiment is a preparation followed by transformations and ending in a measurement. The records are classical in a plain sense: they can be copied, read by several people, and written in a notebook. Everything else in the description is a recipe. The language does not say what a system is, whether it has properties before measurement, or whether its behavior is determined. Those are questions a theory answers, and different theories answer them differently.

Two recipes can differ in their details and yet never lead to different statistics in any experiment. Such recipes are equivalent for every purpose a theory can address, and we identify them. A state will be an equivalence class of preparations, and similarly for transformations and measurement outcomes. This identification is not a hypothesis about nature; it is the decision to describe a system by what it can do.

1.2.2 Repetition and relative frequencies

A single run of an experiment produces a single outcome. A theory that says anything testable must say how often each outcome occurs when the same experiment is repeated. Suppose an experiment with outcomes \(k=1,\dots,m\) is repeated \(n\) times with the same recipes, and outcome \(k\) occurs \(n_k\) times. The relative frequency is \(n_k/n\). We assume that for each experiment there are numbers \(p_k\), the probabilities, such that the frequencies of long, independent runs cluster around them.

How closely? If each run produces outcome \(k\) with probability \(p\) independently, the number of occurrences has mean \(np\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{np(1-p)}\). The relative frequency therefore has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\). For \(p=1/2\) and \(n=1000\) this is \(\sqrt{0.25/1000}\approx0.016\): observed fractions between about \(0.47\) and \(0.53\) are typical. Probabilities are the theory’s predictions; frequencies are what we record; and the comparison between them is statistical.

This assumption is weaker than it may look. It does not say that outcomes are random. A deterministic theory satisfies it by predicting probabilities that are all \(0\) or \(1\) for completely specified preparations; probabilities between \(0\) and \(1\) can then arise only from incomplete specification, such as an oven that emits atoms with random properties. Classical mechanics with uncertain initial conditions is a theory of this kind. Whether pure states can yield probabilities strictly between \(0\) and \(1\), randomness without ignorance, is something we shall derive in Chapter 6 rather than assume.

Problem 1.2. A theory predicts probability \(1/4\) for an outcome. In \(10\,000\) runs the outcome occurs \(2600\) times. Is this evidence against the theory?

Worked solution

The predicted standard deviation of the relative frequency is \(\sqrt{0.25\times0.75/10\,000}\approx0.0043\). The observed fraction \(0.26\) is \(0.01\) above the prediction, about \(2.3\) standard deviations. Such a deviation occurs by chance roughly \(2\%\) of the time, so it is weak evidence against the theory, worth repeating rather than decisive.

1.3 The Plan of the Course

The course has three kinds of ingredient, and it keeps them separate. Mixing them is the most common way in which a supposed derivation smuggles in what it claims to derive.

1.3.1 Framework, principles, and theorems

The framework is what any testable theory must provide: preparations, transformations, measurements, and probabilities for outcomes of repeated experiments, together with the possibility of choosing a preparation at random. Chapters 2–4 show how much structure follows from the framework alone. States form a convex set; outcome probabilities are linear functions of states; two systems can be considered together. Classical probability theory, quantum theory, and many other theories all fit.

The principles are general claims that a theory may or may not satisfy. We use five, each introduced where it does work:

  1. Finiteness. A system has finitely many perfectly distinguishable states, and finitely many probabilities fix its state (Chapters 2 and 3).
  2. Local tomography. The state of a composite system is fixed by the statistics of measurements on its parts and their correlations (Chapter 4).
  3. Continuous reversibility. Every pure state can be turned into every other by a continuous reversible transformation (Chapter 6).
  4. Equivalence of subspaces. Systems with the same number of perfectly distinguishable states are equivalent, including subsystems of larger systems (Chapter 8).
  5. All measurements allowed. Every assignment of probabilities consistent with the state space is the outcome of some measurement (Chapter 8).

The theorems are the consequences: the state space of the simplest system is a three-dimensional ball (Chapter 7); finite systems are described by density operators on complex Hilbert spaces, with the Born rule for probabilities (Chapters 7 and 8); reversible dynamics is unitary and generated by a Hamiltonian (Chapter 9); and the familiar consequences follow, from uncertainty relations to entanglement and decoherence (Chapters 10–15). Some theorems are proved in full. Others rest on mathematical results that the text states precisely and cites.

1.3.2 Foils: theories that break one principle

How can a principle be justified if not by data? Part of the answer is generality, part is that each principle has testable content, and part is an argument from the alternatives. For each principle the course presents a foil: a mathematically consistent theory that satisfies the framework and the other principles but violates this one. Classical probability theory violates continuous reversibility. Real quantum theory, with real instead of complex amplitudes, violates local tomography. Boxworld, a theory of maximally nonlocal correlations, violates the principles that select quantum theory in a different way. Theories with restricted measurements violate the last principle.

Foils do two jobs. They show that each principle is independent, so none can be dropped without changing the theory. And they show what the world would be like without the principle: a classical world with no continuous change between distinguishable states, a real-amplitude world in which two systems hold information that no local measurements reveal. Judging a principle then becomes judging which world is ours, a question experiments can address. We return to these comparisons in Chapter 17.

Problem 1.3. Classify each statement as part of the framework, a principle, or a theorem: (a) the probabilities of the outcomes of a measurement sum to one; (b) the state of a composite system is fixed by local measurement statistics; (c) the pure states of a qubit form a sphere.

Worked solution

(a) Framework: a measurement produces exactly one of its outcomes, so their probabilities sum to one in any theory. (b) A principle, local tomography; real quantum theory is a consistent theory that violates it. (c) A theorem: Chapter 7 derives it from the principles.

1. Quick check §

Sort each item into recipe, record, or theoretical description: (a) an oven of silver held at a fixed temperature; (b) a spot in the upper region of a screen; (c) a magnet rotated by \(30^\circ\) from vertical; (d) “the atom’s spin points up.”

Hint 1

A recipe says what is done; a record is what is written down afterward.

Hint 2

Ask whether the item could be stated without any physical theory of the atom.

Worked solution

(a) and (c) are recipes: instructions for building and arranging equipment. (b) is a record, a classical outcome that anyone can read. (d) is a theoretical description: it interprets records using a concept, spin direction, that belongs to a theory. The operational language uses only the first two kinds, so that different theories can be compared on the same footing.

2. Quick check §

An outcome has probability \(1/2\). What is the standard deviation of its relative frequency in \(400\) runs? How many runs reduce it to \(0.005\)?

Hint 1

The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}\).

Hint 2

Solve \(\sqrt{0.25/n}=0.005\).

Worked solution

\(\sqrt{0.25/400}=0.025\). To reach \(0.005\) we need \(0.25/n=2.5\times10^{-5}\), so \(n=10\,000\). Halving the statistical uncertainty costs four times as many runs.

3. Quick check §

Classify each as a principle theory or a constructive theory: the kinetic theory of gases, thermodynamics, special relativity, the Bohr model of the hydrogen atom.

Hint 1

A constructive theory builds phenomena from assumed parts.

Hint 2

A principle theory starts from general constraints on all processes.

Worked solution

Kinetic theory and the Bohr model are constructive: they assume molecules or an orbiting electron and derive behavior from them. Thermodynamics and special relativity are principle theories: they derive constraints from general principles that hold whatever the system is made of. The course presents quantum theory in the second way.

4. Worked problem §

A “lookup-table theory” records the outcomes of the first \(1000\) runs of every experiment performed so far and predicts, for each, probabilities equal to the observed frequencies. (a) Does it fit the data? (b) What does it predict for a magnet tilted at an angle never tried? (c) Which obstacle of Section 1.1.1 does it illustrate?

Hint 1

It fits by construction.

Hint 2

A theory must say something about arrangements that have not been built.

Worked solution

(a) Perfectly, by construction, up to statistical fluctuation. (b) Nothing: it has no rule connecting different arrangements, so it cannot predict a new angle. (c) It illustrates finiteness and universality together. Infinitely many rules fit any finite table, and a physical theory must make claims about every arrangement. Choosing between candidate rules needs more than the data, which is why the course turns to principles.

5. Worked problem §

An oven emits atoms each carrying a hidden number \(\lambda\), uniformly distributed between \(0\) and \(1\). A magnet tilted by \(\theta\) sends an atom up if \(\lambda<\cos^2(\theta/2)\). (a) What fraction goes up? (b) What does a preparation that fixes \(\lambda\) exactly predict? (c) What does this show about the framework’s assumption of probabilities?

Hint 1

The fraction of uniformly distributed \(\lambda\) below a threshold equals the threshold.

Hint 2

With \(\lambda\) fixed, the outcome is determined.

Worked solution

(a) \(\cos^2(\theta/2)\), matching the observed single-device statistics. (b) Probability \(0\) or \(1\) for every angle: the model is deterministic, and its intermediate probabilities express ignorance of \(\lambda\). (c) A theory that predicts probabilities need not be random. The framework includes deterministic theories, so assuming probabilities does not assume randomness; that must be derived, as Chapter 6 does.

6. Worked problem §

Theory A predicts probability \(0.50\) for an outcome; theory B predicts \(0.52\). Roughly how many runs are needed for the difference to be three standard deviations of the relative frequency?

Hint 1

Use \(\sqrt{0.25/n}\) for the standard deviation.

Hint 2

Set \(0.02=3\sqrt{0.25/n}\).

Worked solution

We need \(\sqrt{0.25/n}=0.02/3\), so \(\sqrt n=3\times0.5/0.02=75\) and \(n\approx5600\). Small differences between theories require long runs. This is one reason the course does not try to select quantum theory by fitting particular numbers: principles are tested by experiments designed to separate definite alternatives.

8. Misconception §

A student argues: “Bell’s model reproduces all single-spin statistics with predetermined outcomes, so the randomness of quantum mechanics is just ignorance.” What is wrong with the argument?

Hint 1

Bell’s model is a different theory with a different state space.

Hint 2

Consider pairs of spins.

Worked solution

Bell’s model adds hidden states beneath the quantum states, so it is a different theory. It agrees with quantum theory on single spins but not on pairs, where its correlations obey the Bell inequalities that experiments violate. Within quantum theory, Chapter 6 derives randomness for pure states, which cannot be decomposed further; Chapters 12 and 13 show that any hidden-variable completion must be contextual and nonlocal. Agreement on one family of experiments does not establish that the randomness is ignorance.

9. Extension §

In geometry, a model in which all of Euclid’s axioms hold except the parallel postulate proves that the postulate does not follow from the others. Explain how the foils of this course play the same role, using classical probability theory as the example.

Hint 1

Independence of an axiom is shown by a model that violates it alone.

Hint 2

Which principles does classical theory satisfy?

Worked solution

Classical probability theory satisfies the framework, finiteness, local tomography, equivalence of subspaces, and the availability of all measurements, but violates continuous reversibility. It is a consistent model of all principles but one, so continuous reversibility cannot be derived from the others. Without it, classical theory would be an equally good candidate. Each foil plays this role for its principle and also shows concretely what the world would look like without it.

10. Extension §

Thermodynamics bounds the efficiency of every heat engine operating between temperatures \(T_h\) and \(T_c\) by \(1-T_c/T_h\). What observation would refute this bound, and what would it refute? Draw the analogy with a principle of this course.

Hint 1

A principle theory is refuted by refuting a principle.

Hint 2

Compare an engine beating the Carnot bound with correlations beating Tsirelson’s bound.

Worked solution

A reliable engine exceeding \(1-T_c/T_h\) would refute the second law, the principle from which the bound is derived, whatever the engine is made of. Analogously, an experiment showing CHSH correlations above \(2\sqrt2\) between separated systems, or composite states not determined by local statistics, would refute a principle of this course and with it the derivation of quantum theory. Principle theories are exposed to experiment through the general consequences of their principles.
