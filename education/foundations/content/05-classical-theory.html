5.1 The Simplex

Classical probability theory satisfies the framework, finiteness, no-signaling, and local tomography. If those were all our principles, there would be no reason for quantum theory. This chapter locates precisely what classical theory cannot do, and introduces the counting argument that frames the rest of the derivation.

Predict first. A classical bit is in state 0. We want it to be in state 1 one second later, by a process that is reversible at every moment and changes continuously in time. Is this possible?

Compare your reasoning

No. The only reversible transformations of a classical bit are “do nothing” and “swap,” and no continuous path connects them. A continuous change must pass through mixtures, which no reversible map can produce from a pure state. Section 5.2 proves this, and it is the crack through which quantum theory enters.

5.1.1 Classical state spaces

A classical system with \(N\) configurations has states \((p_1,\dots,p_N)\) with nonnegative entries summing to one. Geometrically this is a simplex: a segment for \(N=2\), a triangle for \(N=3\), a tetrahedron for \(N=4\). Its corners are the \(N\) pure states, and they are perfectly distinguishable, so \(K=N\).

Classical pure states are deterministic in a precise sense. The extreme effects, those not obtainable by mixing other effects, are the indicator effects of sets of configurations: “the configuration is in set \(S\).” Every pure state assigns each of them probability \(0\) or \(1\). Probabilities strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) arise in only two ways: from mixed states, which express ignorance of the configuration, or from noisy effects, which express an imperfect detector. A classical world can therefore always be read as a world of definite configurations that we know imperfectly.

5.1.2 Unique decomposition into pure states

Every classical state is a mixture of pure states in exactly one way, with weights \(p_j\). This property characterizes simplices among convex sets: a compact convex set of finite dimension in which every point has a unique decomposition into extreme points is a simplex. The disc, the square, and the ball all fail it, as Chapter 2 noted for the disc.

Uniqueness is what makes the ignorance reading of mixed states unambiguous. If a state can be prepared as a mixture of pure states in two different ways, there is no fact, in the state itself, about which pure state the system “really” has. In classical theory this question never arises. In every other theory of the gallery it does.

Problem 5.1. Write the center of the square bit, \((p_X,p_Z)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), as an even mixture of pure states in two different ways.

Worked solution

It is the midpoint of the diagonal from \((0,0)\) to \((1,1)\), and also of the diagonal from \((1,0)\) to \((0,1)\). Both even mixtures give the same state, so the square is not a simplex.

5.2 Reversibility in Classical Theory

A transformation is reversible if another transformation undoes it, returning every state to itself. Physical dynamics of isolated systems is usually reversible at the fundamental level. Classical theory with finitely many configurations has very few reversible transformations, and this is its decisive weakness.

5.2.1 Reversible maps permute pure states

A transformation maps states to states and respects mixing, so it acts linearly on the state space. If \(T\) is reversible, both \(T\) and its inverse map the state space into itself, so \(T\) maps it onto itself. A linear map of a convex set onto itself sends extreme points to extreme points: if \(T\omega\) were a mixture, applying \(T^{-1}\) would exhibit \(\omega\) as a mixture. So a reversible transformation permutes the pure states.

For a simplex, a linear map is fixed by what it does to the corners. The reversible transformations of a classical system with \(N\) configurations are therefore exactly the \(N!\) permutations of its configurations. For a bit there are two: the identity and the swap. For a trit there are six.

5.2.2 No continuous path between pure states

Suppose a bit evolves reversibly and continuously in time, so that the transformation \(T_t\) from time \(0\) to time \(t\) depends continuously on \(t\) and \(T_0\) is the identity. Each \(T_t\) is one of the two permutations. A continuous path in a set of two isolated points cannot move from one to the other, so \(T_t\) is the identity for all \(t\). The bit can never change. The same argument applies for every \(N\): continuous reversible dynamics of a finite classical system is trivial.

A bit can of course change from 0 to 1 continuously, through the mixtures \((1-t,t)\). But a map that takes the pure state \((1,0)\) to the mixture \((1-t,t)\) is not reversible for \(0<t<1\). It loses information, since it must also squeeze the whole segment into a shorter one. A finite classical world must choose between continuity and reversibility.

Classical mechanics escapes this dilemma only because its systems are not finite. A particle has a continuum of configurations, points in phase space, and Hamiltonian flow moves them continuously and reversibly. Our first principle excludes this: a system with finitely many distinguishable states cannot have a continuum of perfectly distinguishable configurations to flow between. Finiteness and continuous reversibility together are therefore incompatible with classical theory. Chapter 6 accepts both and follows the consequences.

Problem 5.2. Show directly that the map on the classical bit \(p\mapsto(1-t)p+t(1-p)\) is not reversible for \(0<t<1\).

Worked solution

The map sends \(p=0\) to \(t\) and \(p=1\) to \(1-t\), so its image is the interval between \(t\) and \(1-t\), shorter than \([0,1]\). Its linear inverse would have to stretch that interval back to \([0,1]\), sending some valid states, such as \(p=0\), to numbers outside \([0,1]\). So the inverse is not a transformation.

5.3 Counting Parameters

Before following continuous reversibility, it is worth seeing how tightly the principles already constrain the relation between \(K\) and \(N\). This argument, due to Lucien Hardy, shows that classical and quantum theory are the first two members of a short list.

5.3.1 The relation between K and N

Assume, anticipating the equivalence of subspaces in Chapter 8, that \(K\) depends only on \(N\): all systems that carry the same number of distinguishable states need the same number of parameters. A system with more distinguishable states contains smaller ones as subsystems, so \(K(N)\) increases strictly with \(N\). Composition with \(N_{AB}=N_AN_B\) and local tomography gives

\[K(N_AN_B)=K(N_A)\,K(N_B).\]

A strictly increasing function on the positive integers that turns products into products must be a power: \(K(N)=N^r\) for some \(r>0\). (This is a classical result in number theory, proved by Paul Erdős in 1946.) Since \(K\) is an integer for every \(N\), \(r\) is a positive integer, as Hardy argued in 2001. The possible theories are indexed by \(r=1,2,3,\dots\).

5.3.2 Hardy’s choice: K = N or K = N²

For \(r=1\), \(K=N\): each state needs only as many numbers as there are distinguishable states. This is classical probability theory, and Section 5.2 showed that it violates continuous reversibility. For \(r=2\), \(K=N^2\). A bit then has \(K=4\), so its normalized states form a three-dimensional convex set. Quantum theory has exactly this count, as Chapter 7 shows.

Hardy closed the argument with a simplicity axiom: \(K\) takes the smallest value consistent with the other principles. Continuity excludes \(r=1\), so simplicity selects \(r=2\). We shall not need that axiom. Chapter 7 derives \(K=4\) for the bit from local tomography and continuous reversible interaction, and so obtains \(r=2\) without appeal to simplicity.

The counting also excludes a famous alternative. Quantum theory with real amplitudes has \(K=N(N+1)/2\), the number of real parameters in a symmetric \(N\times N\) matrix. For \(N=2\) this gives \(3\), and for \(N=4\) it gives \(10\), not \(3\times3=9\). Real quantum theory cannot satisfy local tomography; its composites carry information not visible in local statistics.

Problem 5.3. Under \(K=N^2\), how many real parameters fix a normalized state of a system with three distinguishable states? Of two such systems together?

Worked solution

A single system has \(K=9\), so \(8\) parameters for a normalized state. The pair has \(N=9\) and \(K=81\), so \(80\) parameters, consistent with \(K_{AB}=9\times9\).
