3.1 Effects

Chapter 2 showed that each outcome’s probability is a linear function of the state. We now turn that observation around and treat outcomes as mathematical objects in their own right. Doing so gives a language in which measurements of any theory can be compared.

Predict first. In a theory whose normalized states fill a disc, the pure states are the points of its boundary circle. Can two neighboring points on the circle be told apart with certainty by a single measurement? Can two diametrically opposite points?

Compare your reasoning

Only opposite points can. A linear functional equal to \(1\) at one boundary point and \(0\) at another, while staying between \(0\) and \(1\) on the whole disc, exists only when the points are antipodal (Problem 3.2). Neighboring pure states overlap: there is no perfect test between them. This is the first sign of nonclassical behavior.

3.1.1 Outcomes as linear functionals

Two outcomes, possibly of different measurements, that have the same probability on every state are indistinguishable by any statistics, and we identify them. The resulting equivalence class is called an effect. By Section 2.3 an effect is a linear functional \(e\) on the space spanned by the states, satisfying \(0\le e(\omega)\le1\) for every normalized state \(\omega\).

Effects can be combined in the same way as states. Running one measurement with probability \(\lambda\) and another otherwise, and then reporting a chosen outcome of whichever was run, realizes the effect \(\lambda e_1+(1-\lambda)e_2\). So effects form a convex set too. Two effects that are always zero or one on states have special status; we shall meet them when discussing distinguishability.

For a classical bit with state vector \((p,1-p)\), the effect “heads” is the functional \((p,1-p)\mapsto p\), and “tails” is \((p,1-p)\mapsto1-p\). A noisy detector that reports heads correctly with probability \(0.9\) and falsely with probability \(0.2\) realizes the effect \(0.9p+0.2(1-p)\). Effects describe imperfect as well as ideal measurements.

3.1.2 The unit effect and complete measurements

One effect occurs in every theory: “some outcome occurs.” Its probability is the total probability of any complete measurement, which is \(1\) on normalized states and \(s\) on a state of success probability \(s\). We call it the unit effect \(u\). Because every complete measurement has total probability \(u(\omega)\), a measurement with outcomes \(1,\dots,m\) is a list of effects with

\[e_1+e_2+\dots+e_m=u .\]

Coarse graining merges outcomes: reporting “1 or 2” realizes the effect \(e_1+e_2\). Every effect \(e\) has a complement \(u-e\), and any effect can be completed to a two-outcome measurement \(\{e,u-e\}\), provided the theory makes every such measurement available. Whether it does is a genuine question, settled by the last of our principles in Chapter 8. For now we work with the effects a theory provides and note when an argument needs more.

Problem 3.1. For the classical bit, is the functional \((p,1-p)\mapsto 1.2p-0.1\) an effect? What about \((p,1-p)\mapsto0.5\)?

Worked solution

The first takes the value \(-0.1\) at \(p=0\) and \(1.1\) at \(p=1\), outside \([0,1]\), so it is not an effect. The second is an effect: it is realized by ignoring the system and tossing a fair coin. It equals \(u/2\).

3.2 Distinguishability

A state space describes what preparations predict; distinguishability describes what can be learned from a single run. It is the quantity that connects state spaces to information.

3.2.1 Perfectly distinguishable states

States \(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_n\) are perfectly distinguishable if some measurement with effects \(e_1,\dots,e_n\) satisfies \(e_i(\omega_j)=1\) when \(i=j\) and \(0\) otherwise. Given one system prepared in one of these states, the measurement reveals which, without error, in a single run. Such states can carry a message: a sender who prepares \(\omega_j\) to send symbol \(j\) is decoded perfectly.

Perfectly distinguishable states are linearly independent. Suppose \(c_1\omega_1+\dots+c_n\omega_n=0\). Applying the linear functional \(e_i\) to both sides gives \(c_i=0\), for each \(i\). Since a set of linearly independent vectors in a \(K\)-dimensional space has at most \(K\) members, at most \(K\) states can be perfectly distinguishable.

3.2.2 The capacity N

Finiteness (second part). For each type of system, the largest number \(N\) of perfectly distinguishable states is finite.

We have just shown that \(N\le K\), so the second part follows from the first. We state it separately because \(N\) has its own meaning. It is the number of symbols one system can carry with no error, so \(\log_2N\) is its capacity in bits. A system with \(N=2\) is the simplest nontrivial system of a theory: a bit of that theory. Two theories can both have bits, with \(N=2\), and yet differ in \(K\), in the shape of their state spaces, and in everything else.

The gap between \(K\) and \(N\) measures how much more it takes to describe a state than to use one as a message. In a classical theory, \(K=N\): each state is a probability list over \(N\) distinguishable configurations and needs no more numbers. A theory with \(K>N\) has states that are not mixtures of a single distinguishable set, so its description contains information that cannot be read out in one run. Chapter 5 shows that the principles force \(K=N^2\).

Problem 3.2. In the disc theory, every pure state has an antipode from which it is perfectly distinguishable. Show that no three states of the disc are perfectly distinguishable, so \(N=2\).

Worked solution

Write a state as a vector \(\mathbf r\) with \(|\mathbf r|\le1\); an affine functional is \(e(\mathbf r)=a+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf r\). Its largest and smallest values on the disc are \(a+|\mathbf b|\) and \(a-|\mathbf b|\), so it is an effect exactly when \(a-|\mathbf b|\ge0\) and \(a+|\mathbf b|\le1\). Suppose \(e(\mathbf r_1)=1\) and \(e(\mathbf r_2)=0\). The first forces \(a+|\mathbf b|=1\) with \(\mathbf r_1\) the unit vector along \(\mathbf b\); the second forces \(a-|\mathbf b|=0\) with \(\mathbf r_2\) the unit vector opposite to \(\mathbf b\). Hence any two perfectly distinguishable states are antipodal pure states. Three states would need to be pairwise antipodal, which is impossible, so \(N=2\).

3.3 A Gallery of Theories

The framework so far allows any compact convex set as a state space. A few examples, all with small \(K\), will accompany us through the course. They are not toys: classical probability theory is one of them, quantum theory another, and the rest are the foils against which the principles are tested.

3.3.1 Classical bits and trits

The classical bit has states \((p,1-p)\), a segment with two pure ends, and \(K=N=2\). The classical trit has states \((p_1,p_2,p_3)\), a triangle with three pure corners, and \(K=N=3\). In general a classical system with \(N\) configurations has a simplex with \(N\) corners, and every state is a mixture of corners in exactly one way.

All effects of a classical system are combinations of the indicator effects “configuration \(j\)”: an effect is a list of numbers \(f_j\) between \(0\) and \(1\), with probability \(\sum_jf_jp_j\). Any two distinct pure states are perfectly distinguishable, and every pure state is perfectly distinguishable from every other at once. Classical states have no overlap.

3.3.2 Squares, discs, and balls

The square bit has normalized states \((p_X,p_Z)\in[0,1]^2\), \(K=3\), and four pure states at the corners. Any two corners are perfectly distinguishable: \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\) are separated by the effect \(p_X\), and so are \((0,0)\) and \((1,0)\). Yet no three corners are jointly distinguishable, so \(N=2\). The square bit thus has more pure states than distinguishable ones, like quantum theory, but its pure states never overlap partially. It is the local part of boxworld, the foil of Chapter 4.

The disc and the ball are smooth. Describe a state by a vector \(\mathbf r\) with \(|\mathbf r|\le1\), in two or three dimensions; pure states have \(|\mathbf r|=1\). For each unit vector \(\mathbf n\), the functional

\[e_{\mathbf n}(\mathbf r)=\tfrac12\,(1+\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\]

lies between \(0\) and \(1\) on every state, and \(e_{\mathbf n}+e_{-\mathbf n}=u\). It equals \(1\) at the pure state \(\mathbf n\) and \(0\) at \(-\mathbf n\), so antipodal pure states are perfectly distinguishable. For a pure state at angle \(\theta\) from \(\mathbf n\) it gives \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)\), strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) unless \(\theta\) is \(0\) or \(\pi\). Using \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)=\cos^2(\theta/2)\), this is the dependence on angle reported for tilted Stern–Gerlach magnets in Chapter 1, here appearing as pure geometry of a ball. The disc has \(K=3\) and the ball \(K=4\); both have \(N=2\).

Problem 3.3. In the square bit, try to distinguish the three corners \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\): find the affine function equal to \(1\) at \((0,0)\) and \(0\) at the other two, and check whether it is an effect.

Worked solution

The function is \(1-p_X-p_Z\). At the fourth corner \((1,1)\) it equals \(-1\), so it is not an effect. No measurement singles out \((0,0)\) against both other corners, consistent with \(N=2\).
