States have become vectors. Every theory satisfying finiteness describes its states as a convex set in some \(\mathbb R^K\), and every outcome probability as a linear function on that set. So far we have looked only at the states.

But a theory is tested through its measurements, and the same linear functions that gave probabilities can be studied as objects of their own. Once outcomes are objects, we can ask what a single run of one system can reveal. Answering that needs a second number besides \(K\): the largest number of states one measurement can tell apart without error. The two numbers need not agree, and how they differ is the first sign that a theory is not classical. With both numbers in hand, we can lay out a small gallery of theories whose states and measurements we know completely.

The next chapter uses the gallery to ask how two systems combine.

3.1 Effects

Every outcome’s probability is a linear function of the state. Can we turn this around, and treat the functions themselves as the theory’s measurements? We first identify outcomes that no statistics can tell apart, which gives effects. Then we ask what a whole measurement is, in terms of its effects.

Predict first. In a theory whose normalized states fill a disc, the pure states are the points of its boundary circle. Can two neighboring points on the circle be told apart with certainty by a single measurement? Can two diametrically opposite points?

Compare your reasoning

Only opposite points can. A linear functional equal to \(1\) at one boundary point and \(0\) at another, while staying between \(0\) and \(1\) on the whole disc, exists only when the points are antipodal (Problem 3.2). Neighboring pure states overlap: there is no perfect test between them. This is the first sign of nonclassical behavior.

3.1.1 Outcomes as linear functionals

When are two outcomes, perhaps of different measurements, really the same? Suppose they have the same probability on every state. Then no statistics can tell them apart, and, as we did for preparations, we identify them. The resulting class is called an effect. Since outcome probabilities are linear, an effect is a linear functional \(e\) on the space spanned by the states, with \(0\le e(\omega)\le1\) for every normalized state \(\omega\).

Can effects be mixed, as states can? Suppose we run one measurement with probability \(\lambda\) and another otherwise, then report a chosen outcome of whichever was run. This realizes the effect \(\lambda e_1+(1-\lambda)e_2\). So effects form a convex set too.

Take the classical bit, with state vector \((p,1-p)\). The effect “heads” is the functional \((p,1-p)\mapsto p\), and “tails” is \((p,1-p)\mapsto1-p\). Now suppose a noisy detector reports heads correctly with probability \(0.9\), and falsely, on tails, with probability \(0.2\). It realizes the effect \(0.9p+0.2(1-p)\). So effects describe imperfect measurements as well as ideal ones.

How can we tell an ideal effect from a noisy one? The noisy effect above never reaches \(0\) or \(1\): it lies between \(0.2\) and \(0.9\) on every state. The effect “heads” reaches \(1\) on one state and \(0\) on another. We call an effect sharp if it takes the value \(1\) on some state and the value \(0\) on some other state. A sharp effect gives a certain answer on at least one state each way, so its randomness on other states cannot come from noise in the detector alone.

3.1.2 The unit effect and complete measurements

An effect is one outcome. What makes a list of effects into a whole measurement? One effect occurs in every theory: “some outcome occurs.” Its probability is the total probability of any complete measurement, which is \(1\) on normalized states and \(s\) on a state of success probability \(s\). We call it the unit effect \(u\). Every complete measurement has total probability \(u(\omega)\), so a measurement with outcomes \(1,\dots,m\) is a list of effects with

\[e_1+e_2+\dots+e_m=u .\]

Can we merge outcomes? Reporting “1 or 2” realizes the effect \(e_1+e_2\). This is called coarse graining. Every effect \(e\) also has a complement \(u-e\). Can every effect then be completed to a two-outcome measurement \(\{e,u-e\}\)? Only if the theory makes that measurement available. Whether it does is a genuine question, and one of our principles will settle it. Until then we work with the effects a theory provides, and we say when an argument needs more.

Problem 3.1. For the classical bit, is the functional \((p,1-p)\mapsto 1.2p-0.1\) an effect? What about \((p,1-p)\mapsto0.5\)?

Worked solution

The first takes the value \(-0.1\) at \(p=0\) and \(1.1\) at \(p=1\), outside \([0,1]\), so it is not an effect. The second is an effect: it is realized by ignoring the system and tossing a fair coin. It equals \(u/2\).

3.2 Distinguishability

A state space says what preparations predict over many runs. But a message is read from one run. So we ask which states a single run can tell apart, and find that such states are always linearly independent. Their largest number is a second measure of a system, which we then compare with \(K\).

3.2.1 Perfectly distinguishable states

How many different messages can one run of one system carry? Suppose a sender prepares one of the states \(\omega_1,\dots,\omega_n\) to send symbol \(j\). The receiver can decode without error only if some measurement, with effects \(e_1,\dots,e_n\), satisfies \(e_i(\omega_j)=1\) when \(i=j\) and \(0\) otherwise. States with such a measurement are called perfectly distinguishable. Each effect \(e_i\) is then sharp.

How many states can be perfectly distinguishable? Suppose \(c_1\omega_1+\dots+c_n\omega_n=0\). Applying the linear functional \(e_i\) to both sides gives \(c_i=0\), for each \(i\). So perfectly distinguishable states are linearly independent. A \(K\)-dimensional space holds at most \(K\) linearly independent vectors, so at most \(K\) states can be perfectly distinguishable.

3.2.2 The capacity N

We now have a bound, \(K\). Is it reached, and does the number of messages deserve its own name? Give it one, and state its finiteness as the second part of our first principle.

Finiteness (second part). For each type of system, the largest number \(N\) of perfectly distinguishable states is finite.

We have just shown that \(N\le K\), so the second part follows from the first. We state it separately because \(N\) has its own meaning. It is the number of symbols one system can carry with no error, so \(\log_2N\) is its capacity in bits. A system with \(N=2\) is the simplest nontrivial system of a theory, called a bit of that theory. Two theories can both have bits and yet differ in \(K\), in the shape of their states, and in everything else.

What does a gap between \(K\) and \(N\) mean? \(K\) counts the numbers needed to describe a state, and \(N\) counts the symbols it can carry. In a classical theory \(K=N\): each state is a probability list over \(N\) distinguishable configurations, and needs no more numbers than that. Suppose instead \(K>N\). Then the states are not all mixtures of one distinguishable set. Their description contains information that cannot be read out in one run. We will find that the principles force \(K=N^2\).

Problem 3.2. In the disc theory, every pure state has an antipode from which it is perfectly distinguishable. Show that no three states of the disc are perfectly distinguishable, so \(N=2\).

Worked solution

Write a state as a vector \(\mathbf r\) with \(|\mathbf r|\le1\); an affine functional is \(e(\mathbf r)=a+\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf r\). Its largest and smallest values on the disc are \(a+|\mathbf b|\) and \(a-|\mathbf b|\), so it is an effect exactly when \(a-|\mathbf b|\ge0\) and \(a+|\mathbf b|\le1\). Suppose \(e(\mathbf r_1)=1\) and \(e(\mathbf r_2)=0\). The first forces \(a+|\mathbf b|=1\) with \(\mathbf r_1\) the unit vector along \(\mathbf b\); the second forces \(a-|\mathbf b|=0\) with \(\mathbf r_2\) the unit vector opposite to \(\mathbf b\). Hence any two perfectly distinguishable states are antipodal pure states. Three states would need to be pairwise antipodal, which is impossible, so \(N=2\).

3.3 A Gallery of Theories

So far the framework allows any closed and bounded convex set as a state space. To see what the principles will exclude, we need examples whose states and effects we know completely. We take a few with small \(K\) and ask of each the same question: which pure states can one measurement separate? Classical probability theory answers one way. The other shapes answer differently, and they become the foils against which the principles are tested.

3.3.1 Classical bits and trits

Which pure states of a classical system can one measurement separate? Start with the shapes. The classical bit has states \((p,1-p)\), a segment with two pure ends, and \(K=N=2\). The classical trit has states \((p_1,p_2,p_3)\), a triangle with three pure corners, and \(K=N=3\). A classical system with \(N\) configurations has a simplex with \(N\) corners, the higher-dimensional version of a triangle. Every state is a mixture of corners in exactly one way.

What are its effects? Every effect is a combination of the indicator effects “configuration \(j\).” So an effect is a list of numbers \(f_j\) between \(0\) and \(1\), with probability \(\sum_jf_jp_j\). The indicators separate all the corners in one measurement. So every pure state is perfectly distinguishable from all the others at once. Classical pure states never overlap.

3.3.2 Squares, discs, and balls

Can a theory have more pure states than distinguishable ones? The square bit can. Its normalized states are \((p_X,p_Z)\in[0,1]^2\), with \(K=3\) and four pure states at the corners. Which pairs can one measurement separate? Every pair: \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\) are separated by the effect \(p_X\), and so are \((0,0)\) and \((1,0)\). Can three corners be separated at once? Any three corners include one, say \((0,0)\), whose two neighbours are the other two. An effect equal to \(1\) at it and \(0\) at both neighbours must be the affine function \(1-p_X-p_Z\), which is \(-1\) at the fourth corner. So no measurement separates three corners, and \(N=2\). The square bit has more pure states than distinguishable ones, but no two of its pure states overlap partially. It will reappear as the single system of a foil theory for pairs.

What happens when the boundary is smooth? Take the disc or the ball. Describe a state by a vector \(\mathbf r\) with \(|\mathbf r|\le1\), in two or three dimensions; pure states have \(|\mathbf r|=1\). For each unit vector \(\mathbf n\), consider the functional

\[e_{\mathbf n}(\mathbf r)=\tfrac12\,(1+\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r).\]

It lies between \(0\) and \(1\) on every state, and \(e_{\mathbf n}+e_{-\mathbf n}=u\). It equals \(1\) at the pure state \(\mathbf n\) and \(0\) at \(-\mathbf n\), so opposite pure states are perfectly distinguishable. What does it give on a pure state at angle \(\theta\) from \(\mathbf n\)? It gives \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)\), which lies strictly between \(0\) and \(1\) unless \(\theta\) is \(0\) or \(\pi\). So here, unlike the square, neighbouring pure states overlap. Since \(\tfrac12(1+\cos\theta)=\cos^2(\theta/2)\), this is the dependence on angle we met in the two-device experiment, appearing as pure geometry of a ball. The disc has \(K=3\) and the ball \(K=4\); both have \(N=2\).

Problem 3.3. In the square bit, try to distinguish the three corners \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((0,1)\): find the affine function equal to \(1\) at \((0,0)\) and \(0\) at the other two, and check whether it is an effect.

Worked solution

The function is \(1-p_X-p_Z\). At the fourth corner \((1,1)\) it equals \(-1\), so it is not an effect. No measurement singles out \((0,0)\) against both other corners, consistent with \(N=2\).
