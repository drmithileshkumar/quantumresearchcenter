15.1 Purification

Real systems are never isolated. They interact with environments we do not track, and their evolution, viewed alone, is not reversible. This chapter derives the general description of open systems from a single property of quantum composites, purification, which Chiribella, D’Ariano, and Perinotti took as the central principle of an alternative reconstruction.

Predict first. A qubit is in the maximally mixed state \(I/2\). Is there a pure state of the qubit and some second system whose reduced state on the qubit is \(I/2\)? Could the same be done for a classical bit in the state \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\)?

Compare your reasoning

For the qubit, yes: \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). For the classical bit, no: a pure state of two classical systems is a single pair of configurations, whose marginals are pure. Every quantum mixed state is part of a pure whole; no classical mixed state is.

15.1.1 Every mixed state has a pure extension

Write a density operator in its eigenbasis, \(\rho=\sum_i\lambda_i\ketbra ii\). Introduce a second system B with orthonormal states \(\ket i\) and form

\[\ket\Psi=\sum_i\sqrt{\lambda_i}\,\ket i_A\ket i_B .\]

Its partial trace over B is \(\rho\). So every state is the reduced state of a pure state of a larger system, a purification. In the Hilbert-space theory this is a theorem, a direct consequence of Chapter 13. Chiribella, D’Ariano, and Perinotti turned it around in 2011: they took purification, with uniqueness as below, as a principle and showed that together with five informational axioms it singles out quantum theory. The foil is classical theory, where no mixed state has a purification.

Purification gives mixed states a new reading. A mixed state need not express ignorance of which pure state was prepared; it can express that the system is correlated with something else. Both readings give the same predictions for the system, and Chapter 2 already noted that different ensembles of the same state cannot be distinguished.

15.1.2 Uniqueness up to a reversible map

If \(\ket\Psi\) and \(\ket{\Psi'}\) are two purifications of \(\rho\) on the same system B, then \(\ket{\Psi'}=(I\otimes V)\ket\Psi\) for some unitary \(V\) on B. This follows from the Schmidt decomposition: both have Schmidt coefficients \(\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) and A-basis the eigenvectors of \(\rho\), so they differ only in the B-basis, which a unitary relates. All purifications are equivalent, differing by a reversible change of the purifying system alone.

A consequence is steering. Any decomposition of \(\rho\) into pure states, \(\rho=\sum_kp_k\ketbra{\psi_k}{\psi_k}\), can be produced remotely: a suitable measurement on B, with outcome \(k\) occurring with probability \(p_k\), leaves A in \(\ket{\psi_k}\). Erwin Schrödinger noticed this in 1935; the general statement is due to Lane Hughston, Richard Jozsa, and William Wootters. It does not allow signaling, because A’s overall state remains \(\rho\) until the outcome is communicated.

Problem 15.1. Purify \(\rho=\mathrm{diag}(3/4,1/4)\) and check the reduced state.

Worked solution

\(\ket\Psi=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\ket{00}+\tfrac12\ket{11}\). The partial trace keeps \(\tfrac34\ketbra00+\tfrac14\ketbra11\) and removes the cross terms, giving \(\rho\).

15.2 Channels

An open system evolves by interacting reversibly with an environment and then being considered alone. Purification guarantees that every irreversible process can be seen this way.

15.2.1 Dilations and Kraus operators

Let the system interact with an environment E, initially in a pure state \(\ket{e_0}\), through a unitary \(U\), and then discard E. The system’s state becomes

\[\Phi(\rho)=\tr_E\!\big[U(\rho\otimes\ketbra{e_0}{e_0})U^\dagger\big]=\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger,\qquad K_k=\bra kU\ket{e_0},\]

where \(\ket k\) is any basis of E. The Kraus operators \(K_k\) satisfy \(\sum_kK_k^\dagger K_k=I\), which makes \(\Phi\) preserve trace. Such a map is a channel. It is completely positive: applied to part of a larger system, it still maps states to states, because it came from a unitary on a larger system. Transposition, which Chapter 9 excluded, is positive but not completely positive.

Stinespring’s theorem (1955) gives the converse: every completely positive, trace-preserving map has this form, a unitary dilation followed by discarding. In the reconstruction of Chiribella, D’Ariano, and Perinotti, the existence of such reversible dilations is derived from purification. A simple example is dephasing: with \(K_0=\sqrt{1-p}\,I\) and \(K_1=\sqrt p\,Z\), the diagonal of \(\rho\) is unchanged and the off-diagonal entries are multiplied by \(1-2p\).

15.2.2 Measurements as dilated unitaries

Measurements dilate in the same way. Couple the system to a probe by a unitary, then make a projective measurement on the probe. If outcome \(k\) of the probe leaves the system with the unnormalized state \(K_k\rho K_k^\dagger\), the measured effect on the system is \(E_k=K_k^\dagger K_k\), and the instrument is \(\mathcal I_k(\rho)=K_k\rho K_k^\dagger\). Conversely, every general measurement \(\{E_k\}\) can be realized this way, for example with \(K_k=\sqrt{E_k}\). This is Naimark’s theorem: every POVM is a projective measurement on a larger system.

The Lüders rule of Chapter 11 is the special case \(K_k=P_k\). The trine measurement of Chapter 8, with three effects on a qubit, is realized by coupling the qubit to a probe and measuring the probe in a basis of three states. Nothing in the general theory of measurement goes beyond unitary interaction, projective tests, and discarding systems.

Problem 15.2. Apply dephasing with \(p=0.1\) to \(\ketbra++\). What state results, and what is the probability that a test for \(\ket+\) passes?

Worked solution

\(\ketbra++\) has all entries \(\tfrac12\). Dephasing keeps the diagonal and multiplies the off-diagonal by \(0.8\), giving \(\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.4\\0.4&0.5\end{pmatrix}\). The test passes with probability \(\tr(\rho\ketbra++)=0.5+0.4=0.9\).

15.3 Decoherence and Measurement

Channels explain why quantum interference is rarely seen in everyday objects. They do not explain why measurements have single outcomes, and the course ends this part by stating that problem precisely.

15.3.1 Loss of interference

A qubit in \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) interacts with an environment so that the environment ends in \(\ket{e_0}\) or \(\ket{e_1}\) depending on the qubit’s basis state: \(a\ket0\ket{e_0}+b\ket1\ket{e_1}\). The qubit’s reduced state has diagonal \(|a|^2,|b|^2\), unchanged, and off-diagonal entries multiplied by \(\braket{e_1}{e_0}\). If the environment states are nearly orthogonal, the coherence is nearly gone, and in this basis the qubit behaves like a classical mixture. This is decoherence.

Decoherence is fast for large systems because environments have many parts. If each of \(n\) environmental particles is left in states with overlap \(\cos\theta\), the total overlap is \(\cos^n\theta\). For \(\theta=0.5\) and \(n=10\) this is about \(0.27\); for \(n=100\) it is about \(2\times10^{-6}\). A dust grain scattering air molecules loses positional coherence almost instantly. The basis singled out by the interaction, here \(\ket0,\ket1\), is called the pointer basis.

15.3.2 What the principles do not settle

Treat a measuring apparatus as a quantum system and let it interact unitarily with a qubit in a superposition. The result is an entangled superposition of “qubit 0, pointer reads 0” and “qubit 1, pointer reads 1.” Decoherence makes the two branches unable to interfere. It does not select one of them. Yet a measurement, as the framework of Chapter 1 assumes, produces a single classical record. The tension between universal unitary dynamics and definite outcomes is the measurement problem.

The reconstruction does not resolve it. The framework takes outcomes as primitive, and the principles then fix the theory that predicts their statistics. Interpretations answer the remaining question in different ways: by denying that one outcome is selected (Everett), by adding definite positions that guide outcomes (Bohm), by modifying the dynamics so that superpositions of large objects collapse (Ghirardi, Rimini, and Weber, with empirically distinct predictions), or by treating states as an agent’s or a relation’s information (QBism, relational, and Copenhagen-type views). A principles-first derivation narrows the question; it does not answer it. Chapter 17 returns to this point.

Problem 15.3. The decoherence argument shows that the qubit’s reduced state is \(\mathrm{diag}(|a|^2,|b|^2)\). Does this mean the qubit is in \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), with those probabilities?

Worked solution

Not by itself. The joint state is still a pure entangled superposition; the reduced state is the same as that of a proper mixture, so no measurement on the qubit alone can tell the difference, but the global state includes both branches. Reading the reduced state as ignorance of a definite outcome is precisely the step the measurement problem questions.
