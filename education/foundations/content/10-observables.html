10.1 Observables

The formalism derived so far speaks of states, effects, and transformations. Physics also speaks of quantities: energy, spin components, positions. This chapter shows that the familiar representation of quantities by self-adjoint operators is a bookkeeping device built on projective measurements, and derives the uncertainty relation from it.

Predict first. A qubit is prepared in the state \(\ket0\), with Bloch vector along \(+z\). Its spin components along \(x\) and \(y\) are each measured, on separate runs. Can both measurements have certain outcomes? Must at least one of them be completely random?

Compare your reasoning

Both are completely random: each gives \(\pm1\) with probability one half. The uncertainty relation says the product of their spreads is at least \(|\langle Z\rangle|=1\), and here it is exactly \(1\). For the state \(\ket+\), on the other hand, the \(x\)-component is certain, and the bound becomes zero.

10.1.1 Measurements with numerical outcomes

An observable, in the course’s terms, is a projective measurement whose outcomes are labeled by real numbers. Let the measurement have projections \(P_1,\dots,P_m\), mutually orthogonal and summing to \(I\), with distinct labels \(a_1,\dots,a_m\). The expected value of the label in state \(\rho\) is

\[\langle A\rangle=\sum_ka_k\tr(\rho P_k)=\tr(\rho A),\qquad A=\sum_ka_kP_k .\]

The single Hermitian matrix \(A\) packages the measurement and its labels. It is not a new physical ingredient: everything it predicts was already available from the effects \(P_k\). It earns its place because sums, products, and commutators of such matrices express physical relations compactly, and because, as Chapter 9 showed, Hermitian matrices also generate reversible transformations.

Why projective measurements? A projective measurement is repeatable (Chapter 11) and is the finest possible test of the property it measures. General measurements with numerical labels also exist and are called unsharp observables; they describe imperfect or deliberately gentle measurements and are equally derived from the effects of Chapter 8.

10.1.2 Self-adjoint operators and spectra

Conversely, every Hermitian matrix \(A\) defines an observable. By the spectral theorem it can be written uniquely as \(A=\sum_ka_kP_k\), where the \(a_k\) are its distinct eigenvalues and \(P_k\) the projections onto the corresponding eigenspaces. The eigenvalues are the possible outcomes, and \(\tr(\rho P_k)\) are their probabilities. A function of an observable is defined by relabeling: \(f(A)=\sum_kf(a_k)P_k\). Its variance is \((\Delta A)^2=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2\).

For a qubit, the spin component along a unit vector \(\mathbf n\), in units of \(\hbar/2\), is \(\mathbf n\cdot\vec\sigma\), with eigenvalues \(\pm1\). In the state with Bloch vector \(\mathbf r\) its expectation is \(\tr(\rho\,\mathbf n\cdot\vec\sigma)=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r\). The Bloch vector is therefore the list of expected spin components along \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\): a fiducial description in the sense of Chapter 2.

Problem 10.1. In the state \(\mathbf r=(0.6,0,0.8)\), find the probabilities of \(\pm1\) for the spin component along \(x\) and its variance.

Worked solution

\(\langle X\rangle=0.6\), so the probabilities are \((1\pm0.6)/2\): \(0.8\) and \(0.2\). Since \(X^2=I\), \((\Delta X)^2=1-0.36=0.64\).

10.2 Compatibility

In classical theory any collection of quantities can be measured together, because all are functions of the configuration. In quantum theory this fails, and the failure is expressed by commutators.

10.2.1 Joint measurability

Two observables are compatible, or jointly measurable, if there is a single measurement from whose outcomes both can be read off: a measurement with outcomes \((j,k)\) whose coarse-grainings over \(k\) and over \(j\) reproduce the two observables. For two qubits, \(Z\otimes I\) and \(I\otimes Z\) are compatible: measuring each qubit in its basis gives both. For a single qubit, \(X\) and \(Z\) are not.

Compatibility is a property of the observables, not of the state. Two incompatible observables can both have definite values in no state, while compatible ones can be sharpened together. This is the operational content of the statement that some quantities “cannot be known simultaneously.”

10.2.2 Commutation

For observables given by projective measurements, compatibility is equivalent to commutation: \(AB=BA\). If \(A\) and \(B\) commute, they have a common basis of eigenvectors, and the measurement onto the products \(P_jQ_k\) of their spectral projections reads both. Conversely, if a joint measurement exists with sharp marginals, its effects must be the products \(P_jQ_k\), and the projections of the two observables must commute.

The foil here is a hidden-value model, in which every observable has a definite value in every individual run and measurements merely reveal it. Such a model makes all observables jointly measurable in principle: one could simply read all values at once. Quantum theory has noncommuting observables, so it is not a hidden-value model in this naive sense. Chapter 12 shows that more sophisticated hidden-value models fail as well.

Problem 10.2. Show that \(X\) and \(Z\) have no common eigenvector, and compute \(XZ-ZX\).

Worked solution

The eigenvectors of \(Z\) are \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\); \(X\ket0=\ket1\), so neither is an eigenvector of \(X\). \(XZ-ZX=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{pmatrix}=-2iY\).

10.3 Uncertainty

Incompatibility has a quantitative form. The best known is a bound on the spreads of two observables in the same state.

10.3.1 The Robertson relation

Theorem (Robertson, 1929). For observables \(A\), \(B\) and any state,

\[\Delta A\,\Delta B\ \ge\ \tfrac12\,\big|\langle[A,B]\rangle\big|.\]

Proof for a pure state \(\ket\psi\). Let \(A_0=A-\langle A\rangle\) and \(B_0=B-\langle B\rangle\), and set \(\ket a=A_0\ket\psi\), \(\ket b=B_0\ket\psi\). Then \((\Delta A)^2=\braket aa\) and \((\Delta B)^2=\braket bb\), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives \(\Delta A\,\Delta B\ge|\braket ab|\ge|\mathrm{Im}\braket ab|\). Since \(\braket ab-\braket ba=\langle[A_0,B_0]\rangle=\langle[A,B]\rangle\), the imaginary part is \(\tfrac1{2i}\langle[A,B]\rangle\). \(\square\) A mixed state is an average of pure ones, and the bound survives averaging.

For spin components, \([X,Y]=2iZ\), so \(\Delta X\,\Delta Y\ge|\langle Z\rangle|\). In \(\ket0\), \(\Delta X=\Delta Y=1\) and \(\langle Z\rangle=1\): the bound is reached. In \(\ket+\), \(\Delta X=0\) and \(\langle Z\rangle=0\): the bound is zero and gives no constraint. The Robertson bound depends on the state. A state-independent alternative is the entropic relation of Hans Maassen and Jos Uffink (1988): for \(X\) and \(Z\) on a qubit, the Shannon entropies of the two outcome distributions sum to at least one bit in every state.

10.3.2 Preparation and measurement uncertainty

The Robertson relation concerns preparations. It compares the spread of \(A\) in one set of runs with the spread of \(B\) in another set of runs on identically prepared systems. It says that no preparation makes both sharp. It does not, by itself, say that measuring \(A\) disturbs \(B\), or that a joint approximate measurement must be inaccurate.

Those are separate questions about measurement uncertainty: the error with which a single device can approximate two incompatible observables, and the disturbance that measuring one causes to the other. They have their own relations, developed by Masanao Ozawa and by Paul Busch, Pekka Lahti, and Reinhard Werner, among others. Chapter 11 derives the qualitative core: any measurement that gains information about a state must, in general, disturb it.

Problem 10.3. Check the entropic relation for the states \(\ket0\) and \(\mathbf r=(1/\sqrt2,0,1/\sqrt2)\).

Worked solution

For \(\ket0\), the \(Z\) distribution is \((1,0)\), entropy \(0\), and the \(X\) distribution is \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\), entropy \(1\): the sum is exactly \(1\). For the second state both distributions are \(\big(\tfrac{1+1/\sqrt2}2,\tfrac{1-1/\sqrt2}2\big)\approx(0.854,0.146)\), each with entropy about \(0.60\), so the sum is about \(1.20\), above one bit.
