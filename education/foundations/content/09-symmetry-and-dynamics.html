9.1 Wigner’s Theorem

Chapter 8 stated that the reversible transformations are unitary conjugations. This chapter explains why, and derives the Schrödinger equation from the requirement that reversible change be continuous in time. No dynamical law is postulated.

Predict first. Complex conjugation of all amplitudes in a fixed basis maps pure states to pure states and preserves every transition probability \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\). Could a physical system evolve continuously in time from “nothing has happened” to “all amplitudes have been conjugated”?

Compare your reasoning

No. On the Bloch ball conjugation is a reflection, which reverses orientation, and no continuous path of rotations reaches a reflection. Applied to half of an entangled pair it would even produce negative probabilities. Antiunitary maps are symmetries of the transition probabilities, not possible results of continuous evolution.

9.1.1 Symmetries preserve transition probabilities

A reversible transformation maps the state space onto itself and respects mixing. Because the transition probability between pure states, \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\), is the probability that the test for one passes on the other, and because a reversible transformation carries tests for pure states to tests for their images, it preserves transition probabilities. Any map with this property is a symmetry in Wigner’s sense.

Wigner’s theorem. Every bijection of the pure states of \(\mathbb C^N\) that preserves all transition probabilities is induced by a map \(\ket\psi\mapsto U\ket\psi\) where \(U\) is either unitary or antiunitary. \(U\) is unique up to a phase factor.

Eugene Wigner stated the theorem in 1931; Valentine Bargmann gave a complete proof in 1964. Richard Kadison proved the corresponding statement for mixed states: every bijection of the density operators that respects mixing has the form \(\rho\mapsto U\rho U^\dagger\) or \(\rho\mapsto U\rho^TU^\dagger\), with \(\rho^T\) the transpose. The proof for a qubit is geometric: a bijection of the Bloch ball that respects mixing is a linear map preserving the sphere, hence a rotation or a rotation combined with a reflection.

9.1.2 Unitary and antiunitary maps

An antiunitary map preserves the magnitude of inner products but conjugates them: \(\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\braket\phi\psi^*\). The basic example is complex conjugation of components in a fixed basis. On a qubit, conjugation sends \(\rho\) to its transpose, which changes the sign of the \(Y\) component: \((x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,z)\). It reflects the Bloch ball through a plane.

Two arguments exclude antiunitary maps from continuous dynamics. The first is topological: the identity is unitary, a continuous path of transformations cannot jump between the two classes, and so everything in \(G_0\) is unitary. The second uses composition. Transposing one half of the entangled state \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\) gives a matrix with eigenvalue \(-1/2\), which is not a state. A transformation of one system must remain a valid transformation when that system is part of a larger one, so transposition is not a physical transformation at all, though it remains a symmetry of single-system transition probabilities. Antiunitary operators enter physics as symmetries, such as time reversal, which map one possible history to another, not as processes that happen.

Problem 9.1. Show that \(\rho\mapsto\rho^T\) maps the Bloch vector \((x,y,z)\) to \((x,-y,z)\).

Worked solution

\(\rho=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1+z&x-iy\\x+iy&1-z\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\rho^T=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1+z&x+iy\\x-iy&1-z\end{pmatrix}\), which is the matrix of \((x,-y,z)\). This is a reflection, of determinant \(-1\), so it is not a rotation.

9.2 Continuous Groups

Unitary maps describe single reversible changes. Time evolution is a family of them, one for each duration. Requiring the family to be consistent and continuous determines its form.

9.2.1 One-parameter groups of unitaries

Consider an isolated system whose dynamics does not change in time. Evolving for time \(s\) and then for time \(t\) is the same as evolving for \(t+s\), so the transformations satisfy \(T_{t+s}=T_tT_s\), with \(T_0\) the identity. Each \(T_t\) is represented by a unitary \(U(t)\), and the phases can be chosen so that \(U(t+s)=U(t)U(s)\). By continuous reversibility the dependence on \(t\) is continuous.

Theorem. Every continuous one-parameter group of \(N\times N\) unitary matrices has the form \(U(t)=e^{-iHt}\) for a unique Hermitian matrix \(H\).

Sketch. A continuous homomorphism from the real line into a matrix group is automatically differentiable. Differentiating \(U(t+s)=U(t)U(s)\) at \(s=0\) gives \(U'(t)=U(t)A\) with \(A=U'(0)\), whose solution with \(U(0)=I\) is \(U(t)=e^{tA}\). Differentiating \(U(t)^\dagger U(t)=I\) at \(t=0\) gives \(A^\dagger+A=0\), so \(A=-iH\) with \(H\) Hermitian. \(\square\) In infinite dimensions the same conclusion is Stone’s theorem, and \(H\) may be unbounded; Chapter 16 returns to it.

9.2.2 Generators and the Hamiltonian

The matrix \(H\) is the generator of the evolution. Adding a multiple of the identity to it changes \(U(t)\) only by a phase factor, which has no effect on states, so the generator is fixed up to an additive constant. To give it physical units we write \(U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}\), where \(\hbar\) converts the energy unit of \(H\) into a frequency, and call \(H\) the Hamiltonian.

The identification of the generator of time translations with energy is a physical link, not something the principles supply. It is the same link as in classical mechanics, where the Hamiltonian generates time evolution and is conserved when the dynamics does not depend on time. Here too, \(H\) commutes with every \(U(t)\), so the probabilities of its measurement outcomes never change.

A spin in a magnetic field along \(z\) has \(H=\tfrac12\hbar\omega Z\). Then \(U(t)=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\omega t/2},e^{i\omega t/2})\), which rotates the Bloch ball about the \(z\)-axis at angular frequency \(\omega\): the precession of the spin. For two qubits, \(H=\hbar JZ\otimes Z\) generates an interaction. Starting from the product state in which both qubits point along \(+x\), the time \(t=\pi/(4J)\) produces a state whose reduced state on each qubit is \(I/2\): continuous reversible evolution has created the maximal correlation that Chapter 7 required.

Problem 9.2. A qubit starts in \(\ket+=(1,1)^T/\sqrt2\) and evolves with \(H=\tfrac12\hbar\omega Z\). Find the probability that a test for \(\ket+\) passes at time \(t\).

Worked solution

\(U(t)\ket+=(e^{-i\omega t/2},e^{i\omega t/2})^T/\sqrt2\), and \(\braket{+}{U(t)+}=\cos(\omega t/2)\). The probability is \(\cos^2(\omega t/2)\), oscillating with period \(2\pi/\omega\) as the Bloch vector rotates around the \(z\)-axis.

9.3 The Schrödinger Equation

Differentiating the one-parameter group gives the familiar equations of motion. They are now consequences of continuity and reversibility rather than postulates.

9.3.1 Time translation and energy

For a pure state \(\ket{\psi(t)}=U(t)\ket{\psi(0)}\), differentiating \(U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}\) gives

\[i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=H\ket{\psi(t)},\]

the Schrödinger equation. For a density operator, \(\rho(t)=U(t)\rho(0)U(t)^\dagger\) gives \(\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-\frac i\hbar[H,\rho]\), where \([A,B]=AB-BA\). If \(H\) itself changes in time, the evolution over a short interval is still generated by the Hamiltonian at that time, and the equations keep the same form.

Eigenvectors of \(H\) are stationary: an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(E\) only acquires the phase \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), which changes no prediction. In a superposition of two eigenvectors the relative phase turns at the angular frequency \((E_1-E_0)/\hbar\), which is why energy differences, not energies, appear as observable frequencies.

9.3.2 The Heisenberg picture

Only probabilities \(\tr(\rho E)\) are observable. Since \(\tr\big(U\rho U^\dagger E\big)=\tr\big(\rho\,U^\dagger EU\big)\), we may leave the state fixed and evolve the effects instead: \(E(t)=U(t)^\dagger EU(t)\). This is the Heisenberg picture, in which

\[\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac i\hbar[H,E].\]

The two pictures make identical predictions. The Heisenberg picture makes conservation laws transparent: a quantity whose operator commutes with \(H\) does not change, so its outcome probabilities are constant in every state. Energy is the first example, and Chapter 10 turns to observables in general.

Problem 9.3. For \(H=\tfrac12\hbar\omega Z\), find \(X(t)\) in the Heisenberg picture.

Worked solution

\(U^\dagger XU\) multiplies the off-diagonal entries of \(X\) by \(e^{\pm i\omega t}\), giving \(X(t)=\cos(\omega t)X-\sin(\omega t)Y\). The \(x\)-component of the spin rotates into the \(y\)-component at angular frequency \(\omega\), the Heisenberg description of precession.
