4.1 Causality and No-Signaling

Physics never deals with an isolated system for long. Systems interact, and the description of two systems together must be consistent with the description of each alone. This chapter asks what the framework demands of such a description, and adds the second principle of the course.

Predict first. Two laboratories share a pair of systems. Each chooses one of two measurements and records one of two outcomes. Can the pair be such that the outcomes always satisfy “equal unless both chose the second measurement, in which case different,” while neither laboratory can learn anything about the other’s choice?

Compare your reasoning

Yes, as far as the framework is concerned. The Popescu–Rohrlich box of Section 4.3 does exactly this, with uniformly random local outcomes so that no signal passes. No classical or quantum pair can do it. So consistency with causality alone does not single out the correlations we observe.

4.1.1 Local statistics cannot depend on distant choices

Let two systems A and B be prepared together. Laboratory A applies measurement \(x\) and obtains outcome \(a\); laboratory B applies measurement \(y\) and obtains \(b\). The joint state predicts probabilities \(p(a,b\mid x,y)\). The probability of a local outcome is obtained by summing over the distant one:

\[p(a\mid x,y)=\sum_b p(a,b\mid x,y).\]

If this depended on \(y\), laboratory B could send messages to A by its choice of measurement, instantly and regardless of distance. The framework already forbids later choices from influencing earlier records; for separated laboratories whose operations need not occur in any fixed order, the same reasoning forbids either from signaling to the other. We therefore require no-signaling: \(p(a\mid x,y)\) does not depend on \(y\), and similarly for B.

No-signaling means that the predictions for A alone are those of some state of A, independent of what is done to B. This state, the marginal or reduced state, is what an observer with access to A alone would ascribe. It exists in every theory that respects causality, not only in quantum theory.

4.1.2 Product states and correlations

The simplest joint preparations prepare A and B independently, in states \(\omega_A\) and \(\omega_B\). Their outcome probabilities factorize: \(p(a,b)=p(a)\,p(b)\) for every pair of local effects. We write the resulting joint state \(\omega_A\otimes\omega_B\). A joint outcome formed by recording effect \(e\) on A and \(f\) on B is written \(e\otimes f\), and has probability \(e(\omega_A)f(\omega_B)\) on a product state.

Mixing product states produces correlation: toss a coin, and prepare both systems in state 0 on heads and both in state 1 on tails. Outcomes are then perfectly correlated, but the correlation is explained by the shared coin. States of this kind, mixtures of products, are called separable. Every correlation in classical probability theory is of this type. Whether a theory permits joint states that are not mixtures of products, and what correlations those states produce, are questions the framework leaves open.

Problem 4.1. A joint state of two classical bits gives \(p(0,0)=0.4\), \(p(0,1)=0.1\), \(p(1,0)=0.2\), \(p(1,1)=0.3\). Find the marginal states and decide whether the joint state is a product.

Worked solution

A’s marginal is \((0.5,0.5)\) and B’s is \((0.6,0.4)\). The product would give \(p(0,0)=0.3\), not \(0.4\), so the state is correlated. Like every classical joint state it is a mixture of products of pure states: \(0.4\) of \((0,0)\), \(0.1\) of \((0,1)\), and so on.

4.2 Local Tomography

How many numbers does a joint state need? The marginals do not suffice, because correlated and uncorrelated states can have the same marginals. The correlations between local outcomes contain more. Whether they contain everything is the content of our second principle.

4.2.1 Joint states from local statistics

Local tomography. The state of a composite system is determined by the joint probabilities of outcomes of measurements performed separately on its components.

The principle says that a whole is no more than its parts and their correlations. If it failed, there would be joint states that agree on every local measurement and every correlation between local measurements, yet differ in the prediction of some global measurement on the pair. Two observers who share their records completely could not reconstruct the joint state; some of its information would be accessible only by bringing the systems together. Local tomography rules out this kind of holism. It is a substantive claim about the world, testable in principle, and Chapter 7 exhibits a consistent theory, quantum theory with real amplitudes, that violates it.

4.2.2 The rule K_AB = K_A K_B

Choose fiducial outcomes \(f_1,\dots,f_{K_A}\) for A and \(g_1,\dots,g_{K_B}\) for B. By local tomography the joint probabilities \(p(f_i,g_j)\) fix the joint state, so \(K_{AB}\le K_AK_B\). Conversely, pick \(K_A\) linearly independent states of A and \(K_B\) of B; their \(K_AK_B\) product states are linearly independent as joint states, since their fiducial probabilities form the products of two invertible arrays. So \(K_{AB}\ge K_AK_B\), and

\[K_{AB}=K_A\,K_B .\]

Without local tomography only the inequality \(K_{AB}\ge K_AK_B\) survives. Mathematically, the rule says that joint states live in the tensor product \(\mathbb R^{K_A}\otimes\mathbb R^{K_B}\) of the spaces of local states, whose dimension is the product. The tensor product is not postulated; it is the space of arrays \(p(f_i,g_j)\) that local tomography makes sufficient.

A second counting rule concerns distinguishability. Products of perfectly distinguishable states are perfectly distinguishable by local measurements, so \(N_{AB}\ge N_AN_B\). We shall assume, with Hardy, that composites have no further distinguishable states: \(N_{AB}=N_AN_B\). This holds for classical and quantum theory and expresses that a pair of systems carries exactly the messages of its parts. Combined with \(K_{AB}=K_AK_B\), it severely restricts how \(K\) can depend on \(N\), as Chapter 5 shows.

Problem 4.2. Verify \(K_{AB}=K_AK_B\) and \(N_{AB}=N_AN_B\) for two classical trits, and compute \(K\) for a pair of square bits under local tomography.

Worked solution

A pair of trits is a classical system with \(9\) configurations, so \(N_{AB}=9=3\times3\) and \(K_{AB}=9=3\times3\). A pair of square bits has \(K_{AB}=3\times3=9\), with joint states fixed by the probabilities of pairs of local fiducial outcomes.

4.3 Correlations Beyond Products

Local tomography and no-signaling restrict what a joint state is, but not which joint states exist. A simple game measures how strongly two systems can be correlated, and it separates classical theory, quantum theory, and the foil of this chapter.

4.3.1 The CHSH game

Each laboratory receives a random bit, \(x\) for A and \(y\) for B, and must output a bit, \(a\) and \(b\), without communicating. They win if \(a\oplus b=xy\), where \(\oplus\) is addition modulo 2: outputs should agree unless both inputs are \(1\), when they should differ. The players may share any prepared joint state and choose measurements depending on their inputs. The game is named after Clauser, Horne, Shimony, and Holt.

Suppose the outputs are fixed in advance by a shared plan, possibly chosen at random. Write the outputs as signs \(A_x=(-1)^{a}\) and \(B_y=(-1)^{b}\). A deterministic plan assigns four signs \(A_0,A_1,B_0,B_1\), and

\[A_0B_0+A_0B_1+A_1B_0-A_1B_1=A_0(B_0+B_1)+A_1(B_0-B_1)=\pm2,\]

since one of \(B_0\pm B_1\) is zero and the other is \(\pm2\). Averaging over random plans, the expected value \(S\) of this combination satisfies \(|S|\le2\). In terms of the game, the winning probability is \(\tfrac12+S/8\), so preestablished plans win at most \(3/4\) of the time. Every separable state is a random plan of this kind, so classical theory obeys the same limit.

4.3.2 Boxworld and the PR box

Sandu Popescu and Daniel Rohrlich asked in 1994 whether causality alone limits the game. Consider the joint behavior

\[p(a,b\mid x,y)=\begin{cases}\tfrac12 & a\oplus b=xy,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\]

Each local outcome is uniformly random whatever the other laboratory does, so it does not signal. Yet the game is won with certainty: \(S=4\), the largest value the expression can take. Quantum pairs reach only \(S=2\sqrt2\approx2.83\), a bound proved by Boris Tsirelson that Chapter 13 derives.

The PR box is a joint state in boxworld, a theory whose elementary systems are square bits and whose composites contain every joint state allowed by no-signaling and local tomography. Boxworld satisfies the framework, no-signaling, finiteness, and local tomography. It is therefore a foil showing that these principles do not yet select quantum theory. What boxworld lacks is dynamics: David Gross, Markus Müller, Roger Colbeck, and Oscar Dahlsten proved in 2010 that its only reversible transformations are local operations and permutations of systems, so two square bits can never be brought into correlation reversibly. The principle of Chapter 6, continuous reversibility, is exactly what boxworld lacks.

Problem 4.3. Show that the plan “always output 0” wins the CHSH game with probability \(3/4\), and that no plan with fixed outputs does better.

Worked solution

With \(a=b=0\), \(a\oplus b=0\), which equals \(xy\) for three of the four equally likely input pairs. For any deterministic plan \(S=\pm2\), so the winning probability \(\tfrac12+S/8\) is at most \(\tfrac12+\tfrac14=\tfrac34\).
