13.1 Reduced States

Chapter 4 set up composite systems in any theory; Chapters 7 and 8 found that quantum composites are described on the tensor product of Hilbert spaces. This chapter draws out the consequences: pure wholes with mixed parts, entanglement, and correlations that lie strictly between the classical limit and boxworld.

Predict first. Two qubits are in the pure state \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). What state should an observer with access to the first qubit alone assign to it? Is it pure?

Compare your reasoning

The maximally mixed state \(I/2\): every local measurement gives each outcome with probability one half. The joint state is pure, yet its part is maximally mixed. In classical theory a pure joint state always has pure parts.

13.1.1 Partial trace from local statistics

By no-signaling (Chapter 4), the statistics of measurements on A alone define a state \(\rho_A\): for every effect \(E\) on A, \(\tr(\rho_AE)=\tr\big(\rho_{AB}(E\otimes I)\big)\). Since this holds for all \(E\), it determines \(\rho_A\) uniquely. The resulting map \(\rho_{AB}\mapsto\rho_A\) is the partial trace over B. In components, with bases \(\ket i\) of A and \(\ket j\) of B,

\[(\rho_A)_{ii'}=\sum_j\big(\rho_{AB}\big)_{ij,\,i'j}.\]

The partial trace is not a separate postulate. It is what the marginal state of Chapter 4 must be once states are density operators and local effects are \(E\otimes I\).

13.1.2 Pure joint states with mixed parts

For \(\ket{\Phi^+}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\), the joint density operator has entries \(\tfrac12\) in the four corners of the \(4\times4\) matrix indexed by \(00,01,10,11\). Summing over the second index gives \(\rho_A=\tfrac12\ketbra00+\tfrac12\ketbra11=I/2\). The whole is in a pure state, maximally specified, while each part is maximally uncertain.

This cannot happen classically. A pure state of two classical systems is a single pair of configurations, and its marginals are single configurations, hence pure. In quantum theory the information in a pure joint state can reside entirely in correlations, with none in the parts. This is the property that Chapter 15 elevates to the purification principle.

Problem 13.1. Find \(\rho_A\) for \(\cos\alpha\ket{00}+\sin\alpha\ket{11}\). For which \(\alpha\) is it pure?

Worked solution

The cross terms \(\ket{00}\bra{11}\) disappear under the partial trace, leaving \(\rho_A=\mathrm{diag}(\cos^2\alpha,\sin^2\alpha)\). It is pure only when \(\cos\alpha\) or \(\sin\alpha\) is zero, that is, when the joint state is a product.

13.2 Entanglement

Pure joint states with mixed parts are the simplest examples of entanglement. We define it in general and find the canonical form of any pure joint state.

13.2.1 Product and entangled states

A pure joint state is a product if it has the form \(\ket a\otimes\ket b\), and entangled otherwise. A pure state is a product exactly when its reduced states are pure. For two qubits with \(\ket\psi=\sum c_{ij}\ket{ij}\), the state is a product exactly when the \(2\times2\) array of coefficients has rank one, that is, when \(c_{00}c_{11}=c_{01}c_{10}\).

For mixed states, the definition follows Chapter 4: a state is separable if it is a mixture of products, and entangled otherwise. Separable states can be prepared by local operations and shared randomness; entangled states cannot. Deciding whether a given mixed state is separable is in general a hard problem, but for two qubits it has a simple test, due to Asher Peres and the Horodecki family: a state is entangled exactly when its partial transpose has a negative eigenvalue.

13.2.2 The Schmidt decomposition

Write a pure state as \(\ket\psi=\sum_{ij}c_{ij}\ket i\ket j\) and regard \(c\) as a matrix. Its singular value decomposition gives orthonormal bases \(\ket{a_k}\) of A and \(\ket{b_k}\) of B and nonnegative numbers \(s_k\) with

\[\ket\psi=\sum_ks_k\ket{a_k}\otimes\ket{b_k},\qquad\sum_ks_k^2=1 .\]

This is the Schmidt decomposition. It shows that \(\rho_A=\sum_ks_k^2\ketbra{a_k}{a_k}\) and \(\rho_B=\sum_ks_k^2\ketbra{b_k}{b_k}\) have the same nonzero eigenvalues. The number of nonzero \(s_k\) is the Schmidt rank; the state is a product exactly when it is one. The entropy of the distribution \(s_k^2\) measures how entangled the pure state is, and it is the same whether computed from A or from B.

Problem 13.2. Decide whether \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})/2\) and \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}-\ket{11})/2\) are products.

Worked solution

For the first, \(c_{00}c_{11}=\tfrac14=c_{01}c_{10}\): it is \(\ket+\ket+\). For the second, \(c_{00}c_{11}=-\tfrac14\ne\tfrac14\): it is entangled. In fact its Schmidt coefficients are both \(1/\sqrt2\), so it is maximally entangled.

13.3 Bell Inequalities

The CHSH game of Chapter 4 separated classical theory (\(S\le2\)) from boxworld (\(S=4\)). Quantum theory lies strictly between, and the principles explain why it stops where it does.

13.3.1 The CHSH bound for local models

A local hidden-variable model assigns to each run a variable \(\lambda\), drawn from a distribution independent of the measurement choices, and lets each laboratory’s outcome depend only on its own choice and \(\lambda\). For each \(\lambda\) the outputs are then a preestablished plan, possibly randomized locally, and the argument of Chapter 4 gives \(|S|\le2\). This is Bell’s theorem in the form of John Clauser, Michael Horne, Abner Shimony, and Richard Holt.

Quantum theory violates the bound. Share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) and measure the \(\pm1\) observables \(A_0=Z\), \(A_1=X\) on the first qubit and \(B_0=(Z+X)/\sqrt2\), \(B_1=(Z-X)/\sqrt2\) on the second. For this state and observables in the \(xz\)-plane of the Bloch ball, \(\langle\mathbf a\cdot\vec\sigma\otimes\mathbf b\cdot\vec\sigma\rangle=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b\). Each of the four correlations equals \(1/\sqrt2\) in magnitude with the right sign, so \(S=4/\sqrt2=2\sqrt2\approx2.83\). Loophole-free experiments in 2015, by groups in Delft, Vienna, and Boulder, observed violations of Bell inequalities while closing the main experimental loopholes together.

13.3.2 Tsirelson’s bound, no-signaling, and no-cloning

Tsirelson’s bound. For any quantum state and any \(\pm1\) observables, \(|S|\le2\sqrt2\). Let \(C=A_0\otimes(B_0+B_1)+A_1\otimes(B_0-B_1)\). Using \(A_x^2=B_y^2=I\), a direct calculation gives \(C^2=4\,I-[A_0,A_1]\otimes[B_0,B_1]\). Each commutator has norm at most \(2\), so \(C^2\) has eigenvalues at most \(8\), and \(|S|=|\langle C\rangle|\le\sqrt8=2\sqrt2\). The PR box is excluded because quantum correlations are expectations of operators on a Hilbert space, and the commutators are bounded.

No-signaling is automatic: any operation on B, described by a map that preserves the trace, leaves \(\rho_A\) unchanged, since the partial trace over B ignores it. And no unknown state can be copied. If a unitary \(U\) satisfied \(U\ket\psi\ket0=\ket\psi\ket\psi\) and \(U\ket\phi\ket0=\ket\phi\ket\phi\), comparing inner products would give \(\braket\phi\psi=\braket\phi\psi^2\), so \(\braket\phi\psi\) is \(0\) or \(1\). Only perfectly distinguishable or identical states can be cloned, which is the quantum form of the overlap of pure states found in Chapter 3.

Problem 13.3. With the same state and \(A_0=Z\), \(A_1=X\), use \(B_0=Z\), \(B_1=X\) instead. Compute \(S\).

Worked solution

The correlations are \(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b\): \(Z\)–\(Z\) gives \(1\), \(Z\)–\(X\) and \(X\)–\(Z\) give \(0\), \(X\)–\(X\) gives \(1\). So \(S=1+0+0-1=0\). The violation depends on choosing measurements at intermediate angles.
