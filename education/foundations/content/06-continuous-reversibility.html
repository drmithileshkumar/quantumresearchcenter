6.1 The Principle

Chapter 5 found that a finite classical system cannot change continuously and reversibly. This chapter turns that failure into a principle and draws its first two consequences: outcomes of measurements on pure states must sometimes be random, and the state space of a bit must be round.

Predict first. Two pure states can be told apart perfectly by a measurement with outcomes 0 and 1. A continuous reversible process carries the first into the second. Must the process pass through a pure state for which the outcome is uncertain?

Compare your reasoning

Yes. Reversible maps keep pure states pure, so every intermediate state is pure, and the probability of outcome 1 moves continuously from \(0\) to \(1\). Somewhere it equals \(1/2\). That pure state yields a random outcome without any ignorance behind it.

6.1.1 Reversible change as a group

The reversible transformations of a system form a group: the composition of two reversible transformations is reversible, the identity is reversible, and each has an inverse. Each acts on the state space as a linear map carrying it onto itself, and so permutes the pure states (Section 5.2). Because these maps preserve a bounded convex set that spans the state space, they form a compact group. We call it \(G\).

Continuous reversibility. For any two pure states there is a reversible transformation taking the first to the second that can be connected to the identity by a continuous path of reversible transformations.

Equivalently, the part \(G_0\) of the group that is connected to the identity acts transitively on the pure states: any pure state can be carried to any other within it. The principle combines two ideas. Transitivity says no pure state is special; all are equivalent up to a reversible change. Connectedness says the change can happen gradually.

6.1.2 Why continuity is reasonable

Physical evolution unfolds in time. If a system evolves reversibly from time \(0\) to time \(t\), the transformations obtained by stopping at intermediate times trace a path from the identity to the final transformation. If the dynamics depends continuously on time, this path is continuous. Every transformation that arises as continuous time evolution therefore lies in \(G_0\).

Transitivity is a statement of homogeneity: for a fundamental system, any pure preparation can be converted into any other by suitably arranged dynamics. It does not say that every system actually evolves this way, only that the theory allows it. Classical theory with finitely many configurations violates the principle, as we saw. So does boxworld, whose reversible transformations are discrete. The square bit fails too: the linear maps of a square onto itself are its eight rotations and reflections, and none except the identity lies on a continuous path.

Problem 6.1. In the disc theory, rotations about the center map the disc onto itself. Show that the disc satisfies continuous reversibility.

Worked solution

A rotation by angle \(\alpha\) is linear, maps the disc onto itself, and is undone by the rotation by \(-\alpha\). Rotation by \(t\alpha\), for \(t\) from \(0\) to \(1\), is a continuous path from the identity. Any pure state on the circle can be rotated to any other, so the rotations act transitively on pure states.

6.2 Intrinsic Randomness

The first consequence needs nothing beyond the principle and the existence of two distinguishable states. It is also the most important for the interpretation of the theory.

6.2.1 States between distinguishable states

Theorem. In any theory with \(N\ge2\) that satisfies continuous reversibility, there is a pure state and an effect whose probability on it is exactly \(1/2\).

Proof. Let \(\omega_0\) and \(\omega_1\) be perfectly distinguishable pure states, with an effect \(e\) such that \(e(\omega_0)=0\) and \(e(\omega_1)=1\). By the principle there is a continuous path \(T_t\), \(0\le t\le1\), of reversible transformations with \(T_0\) the identity and \(T_1\omega_0=\omega_1\). Each \(\psi_t=T_t\omega_0\) is pure, because reversible maps send pure states to pure states. The function \(f(t)=e(\psi_t)\) is continuous, being a linear functional applied to a continuously varying state, with \(f(0)=0\) and \(f(1)=1\). By the intermediate value theorem there is a \(t\) with \(f(t)=1/2\). \(\square\)

The same argument produces pure states with any probability between \(0\) and \(1\). It also shows that a theory satisfying the principle has infinitely many pure states, since each value of \(f\) requires a different one, while it has only finitely many distinguishable ones. So \(K>N\), and the state space is not a simplex.

6.2.2 Randomness without ignorance

In classical theory a probability of \(1/2\) on a pure state can arise only from a noisy measurement. Here the effect \(e\) is the sharp effect that perfectly distinguishes \(\omega_0\) from \(\omega_1\), and \(\psi_t\) is pure, so it is not a mixture of states with definite outcomes. Within the theory, the randomness of the outcome cannot be read as ignorance of a finer state; there is no finer state.

The conclusion concerns the theory’s own states. A different theory might add hidden states beneath these, as Bell’s model of Chapter 1 does, but such a theory has a different state space and must answer to its own principles; Chapter 12 shows what it must give up. What we have derived is that randomness without ignorance follows from finiteness and continuous reversibility. It was not assumed in the framework, which treats deterministic and random theories alike.

Problem 6.2. In the disc, take \(\omega_0=-\mathbf n\), \(\omega_1=\mathbf n\), the effect \(e_{\mathbf n}(\mathbf r)=\tfrac12(1+\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\), and the path of rotations by angle \(\pi t\). Compute \(f(t)\) and find where it equals \(1/2\).

Worked solution

The rotated state makes angle \(\pi-\pi t\) with \(\mathbf n\), so \(f(t)=\tfrac12(1+\cos(\pi-\pi t))=\tfrac12(1-\cos\pi t)\). It equals \(1/2\) at \(t=1/2\), where the state is perpendicular to \(\mathbf n\).

6.3 The Shape of a Bit

The second consequence concerns geometry. A compact group acting transitively on pure states imposes a strong symmetry on the state space. For a bit, the outcome is a ball.

6.3.1 Symmetry and the state space

Average any pure state over the group \(G\), weighting all transformations equally. (For a compact group such an average, with respect to its Haar measure, exists.) The result \(\mu\) is a state that every reversible transformation leaves unchanged. Because \(G\) acts transitively, the average is the same whichever pure state we start from. We call \(\mu\) the maximally mixed state.

A compact group of linear maps also preserves some inner product, obtained by averaging any inner product over the group. Measure states from \(\mu\) using this inner product. Every reversible transformation is then a rotation or reflection about \(\mu\), so it preserves distances from \(\mu\). Since any pure state can be carried to any other, all pure states lie at the same distance from \(\mu\): the pure states lie on a sphere.

This already excludes polygons and polyhedra other than the segment. It does not yet say that every point of the sphere is a pure state, or what its dimension is.

6.3.2 Balls of every dimension

For a bit, \(N=2\), more can be shown. Masanes and Müller proved in 2011 that, in a theory satisfying the principles of this course (including the two stated formally in Chapter 8), a system with \(N=2\) has a state space that is a Euclidean ball of some dimension \(d\), with every point of its boundary sphere a pure state and every pair of antipodal points perfectly distinguishable. The proof uses the classification of compact groups acting transitively on spheres and lies beyond this chapter; Borivoje Dakić and Časlav Brukner gave a related argument. We take the result as established, with its assumptions stated.

Each \(d\) defines a candidate theory of a bit. For \(d=2\) the bit is the disc, which is the qubit of quantum theory with real amplitudes. For \(d=3\) it is the ball of Section 3.3, which will turn out to be the ordinary qubit. For \(d=5\) it is the bit of quantum theory with quaternion amplitudes. Every \(d\ge2\) gives a consistent single system: each satisfies everything assumed so far. (The case \(d=1\), a segment, is the classical bit, already excluded.) A single system cannot tell us which ball we inhabit. Chapter 7 shows that composition can.

Problem 6.3. For the disc and for the three-dimensional ball, what is \(K\), and where is \(\mu\)?

Worked solution

A ball of dimension \(d\) has normalized states with \(d\) free parameters plus normalization, so \(K=d+1\): \(K=3\) for the disc and \(K=4\) for the ball. In both, \(\mu\) is the center, the only point fixed by every rotation.
