12.1 Noncontextual Values

Chapter 6 derived randomness for the states of the theory. Could the randomness still reflect ignorance of deeper variables that fix every outcome in advance? This chapter shows what such variables would have to be like, and adds a second, independent derivation of the Born rule.

Predict first. Nine observables, each with values \(\pm1\), are arranged in a \(3\times3\) grid. The observables in each row and in each column can be measured together. Quantum theory says the product of the outcomes is \(+1\) for every row and for the first two columns, and \(-1\) for the third column. Can each observable carry a preassigned value that a measurement simply reveals?

Compare your reasoning

No. Multiplying all nine values row by row gives \(+1\); multiplying them column by column gives \(-1\). The same nine numbers cannot do both. This is the Peres–Mermin square, constructed explicitly in Section 12.1.

12.1.1 Assigning outcomes in advance

A value assignment gives each observable \(A\) a definite value \(v(A)\), one of its eigenvalues, which any measurement of \(A\) would reveal. For it to be consistent with the observed statistics, it must respect the relations among compatible observables. If \(A\) and \(B\) commute, they can be measured together, and the product of their outcomes is the outcome of \(AB\) measured in the same run. So we require \(v(AB)=v(A)v(B)\) for commuting \(A\) and \(B\).

The assignment is noncontextual if \(v(A)\) does not depend on which other compatible observables are measured alongside \(A\). This is the natural reading of “the value was there before the measurement”: the value of \(A\) is a property of the system, not of the experimental arrangement. A contextual assignment allows \(v(A)\) to depend on the whole arrangement.

12.1.2 The Peres–Mermin square

For two qubits, arrange nine observables, each with eigenvalues \(\pm1\):

\[\begin{array}{ccc} X\otimes I & I\otimes X & X\otimes X\\ I\otimes Z & Z\otimes I & Z\otimes Z\\ X\otimes Z & Z\otimes X & Y\otimes Y\end{array}\]

The three observables in each row commute, and so do the three in each column. Multiplying them, using \(XZ=-iY\) and \(ZX=iY\), gives \(+I\) for each row and for the first two columns, and \(-I\) for the third column: \((X\otimes X)(Z\otimes Z)(Y\otimes Y)=(XZY)\otimes(XZY)=(-iI)\otimes(-iI)=-I\).

A noncontextual assignment must therefore satisfy six equations: the product of the values in each row is \(+1\), the product in the first two columns is \(+1\), and in the third column it is \(-1\). Multiplying the three row equations gives the product of all nine values as \(+1\). Multiplying the three column equations gives \(-1\). Each value appears once in a row and once in a column, so these are the same product. No assignment exists, whatever the state. Asher Peres and David Mermin found this argument in 1990.

Problem 12.1. Verify that the third row multiplies to \(+I\).

Worked solution

\((X\otimes Z)(Z\otimes X)(Y\otimes Y)=(XZY)\otimes(ZXY)\). Now \(XZY=-iYY=-iI\) and \(ZXY=iYY=iI\), so the product is \((-i)(i)I=I\).

12.2 Gleason’s Theorem

The Born rule was derived in Chapters 7 and 8 from the shape of the state space. A theorem of Andrew Gleason derives it from a different starting point, using only the structure of projective measurements.

12.2.1 Frame functions

Suppose we know only which projective measurements exist on \(\mathbb C^N\): every orthogonal decomposition of the identity into projections. Ask for an assignment of probabilities \(f(P)\in[0,1]\) to projections such that, for every projective measurement \(\{P_k\}\), the probabilities sum to one. Crucially, \(f(P)\) depends only on \(P\), not on the measurement it belongs to. Such an \(f\) is called a frame function.

In the operational framework this independence is automatic: an effect is defined by its probabilities, so a projection that occurs in two measurements is the same effect in both. Here it is a hypothesis, the noncontextuality of probabilities, and Gleason asks what it implies on its own.

12.2.2 The Born rule without further assumptions

Gleason’s theorem (1957). If \(N\ge3\), every frame function has the form \(f(P)=\tr(\rho P)\) for a unique density operator \(\rho\).

The proof is long and uses the continuity of frame functions on the sphere of unit vectors in three real dimensions, extended to all \(N\ge3\). The theorem fails for \(N=2\): a qubit’s projective measurements are just antipodal pairs of points on the Bloch sphere, and any function with \(g(\mathbf n)+g(-\mathbf n)=1\) is a frame function, including wildly discontinuous ones. Paul Busch showed in 2003 that the conclusion holds for every \(N\), including \(2\), if probabilities are assigned to all effects \(0\le E\le I\) and are additive over all general measurements.

Gleason’s theorem also excludes determinism. A noncontextual value assignment to projections would be a frame function taking only the values \(0\) and \(1\). But \(\tr(\rho P)\) varies continuously as \(P\) is rotated, and a continuous function on a connected set that takes both values must take everything in between. So for \(N\ge3\) no such assignment exists.

Problem 12.2. For a qubit, define \(g(\mathbf n)=1\) if \(n_z>0\), \(g(\mathbf n)=0\) if \(n_z<0\), and on the equator choose \(1\) on one half-circle and \(0\) on the other. Show that \(g\) is a frame function, and explain why it is not of Born form.

Worked solution

Every projective measurement on a qubit is a pair \(\{\mathbf n,-\mathbf n\}\), and the definition makes exactly one of them \(1\), so the values sum to one. A Born-form function \(\tr(\rho P_{\mathbf n})=\tfrac12(1+\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n)\) is continuous in \(\mathbf n\), while \(g\) jumps. This is why Bell could build a deterministic model for a single qubit.

12.3 What Contextuality Excludes

The Peres–Mermin square and Gleason’s theorem both show that quantum outcomes cannot be preassigned in a noncontextual way. It is important to state exactly what this excludes and what it does not.

12.3.1 The Kochen–Specker theorem

Kochen–Specker theorem (1967). For \(N\ge3\), there is no assignment of values \(0\) or \(1\) to all projections on \(\mathbb C^N\) such that, in every projective measurement, exactly one projection receives the value \(1\).

Simon Kochen and Ernst Specker proved it with 117 vectors in three real dimensions; later proofs use fewer, for example 18 vectors in four dimensions found by Adán Cabello and collaborators. The Peres–Mermin square is a proof in terms of observables in dimension four. These proofs are state-independent: they show that no value assignment exists at all, not merely that one fails for some state. Experiments have tested the square directly, for example with trapped ions by Gerhard Kirchmair and collaborators in 2009, confirming the quantum predictions that make noncontextual assignments impossible.

12.3.2 Contextual hidden variables

The theorem does not exclude hidden variables. It excludes those whose values are independent of the measurement context. A theory in which the outcome of measuring \(A\) depends on which compatible observables are measured with it, or on the details of the apparatus, escapes the argument. Bohmian mechanics is such a theory: particles have definite positions, but the outcomes of other measurements depend on the whole experimental arrangement.

So the situation is this. The states of quantum theory are intrinsically random, as Chapter 6 derived. Deeper variables could be added, but only at the price of contextuality, and Chapter 13 adds a second price for composite systems: nonlocality. Whether such variables exist is not decided by the principles of this course. What is decided is that a noncontextual, predetermined world would not be described by quantum theory.

Problem 12.3. Why does the Peres–Mermin argument fail for a single qubit?

Worked solution

On a single qubit, two observables commute only if one is a function of the other: distinct spin components never commute. There is no grid of observables with nontrivial compatible rows and columns, and indeed noncontextual models such as Bell’s exist for one qubit. Contextuality proofs need at least three dimensions.
