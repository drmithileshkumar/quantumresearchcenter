14.1 Indistinguishability

Composite systems in Chapter 13 had parts that could be told apart: one qubit in Alice’s laboratory, one in Bob’s. Two electrons, or two photons, are different. No measurement can determine which is “the first.” This chapter derives what follows from taking that operationally, and is explicit about what does not follow.

Predict first. Two photons enter a balanced beam splitter, one through each input port, at the same moment and in identical modes. How often do the two output detectors click together? How often would they if the photons were distinguishable, say by polarization?

Compare your reasoning

For identical photons, never: both leave through the same port. For distinguishable photons, half the time. The difference, observed by Hong, Ou, and Mandel in 1987, comes from the symmetry of the two-photon state, derived in Section 14.2.

14.1.1 Labels that no measurement can read

Describe two systems of the same type on \(\mathcal H\otimes\mathcal H\), labeling the factors 1 and 2 for bookkeeping. Let \(S\) be the swap operator, \(S\ket a\ket b=\ket b\ket a\). The systems are indistinguishable if no physical operation can depend on the labels: every effect \(E\) of the pair satisfies \(SES=E\), and so does every Hamiltonian.

This is an operational principle in the spirit of the course. The labels are not properties of the systems; they belong to our description, and the principle says the description must not let them do physical work. Its foil is a theory of labeled particles, which is consistent and is the correct description whenever the particles differ in some property, such as mass or a long-lived internal state.

14.1.2 Permutation symmetry

The swap satisfies \(S^2=I\), so its eigenvalues are \(\pm1\) and the space splits into a symmetric part (\(S=+1\)) and an antisymmetric part (\(S=-1\)), with projections \((I\pm S)/2\). Every allowed effect commutes with \(S\), so it maps each part into itself. Consequently no measurement can detect a coherent superposition of a symmetric and an antisymmetric state: such a superposition gives exactly the same statistics for all allowed effects as the corresponding mixture. The two sectors are separated by a superselection rule.

For a given kind of particle, the states that occur lie in one sector. For two particles the sectors are just these two, and the particles are called bosons if their states are symmetric and fermions if antisymmetric. For two qubit-like systems the symmetric part has dimension \(3\) and the antisymmetric part dimension \(1\).

Problem 14.1. Show that \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket s+\ket a)\) and \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket s-\ket a)\), with \(\ket s\) symmetric and \(\ket a\) antisymmetric, give the same probability for every effect commuting with \(S\).

Worked solution

The probabilities differ by \(\pm\mathrm{Re}\,\bra sE\ket a\) terms. Since \(E\) commutes with \(S\), \(E\ket a\) is antisymmetric, so \(\bra sE\ket a=0\). Both states give \(\tfrac12(\bra sE\ket s+\bra aE\ket a)\).

14.2 Bosons and Fermions

The symmetry of the joint state has striking consequences for where particles can be found.

14.2.1 Symmetric and antisymmetric states

Two particles in single-particle states \(\ket a\) and \(\ket b\), with \(\braket ab=0\), are described by

\[\ket{a,b}_\pm=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket a\ket b\pm\ket b\ket a\big),\]

with \(+\) for bosons and \(-\) for fermions. For fermions with \(a=b\), the state vanishes: two fermions cannot occupy the same single-particle state. This is the Pauli exclusion principle, which underlies the structure of atoms and the stability of matter. For bosons, the state \(\ket a\ket a\) is allowed, and bosons tend to share states.

The reduced state of one particle in \(\ket{a,b}_\pm\) is \(\tfrac12(\ketbra aa+\ketbra bb)\), a mixture, even though nothing has interacted. Whether this formal entanglement is usable as a resource is a subtle question, because the labels it refers to cannot be accessed. Operationally meaningful entanglement between identical particles is defined between modes, such as two locations, rather than between labels.

14.2.2 Exclusion and bunching

A balanced beam splitter acts on each particle’s path as \(\ket1\mapsto(\ket1+\ket2)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket2\mapsto(\ket1-\ket2)/\sqrt2\). Send one particle in each input. For distinguishable particles the input is \(\ket1\ket2\), and the output has amplitude for each of the four path combinations of magnitude \(1/2\): coincidences, one particle in each output, occur with probability \(1/2\).

For bosons the input is \(\ket{1,2}_+\). The two amplitudes for one particle in each output, one where both are transmitted and one where both are reflected, cancel exactly, and the coincidence probability is \(0\): the photons bunch. For fermions they add, and the particles always leave through different ports. These three answers, \(1/2\), \(0\), and \(1\), are direct tests of indistinguishability and of the sector. Hong, Ou, and Mandel observed the bosonic dip for photons in 1987.

Problem 14.2. Compute the bosonic output of the beam splitter for the input \(\ket{1,2}_+\) and confirm that both particles exit together.

Worked solution

\(\ket1\ket2\mapsto\tfrac12(\ket1+\ket2)(\ket1-\ket2)\) and \(\ket2\ket1\mapsto\tfrac12(\ket1-\ket2)(\ket1+\ket2)\). Adding and dividing by \(\sqrt2\), the \(\ket1\ket2\) and \(\ket2\ket1\) terms cancel, leaving \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1\ket1-\ket2\ket2)\): both particles in port 1 or both in port 2.

14.3 Limits of the Argument

The principle of indistinguishability explains why states of identical particles have definite symmetry. It does not explain everything in the usual treatment, and the course should say what remains assumed.

14.3.1 Parastatistics

For three or more particles, permutations form a larger group, and the space splits into more sectors: the totally symmetric, the totally antisymmetric, and sectors of mixed symmetry in which permutations act by matrices rather than by signs. Indistinguishability alone permits all of them. Particles described by mixed sectors obey parastatistics.

The usual statement that only the totally symmetric and antisymmetric sectors occur is called the symmetrization postulate. Albert Messiah and Oscar Greenberg examined its status in 1964 and concluded that it is an additional assumption. In relativistic quantum field theory in three spatial dimensions, the analysis of Sergio Doplicher, Rudolf Haag, and John Roberts showed that parastatistics can be rewritten as ordinary Bose or Fermi statistics together with a hidden internal degree of freedom. Within the finite-dimensional principles of this course, the postulate is not derived.

14.3.2 Spin and statistics

Which particles are bosons and which are fermions is also not settled by the principles. Experience says particles of integer spin are bosons and those of half-integer spin are fermions. This connection is a theorem of relativistic quantum field theory, due to Markus Fierz and Wolfgang Pauli around 1940, and it relies on Lorentz invariance, locality, and positivity of energy, which go beyond the setting of this course.

In two spatial dimensions, exchanging particles is described by paths that can wind around each other, and the possible statistics include anyons, whose exchange multiplies the state by an arbitrary phase or matrix. This shows that statistics depend on the geometry of space, not on the finite-dimensional state spaces alone, and is one more reason to treat spin and statistics as an input from outside the reconstruction.

Problem 14.3. For two particles, why are there no parastatistics?

Worked solution

The permutation group of two objects has only the identity and the swap, and \(S^2=I\) forces every sector to be an eigenspace of \(S\) with eigenvalue \(\pm1\). Mixed-symmetry sectors need at least three particles, where permutations need not commute.
