2.1 Mixing and Convexity

Chapter 1 defined a state as everything a preparation implies about future measurement statistics. That definition sounds empty, but it already carries structure. The reason is that preparations can be combined by chance, and the framework tells us exactly what a chance combination predicts.

Predict first. Preparation A gives outcome “up” of some measurement with probability \(0.9\); preparation B gives it with probability \(0.3\). A new recipe tosses a fair coin and runs A on heads, B on tails, then forgets the coin. What probability does the new recipe give for “up”? Did you need to know anything about what the system is?

Compare your reasoning

The probability is \(\tfrac12(0.9)+\tfrac12(0.3)=0.6\). This follows from ordinary probability applied to the recipe: one half of the runs come from each preparation. Nothing about the system’s nature was used, which is why the rule holds in every theory.

2.1.1 Random choices of preparations

Given two preparations and a number \(\lambda\) between \(0\) and \(1\), we can build a third: run the first with probability \(\lambda\) and the second otherwise, using some independent random device, and discard the record of which was run. The framework allows this because the random device is itself a laboratory procedure. We take it as part of the framework that such mixing is always possible.

What does the mixture predict? Fix any outcome of any measurement, and let its probabilities be \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) for the two preparations. Among many runs of the mixture, a fraction \(\lambda\) used the first preparation and a fraction \(1-\lambda\) the second. Within each group the outcome occurs with its own probability, so overall it occurs with probability

\[p_{\text{mix}}=\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2 .\]

This holds for every outcome of every measurement at once. It is the law of total probability, applied to a recipe. So if states are described by their outcome probabilities, the state of the mixture is the weighted average of the two states. A set that contains every weighted average of any two of its members is called convex. The state space of every theory in the framework is convex.

2.1.2 Pure and mixed states

A state is mixed if it can be written as a mixture of two different states with \(0<\lambda<1\), and pure otherwise. Geometrically, pure states are the extreme points of the convex set: the corners of a polygon, every point on the boundary of a disc. A pure state is a preparation that cannot be understood as ignorance about which of several other preparations was made.

For a coin, a state is the probability \(p\) of heads, a point of the interval \([0,1]\). The only pure states are \(p=0\) and \(p=1\), and every other state is a mixture of them in exactly one way: \(p\) of heads-for-certain and \(1-p\) of tails-for-certain. For a die the states are probability lists \((p_1,\dots,p_6)\), the pure states are the six certain outcomes, and again each state decomposes into pure states in exactly one way.

Uniqueness is a special feature, not a law of the framework. Picture a theory whose states fill a disc, with pure states on its circle. The center is the even mixture of any two diametrically opposite points, and of infinitely many other pairs. Because a state records only predictions, all these recipes produce the same state, and no measurement can reveal which mixture was used. We shall see that this is exactly the situation in quantum theory, and that it has consequences for what can be learned and copied.

Problem 2.1. A preparation gives probabilities \((0.9,0.1)\) for a two-outcome measurement, another gives \((0.3,0.7)\). What does the mixture with \(\lambda=1/4\) on the first give? Can the state \((0.95,0.05)\) be obtained by mixing these two?

Worked solution

The mixture gives \(0.25(0.9)+0.75(0.3)=0.45\) for the first outcome and \(0.55\) for the second. No mixture of the two gives \(0.95\): weighted averages lie between \(0.3\) and \(0.9\). Mixing only moves inward, which is why pure states cannot be produced from mixtures.

2.2 Fiducial Measurements

A state assigns a probability to every outcome of every possible measurement, a list with infinitely many entries. A useful theory cannot require infinitely many numbers to specify a preparation. The first principle of the course says it does not have to.

2.2.1 Finitely many numbers fix a state

Finiteness (first part). For each type of system there is a finite set of measurement outcomes, called fiducial outcomes, whose probabilities determine the probability of every outcome of every measurement.

This is a principle, not part of the framework. A theory could require ever more numbers to pin down its states, and the infinite-dimensional systems of Chapter 16 do. But for systems with finitely many distinguishable configurations it is a natural demand: if finitely many experiments sufficed to tell any two preparations apart, a finite list of their statistics is a complete description. The procedure that estimates those probabilities from repeated runs and so identifies an unknown state is called tomography.

For a coin, one fiducial outcome suffices: knowing the probability of heads fixes everything. For a polarization measurement on light, measuring at a single angle does not suffice; two preparations can agree on the probability at one angle and disagree at another. Determining a fiducial set is part of what it means to understand a type of system.

2.2.2 The dimension K

It helps to allow preparations that sometimes fail. A source that emits a system only with probability \(s\), with failures recorded as “no system,” produces a state whose outcome probabilities are \(s\) times those of a successful run. Such subnormalized states form a cone: multiplying a state by a number between \(0\) and \(1\) gives another. The total probability of a complete measurement on a subnormalized state is \(s\), the probability that the system is there.

Let \(K\) be the smallest number of fiducial probabilities that fix every subnormalized state. This counts normalization as one of the numbers, because \(s\) itself must be specified. For normalized states one number is fixed and \(K-1\) remain free. A coin has \(K=2\): the probabilities of heads and of tails, which for a normalized coin sum to one. A die has \(K=6\). A classical system with \(N\) configurations has \(K=N\).

The number \(K\) is a property of the theory, not of a chosen measurement. Different fiducial sets are possible, but all minimal ones have the same size, since \(K\) is the dimension of the space spanned by the states. We shall later compare \(K\) with \(N\), the number of states that one measurement can tell apart perfectly; the relation between these numbers separates classical from quantum theory.

Problem 2.2. A system admits two two-outcome measurements, X and Z. Experiments show that the probability of the first outcome of X and of Z can be any two numbers in \([0,1]\), independently, and that these two numbers fix every other prediction. What is \(K\)? What is the shape of the set of normalized states?

Worked solution

A subnormalized state needs the success probability and the two first-outcome probabilities, so \(K=3\). Normalized states correspond to all pairs \((p_X,p_Z)\in[0,1]^2\): the state space is a square. This is the square bit, a foil met again in Chapters 3 and 4.

2.3 Linearity of Probabilities

We now combine the two previous ideas. Mixing says what a chance combination predicts; finiteness says a state is a finite list of numbers. Together they force every outcome probability to be a linear function of that list. No probability rule has been assumed.

2.3.1 Why predictions respect mixtures

Write a state as its list of fiducial probabilities, a vector \(\omega\) in \(\mathbb R^K\). Any other outcome \(e\) has a probability that depends only on the state, so it is some function \(e(\omega)\). The mixing rule of Section 2.1 applies to every outcome, fiducial or not, so

\[e\big(\lambda\omega_1+(1-\lambda)\omega_2\big)=\lambda\,e(\omega_1)+(1-\lambda)\,e(\omega_2).\]

A function on a convex set with this property is called affine. Subnormalized states add one more condition: running a preparation with success probability \(s\) scales all probabilities, so \(e(s\omega)=s\,e(\omega)\). An affine function that also scales this way extends uniquely to a linear function on the whole space spanned by the states. (Define it on any vector \(v=a\omega_1-b\omega_2\) by \(a\,e(\omega_1)-b\,e(\omega_2)\); the two conditions guarantee that different ways of writing \(v\) give the same value.) Every outcome probability is therefore given by a linear functional on \(\mathbb R^K\).

The contrast with a nonlinear rule shows what has been excluded. Suppose some theory claimed that an outcome had probability \(p^2\), where \(p\) is a fiducial probability. Mixing a state with \(p=0\) and one with \(p=1\) equally would then require the outcome probability to be \(\tfrac12(0)+\tfrac12(1)=\tfrac12\) by the recipe, but \((1/2)^2=1/4\) by the rule. No consistent theory in the framework has such a rule.

2.3.2 States as vectors

We now have a precise picture shared by every theory satisfying finiteness. The normalized states form a convex set \(\Omega\) inside a \((K-1)\)-dimensional slice of \(\mathbb R^K\); the subnormalized states form the cone of its multiples. Outcomes act as linear functionals taking values between \(0\) and \(1\) on every state. A theory is specified, at this level, by the shape of \(\Omega\) and by which functionals correspond to real outcomes.

Several shapes are already on the table. The classical bit has a segment, the classical trit a triangle, a classical \(N\)-level system a simplex with \(N\) corners. The square bit of Problem 2.2 has a square. A disc and a three-dimensional ball are also convex sets with smooth boundaries. Each shape defines a different theory with different predictions. The framework permits all of them; the principles in the coming chapters will select one.

Notice that “linear” here is a derived property of probabilities, not of any underlying wave or amplitude. When amplitudes appear in Chapter 7, their probabilities will be quadratic in the amplitudes and still linear in the state, exactly as this section requires.

Problem 2.3. In the square bit, an outcome \(e\) has probability \(e(p_X,p_Z)\) on normalized states. Suppose \(e(0,0)=0\), \(e(1,0)=0.5\), and \(e(0,1)=0.5\). What is \(e(1,1)\), and what is \(e(0.5,0.5)\)?

Worked solution

An affine function on the plane has the form \(a+bp_X+cp_Z\). The data give \(a=0\), \(b=c=0.5\), so \(e(1,1)=1\) and \(e(0.5,0.5)=0.5\). The value at \((1,1)\) is forced by linearity once three non-collinear points are known.
