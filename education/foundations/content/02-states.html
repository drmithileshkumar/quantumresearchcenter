We have a language for every testable theory: preparations, transformations, measurements, and the probabilities of their outcomes. A state is whatever a preparation implies about the statistics of future measurements. That definition says what a state does, but not what it looks like.

Can the framework alone tell us anything about the shape of a theory’s states? It can, because the framework lets us choose preparations at random, and ordinary probability then says exactly what such a choice predicts. This forces the states of every theory to form a convex set. A state still assigns a probability to infinitely many outcomes, so we next ask how many numbers are really needed to fix one. Our first principle answers that only finitely many are. Together, mixing and finiteness force every outcome probability to be a linear function of a finite list of numbers, so that states become vectors.

The next chapter turns from states to the outcomes that measure them.

2.1 Mixing and Convexity

The only freedom the framework gives us beyond running recipes is choosing among them by chance. We first work out what such a chance choice predicts, which shows that states form a convex set. Then we ask which states cannot be produced by chance at all.

Predict first. Preparation A gives outcome “up” of some measurement with probability \(0.9\); preparation B gives it with probability \(0.3\). A new recipe tosses a fair coin and runs A on heads, B on tails, then forgets the coin. What probability does the new recipe give for “up”? Did you need to know anything about what the system is?

Compare your reasoning

The probability is \(\tfrac12(0.9)+\tfrac12(0.3)=0.6\). This follows from ordinary probability applied to the recipe: one half of the runs come from each preparation. Nothing about the system’s nature was used, which is why the rule holds in every theory.

2.1.1 Random choices of preparations

Suppose we have two preparations and a number \(\lambda\) between \(0\) and \(1\). Can we build a third? We can: run the first with probability \(\lambda\) and the second otherwise, using an independent random device, and discard the record of which was run. The random device is itself a laboratory procedure, so the framework allows this. We take it as part of the framework that such mixing is always possible.

What does the mixture predict? Fix any outcome of any measurement, and let its probabilities be \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) for the two preparations. Among many runs of the mixture, a fraction \(\lambda\) used the first preparation and a fraction \(1-\lambda\) used the second. Within each group the outcome occurs with its own probability. So overall it occurs with probability

\[p_{\text{mix}}=\lambda p_1+(1-\lambda)p_2 .\]

This is the law of total probability, applied to a recipe, and it holds for every outcome of every measurement at once. So if we describe states by their outcome probabilities, the state of the mixture is the weighted average of the two states. A set that contains every weighted average of any two of its members is called convex. The states of every theory in the framework form a convex set.

2.1.2 Pure and mixed states

Mixing produces new states from old ones. Are there states that no mixing can produce? Call a state mixed if it is a mixture of two different states with \(0<\lambda<1\), and pure otherwise. A pure state is a preparation that cannot be understood as ignorance about which of several other preparations was made. Geometrically, pure states are the extreme points of the convex set: the corners of a polygon, or every point on the boundary circle of a disc.

Take a coin first. Its state is the probability \(p\) of heads, a point of the interval \([0,1]\). The only pure states are \(p=0\) and \(p=1\). Every other state is a mixture of them in exactly one way: \(p\) of heads-for-certain and \(1-p\) of tails-for-certain. For a die, the states are probability lists \((p_1,\dots,p_6)\), and the pure states are the six certain outcomes. Again each state is a mixture of pure states in exactly one way.

Is that uniqueness forced by the framework? No. Picture a theory whose states fill a disc, with its pure states on the circle. The center is the even mixture of any two opposite points on the circle, and there are infinitely many such pairs. A state records only predictions, so all these recipes produce the same state. No measurement can reveal which mixture was used. We will find that quantum theory is of this kind, and that this costs us something in what can be learned and copied.

Problem 2.1. A preparation gives probabilities \((0.9,0.1)\) for a two-outcome measurement, another gives \((0.3,0.7)\). What does the mixture with \(\lambda=1/4\) on the first give? Can the state \((0.95,0.05)\) be obtained by mixing these two?

Worked solution

The mixture gives \(0.25(0.9)+0.75(0.3)=0.45\) for the first outcome and \(0.55\) for the second. No mixture of the two gives \(0.95\): weighted averages lie between \(0.3\) and \(0.9\). Mixing only moves inward, which is why pure states cannot be produced from mixtures.

2.2 Fiducial Measurements

A state assigns a probability to every outcome of every possible measurement. That is a list with infinitely many entries, and no laboratory can check infinitely many numbers. So we ask whether a finite list can stand in for the whole, which leads to our first principle. Then we count how long that list must be.

2.2.1 Finitely many numbers fix a state

Could a finite laboratory ever confirm which state it has prepared, if a state needed infinitely many numbers? It could not. Every estimate of a probability takes many runs, and a laboratory can run only finitely many experiments. If no finite set of probabilities ever settled the state, two preparations could agree on every test we had made and still differ on the next one. Identifying a state would never be finished.

So suppose instead that some finite set of tests always suffices. Then a state is completely described by finitely many numbers. We raise this to a principle.

Finiteness (first part). For each type of system there is a finite set of measurement outcomes, called fiducial outcomes, whose probabilities determine the probability of every outcome of every measurement.

The argument above makes the principle reasonable but does not prove it. The framework allows theories that need ever more numbers. A particle that can sit anywhere on a line is described in quantum theory by a function, which no finite list fixes, and we return to such systems at the end of the course. Finiteness is a demand that we place on systems with finitely many distinguishable configurations, and that foil shows what it excludes.

Estimating the fiducial probabilities from repeated runs, and so identifying an unknown state, is called tomography. For a coin, one fiducial outcome suffices: the probability of heads fixes everything. For the disc theory, one two-outcome measurement does not suffice. Two states on the circle can give the same probability for that measurement and differ for another.

2.2.2 The dimension K

How many fiducial numbers does a system need? To count them cleanly, we allow preparations that sometimes fail. Suppose a source emits a system only with probability \(s\), and records failures as “no system.” Its outcome probabilities are then \(s\) times those of a successful run. Such subnormalized states form a cone: multiplying a state by a number between \(0\) and \(1\) gives another. The probabilities of all outcomes of a measurement on such a state add up to \(s\), the probability that the system is there.

Let \(K\) be the smallest number of fiducial probabilities that fix every subnormalized state. This counts normalization as one of the numbers, because \(s\) itself must be specified. For normalized states one number is fixed, and \(K-1\) remain free. A coin has \(K=2\): the probabilities of heads and of tails, which for a normalized coin sum to one. A die has \(K=6\). A classical system with \(N\) configurations has \(K=N\).

Could a cleverer choice of fiducial outcomes give a smaller count? We will see that it cannot: once states are vectors, \(K\) is the dimension of the space they span, so every minimal fiducial set has the same size. Later we compare \(K\) with \(N\), the number of states that one measurement can tell apart perfectly. The relation between these two numbers will separate classical theory from quantum theory.

Problem 2.2. A system admits two two-outcome measurements, X and Z. Experiments show that the probability of the first outcome of X and of Z can be any two numbers in \([0,1]\), independently, and that these two numbers fix every other prediction. What is \(K\)? What is the shape of the set of normalized states?

Worked solution

A subnormalized state needs the success probability and the two first-outcome probabilities, so \(K=3\). Normalized states correspond to all pairs \((p_X,p_Z)\in[0,1]^2\): the state space is a square. This is the square bit, a foil we meet again when we study measurements and pairs of systems.

2.3 Linearity of Probabilities

We now have two facts about states: chance combinations average them, and a finite list fixes them. What do the two together say about the probability of an arbitrary outcome? They force it to be a linear function of the list, without any probability rule assumed. That turns each theory into a convex set of vectors, and we then compare the shapes such sets can take.

2.3.1 Why predictions respect mixtures

Write a state as its list of fiducial probabilities, a vector \(\omega\) in \(\mathbb R^K\). Any other outcome \(e\) has a probability that depends only on the state, so it is some function \(e(\omega)\). What kind of function can it be? The mixing rule applies to every outcome, fiducial or not, so

\[e\big(\lambda\omega_1+(1-\lambda)\omega_2\big)=\lambda\,e(\omega_1)+(1-\lambda)\,e(\omega_2).\]

A function on a convex set with this property is called affine. Subnormalized states add one more condition. Running a preparation with success probability \(s\) scales all probabilities, so \(e(s\omega)=s\,e(\omega)\).

Does an affine function with this scaling extend to a linear function on all of \(\mathbb R^K\)? First extend it to every nonnegative multiple \(t\omega\) by \(e(t\omega)=t\,e(\omega)\). Then \(e\) adds on the cone: for normalized \(\omega_1,\omega_2\) and \(a,d\geq0\), the vector \(a\omega_1+d\omega_2\) is \((a+d)\) times a mixture, so mixing and scaling give \(e(a\omega_1+d\omega_2)=a\,e(\omega_1)+d\,e(\omega_2)\). Now define \(e\) on any difference \(v=a\omega_1-b\omega_2\) by \(a\,e(\omega_1)-b\,e(\omega_2)\). Could two ways of writing \(v\) give different values? Suppose \(a\omega_1-b\omega_2=c\omega_3-d\omega_4\). Rearranging gives \(a\omega_1+d\omega_4=c\omega_3+b\omega_2\), an equality between vectors of the cone. Additivity on the cone gives \(a\,e(\omega_1)+d\,e(\omega_4)=c\,e(\omega_3)+b\,e(\omega_2)\), which rearranges to the same value both ways. So every outcome probability is given by a linear function on \(\mathbb R^K\), a linear functional.

What has this excluded? Suppose a theory claimed that an outcome had probability \(p^2\), where \(p\) is a fiducial probability. Mix a state with \(p=0\) and one with \(p=1\) equally. The recipe then gives the outcome probability \(\tfrac12(0)+\tfrac12(1)=\tfrac12\), but the rule gives \((1/2)^2=1/4\). No consistent theory in the framework has such a rule.

2.3.2 States as vectors

What does a theory look like now? Every theory satisfying finiteness has the same outline. The normalized states form a convex set \(\Omega\) in a \((K-1)\)-dimensional slice of \(\mathbb R^K\), and the subnormalized states form the cone of its multiples. Outcomes act as linear functionals that take values between \(0\) and \(1\) on every state. At this level, a theory is specified by the shape of \(\Omega\) and by which functionals are real outcomes.

This also settles the question left open about \(K\). If the states of a minimal fiducial set did not span \(\mathbb R^K\), some linear relation would hold among the fiducial probabilities of every state, and one of them could be dropped. So the states span \(\mathbb R^K\). Any other fiducial set gives probabilities that are linear in \(\omega\) and still distinguish all states. Could such a linear map send some nonzero vector \(v\) to zero? Take a state \(\omega\) in the interior of the cone. Then \(\omega+\varepsilon v\) is also in the cone for small \(\varepsilon\), and the map would not tell it from \(\omega\). So the map is one-to-one on all of \(\mathbb R^K\), and it needs at least \(K\) outputs. Every minimal fiducial set therefore has exactly \(K\) members, the dimension of the space spanned by the states.

Which shapes are possible? Several are already on the table. The classical bit has a segment, the classical trit a triangle, and a classical \(N\)-level system a simplex with \(N\) corners. The square bit has a square. A disc and a three-dimensional ball are also convex sets, with smooth boundaries. Each shape defines a different theory with different predictions. The framework permits all of them, and the principles will select one.

Is this linearity a hint of waves or amplitudes underneath? No. It is a property of probabilities as functions of the state, derived from mixing alone. Any theory in the framework has it, whatever lies underneath.

Problem 2.3. In the square bit, an outcome \(e\) has probability \(e(p_X,p_Z)\) on normalized states. Suppose \(e(0,0)=0\), \(e(1,0)=0.5\), and \(e(0,1)=0.5\). What is \(e(1,1)\), and what is \(e(0.5,0.5)\)?

Worked solution

An affine function on the plane has the form \(a+bp_X+cp_Z\). The data give \(a=0\), \(b=c=0.5\), so \(e(1,1)=1\) and \(e(0.5,0.5)=0.5\). The value at \((1,1)\) is forced by linearity once three non-collinear points are known.
