After this chapter, you should be able to:

  • Specify a variational model by its four defining choices, and count the parameters it exposes to a learner.
  • Turn a Hamiltonian or a list of observables into a measurement plan, and price that plan in shots.
  • Explain why the cost landscape is a property of the circuit, and what a global cost does to trainability.

Study method: Keep a calculator open. Almost every quantity in this chapter is a count—of parameters, of measurement settings, of shots—and the counts are what decide whether a proposal is runnable. Read Section 4.2 twice: measurement is where most variational proposals quietly become too expensive.

Notation. \(n\) is the number of qubits, \(L\) the number of ansatz layers, \(p\) the number of trainable angles, \(\mathbf\theta\in\mathbb R^p\) the parameter vector, \(U(\mathbf\theta)\) the circuit unitary, and \(\mathbf\phi(x)\) the encoding of Section 2. \(P_j\in\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}\) are Pauli strings with coefficients \(c_j\), and \(S\) is a shot count.

Chapter 3 treated a quantum model as a kernel: fix a feature map, measure overlaps, and hand the resulting matrix to a classical solver. That view is honest but narrow, because most proposed quantum models are variational—a circuit with adjustable angles whose output is measured, trained by a classical optimiser against a loss. This chapter builds that object carefully, from the ansatz down to the last shot. Chapter 5 supplies the optimiser, and Chapter 6 explains the failure mode that decides most of these models' fate.

4.1 Building a variational ansatz

An ansatz is a circuit family with free parameters. The name suggests a guess, and that is accurate: every structural decision—which gates, which connectivity, how deep—is a prior about the solution, made before any data is seen.

4.1.1 The four choices that define a model

A variational model is fixed by four decisions, and confusing them is a common source of muddled comparisons.

The first is the data-encoding map \(x\mapsto\rho(x)\) or \(x\mapsto|\psi(x)\rangle\), which Chapter 2 analysed. The second is the ansatz \(U(\mathbf\theta)\), the parameterised circuit applied after—or interleaved with—the encoding. The third is the readout: which observables are measured and how their expectation values are combined into a scalar. The fourth is the training rule: the loss, the optimiser, and the stopping condition. Only the second is usually called "the ansatz", yet all four determine what the model can represent, and the readout alone can change a trainable model into an untrainable one (Section 4.3.2).

The output of the model is an expectation value,

\[\hat y(x;\mathbf\theta)=\langle 0|U^{\dagger}(\mathbf\theta)\,U^{\dagger}_{\phi}(x)\,O\,U_{\phi}(x)\,U(\mathbf\theta)|0\rangle ,\]

where \(O\) is a Hermitian observable. Since \(\mathbf\theta\) enters through unitary gates, \(\hat y\) is a smooth, bounded, periodic function of each angle: a trigonometric polynomial. That single fact explains both the smoothness that optimisers exploit and the redundancies that make parameter space larger than it needs to be.

Worked example

A hardware-efficient layer on \(n=10\) qubits applies \(R_y\) and \(R_z\) to every qubit, then a ladder of \(n-1\) nearest-neighbour CNOTs. Count the parameters and two-qubit gates for \(L=3\) layers.

Solution and reasoning

Each layer carries \(2n=20\) angles and \(n-1=9\) CNOTs, so three layers give \(p=60\) parameters and 27 CNOTs. Compare that with the \(2^{11}-2=2046\) real parameters of a general 10-qubit pure state: the ansatz explores a 60-dimensional slice of a 2046-dimensional manifold. That ratio is the useful statement of expressivity, and it is why "the state space is exponentially large" says nothing about the model. It also sets the scale for generalisation questions in Chapter 7: 60 parameters against a few hundred samples is a very different statistical situation from 2046.

4.1.2 Hardware-efficient and problem-inspired circuits

Two families dominate practice, and they sit at opposite ends of a trade-off between what the hardware likes and what the problem knows.

Hardware-efficient ansätze use gates that the device executes natively—single-qubit rotations and the entanglers available on its connectivity graph—arranged in repeating layers. They are shallow, calibration-friendly, and problem-agnostic: nothing about the circuit knows whether the task is chemistry, classification, or optimisation. Their weakness is that being agnostic they must be expressive, and expressiveness is exactly what Section 4.1.3 and Chapter 6 identify as dangerous.

Problem-inspired ansätze borrow structure. The Hamiltonian variational ansatz and the unitary coupled-cluster family build layers from the terms of the problem Hamiltonian, so the circuit conserves particle number and symmetry by construction; QAOA alternates a problem phase separator with a mixer. These circuits are far less expressive, and that is the point: if the target state lies in the family, restricting the search is a gain, not a loss. The risk moves elsewhere—an over-restricted ansatz can miss the ground state entirely, and the residual error looks like an optimisation failure when it is a modelling one.

Worked example

A molecular Hamiltonian ansatz uses one layer per Hamiltonian term, each term being a Trotterised excitation with depth 6. The Hamiltonian has 84 terms. Estimate the circuit depth, and say what happens to accuracy if the depth budget is 200.

Solution and reasoning

One layer costs about 6 gates deep and there are 84 terms, so a single sweep needs roughly 500 operations—already more than twice the stated budget. With a budget of 200 the experimenter must either truncate the term list (losing the correlation energy those terms carry) or use a shallower Trotter step. Both are legitimate, but each must be reported, because a variational energy computed from a truncated ansatz is an upper bound for a different Hamiltonian than the one in the abstract. This is the practical content of "problem-inspired": you inherit the physics and its cost at the same time.

4.1.3 Depth, entanglement, and what a shallow circuit cannot do

Depth is not a free resource, and it has a precise consequence for entanglement that is worth stating as a bound rather than a worry.

Cut a circuit's qubits into two groups. Every entangling gate straddling the cut is a two-qubit gate, and a two-qubit gate can at most double the Schmidt rank across that cut. A circuit of depth \(L\)—that is, \(L\) layers, each acting on all qubits—therefore produces a state whose Schmidt rank across any contiguous cut is at most \(2^{L}\). For \(n=10\) qubits cut into \(5+5\), a maximally entangled state needs rank \(2^{5}=32\), so \(L=3\) layers can supply at most rank 8. If the target state requires more, the ansatz has a floor in its loss that no amount of training removes.

This is a statement about what shallow circuits cannot do, and it is easy to over-read. Most learning targets are not maximally entangled; a classical model's target function is usually low-complexity, and the corresponding quantum state may be reachable at modest depth. The useful discipline is to ask, for a given target, which side of the bound you are on, and to test it by measuring the rank or the entanglement entropy of the trained state.

Expressibility is measured rather than assumed. The Meyer–Wallach measure

\[Q(|\psi\rangle)=1-\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{tr}\bigl(\rho_k^2\bigr)\]

is zero for product states and grows as the state's local entanglement grows, and the frame potential or a divergence from the Haar measure compares an ansatz's output distribution with the uniform one. The empirical regularity, documented across many ansatz families, is that expressibility and trainability pull in opposite directions: the circuits expressive enough to contain the target are often the ones whose gradients vanish. Chapter 6 quantifies that.

Worked example

Compute the Meyer–Wallach measure for \(|000\rangle\) and for the three-qubit GHZ state \((|000\rangle+|111\rangle)/\sqrt2\).

Solution and reasoning

For \(|000\rangle\) every single-qubit reduced state is pure, so \(\operatorname{tr}(\rho_k^2)=1\) and \(Q=0\). For GHZ every single-qubit reduced state is maximally mixed, \(\operatorname{tr}(\rho_k^2)=1/2\), so \(Q=1-\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac32=1/2\). A non-zero \(Q\) therefore certifies genuine multipartite entanglement—but \(Q\) cannot distinguish a state that is useful for a task from one that is merely entangled, and it takes no account of the circuit cost of preparing the state. It is a diagnostic of the ansatz, not an objective.

4.2 From expectation value to loss

A circuit on its own produces nothing usable. Between the unitary and the optimiser sit three steps with their own costs: decomposing the observable, choosing measurement settings, and paying for statistical precision.

4.2.1 Pauli decomposition and measurement settings

Almost every observable of interest is a sum of Pauli strings,

\[O=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_j P_j ,\qquad \langle O\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_j\langle P_j\rangle .\]

Each \(P_j\) has to be estimated, and a single circuit run yields one bit-string, so it estimates many \(P_j\) at once only if they can be measured in the same basis. Two Pauli strings are qubit-wise commuting when, on every qubit, they either share the same Pauli or one of them is the identity; such a set can be read out from a single setting by rotating each qubit into the right basis. Strings that commute but are not qubit-wise commuting can also be grouped, at the price of extra basis-changing gates inside the circuit. The grouping problem is a graph-colouring problem, and it is worth solving before writing any code, because the number of settings multiplies every subsequent cost.

Worked example

An operator on four qubits has the eight terms grouped in Figure 4-2 with coefficients \(0.5,0.5,-0.2,-0.2,0.3,0.1,0.15,0.15\). Estimate how many settings are needed and how they are measured.

Solution and reasoning

Three settings suffice. The six \(Z\)-type strings are measured together after no basis change. \(XXII\) requires a Hadamard on qubits 1 and 2 before readout and \(YYII\) requires the \(Y\)-basis rotation \(S^{\dagger}H\) on both. Note that a commutation-based grouping would do no better here: \(XXII\) and \(YYII\) anticommute on qubits 1 and 2. In larger Hamiltonians the gain from grouping is substantial—typically an order of magnitude in the number of settings—which is why measurement planning is a first-class part of the compilation problem rather than an afterthought.

4.2.2 Shot noise and the precision target

Every expectation value is an estimate. For a Pauli string \(P\) measured in \(S\) shots, the estimator \(\hat{\langle P\rangle}\) has variance

\[\operatorname{Var}\bigl(\hat{\langle P\rangle}\bigr)=\frac{1-\langle P\rangle^2}{S}\le\frac1S ,\]

so its standard deviation is at worst \(1/\sqrt S\). To resolve a quantity to \(\pm\epsilon\) you need \(S\approx1/\epsilon^2\) shots: two decimal digits cost ten thousand shots, three digits a million. Because the terms combine, the error on the total is

\[\sigma_O=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{c_j^2\,(1-\langle P_j\rangle^2)}{S_j}}\ ,\]

which tells you where to spend: terms with large coefficients deserve most of the shots, and this allocation problem has a closed-form solution when the variances are known or can be estimated from a pilot run.

The consequence for training is severe and easy to forget. A gradient or a finite difference is a difference of expectation values, so its signal is the small quantity while the noise is the same large quantity. If the loss is measured to \(\pm0.01\) and the gradient signal is \(0.005\), the optimiser is following noise. Precision requirements therefore have to be set per training run, and they grow with the number of parameters and iterations.

Worked example

A Hamiltonian estimator is built from the eight terms above with \(S_j=2000\) shots each, and all expectation values are taken to be zero for a worst-case bound. Estimate the standard deviation of the energy.

Solution and reasoning

The coefficients squared sum to \(0.25+0.25+0.04+0.04+0.09+0.01+0.0225+0.0225=0.7175\). Dividing by 2000 gives \(3.59\times10^{-4}\), so \(\sigma_O\approx0.019\). A chemical accuracy target of \(1.6\times10^{-3}\) hartree then needs roughly \((0.019/0.0016)^2\approx140\) times more shots for the same allocation, and much of that has to be repeated at every optimisation step. This is why variational chemistry proposals quote shot budgets explicitly: an energy curve with \(10^{-3}\) error bars is a different claim from one with \(2\times10^{-2}\) error bars.

4.2.3 Readout error and calibration

Measurement is not ideal. A qubit read out as 1 was sometimes 0, and the effect is systematic rather than random. For a single qubit with symmetric flip probability \(\varepsilon\), the observed and true distributions are related by an assignment matrix,

\[\begin{pmatrix}p^{\text{obs}}_{0}\\ p^{\text{obs}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1-\varepsilon&\varepsilon\\ \varepsilon&1-\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}p^{\text{true}}_{0}\\ p^{\text{true}}_{1}\end{pmatrix},\]

which can be estimated by preparing every computational basis state and inverting. Two cautions follow. The inversion amplifies noise by \(1/(1-2\varepsilon)^2\), so 3% readout error costs about 13% more shots—small—but 15% error costs more than twice as many, and the matrix becomes ill-conditioned. For \(n\) qubits the full matrix has \(4^{n}\) entries, which is why practitioners use a tensored or correlated model fitted to a limited set of calibration circuits, accepting residual bias for tractability.

The same logic governs gate error, but there the mitigation is different: errors accumulate with depth, so a deeper ansatz needs either error mitigation, which trades shots for accuracy, or error correction, which trades qubits. Both change the accounting, and neither is free.

Worked example

A qubit has symmetric readout error \(\varepsilon=0.03\) and the measured excited-state population is \(0.55\). Correct it, and state the cost on the estimator's variance.

Solution and reasoning

Inverting gives \(p^{\text{true}}=(0.55-0.03)/0.94=0.5521\), so the raw number was biased low by about 0.002. The variance inflation is \(1/(1-2\varepsilon)^2=1/0.8836\approx1.13\), so 13% more shots are needed for the same precision. Both effects are small here, and the example is included precisely to make the point that readout mitigation is a correction with a measurable price, not a free improvement—and to give you the arithmetic to check a claimed improvement rather than accepting it.

4.3 The optimisation problem in parameter space

Training means minimising a loss over the \(p\) angles. Everything depends on the shape of that function, and the shape is set by choices made in Section 4.1—before any data arrived.

4.3.1 The landscape is a function of the circuit

Write the empirical loss as \(L(\mathbf\theta)=\frac1N\sum_i\ell\bigl(\hat y(x_i;\mathbf\theta),y_i\bigr)\). It is smooth, because each \(\hat y\) is a trigonometric polynomial in the angles. It is bounded. It is not convex in general, and cannot be made so without giving up the model.

Two structural features are worth naming. First, parameter redundancy: because the circuit is periodic in each angle, distinct parameter vectors produce identical states, so the landscape has flat directions and the Hessian is singular. Second, the map from parameters to loss can be highly curved in some directions and almost flat in others—an ill-conditioned landscape that a first-order optimiser will crawl along. Both are properties of the ansatz, and both are visible before training by examining the spectrum of the classical Fisher information or the Hessian at a few random points.

The practical point is that "the optimiser failed" is a diagnosis of last resort. It is usually a statement about the landscape, and the landscape is a design decision.

4.3.2 Global versus local cost functions

How the measured terms are combined into one number changes trainability, not just scale. A global cost takes the whole observable at once, for instance \(L=1-\langle H\rangle/\|H\|_{\infty}\), where \(H\) may have exponentially many terms. A local cost averages individual low-weight terms, \(L=1-\frac1m\sum_j\langle P_j\rangle\) with \(m\) terms of bounded weight.

The difference is measurable. For global costs, the variance of the gradient at random initialisation decays exponentially in the number of qubits even at shallow depth; for local costs of bounded weight the decay is polynomial at shallow depth, becoming exponential only as depth grows. This is the cost-function-dependent barren-plateau result of Cerezo and colleagues (2021), and it is one of the few pieces of variational-circuit theory with direct design consequences: define the loss over local terms and combine the results classically.

Worked example

A gradient component decays like \(2^{-n}\) while the shot noise on its estimate stays fixed. How many more shots are needed at \(n=10\) to keep the same relative precision as at \(n=1\)?

Solution and reasoning

Relative precision requires \(S\propto 1/g^2\) for a signal \(g\), so a signal smaller by \(2^{-n}\) costs \(4^{n}\) times more shots. At \(n=10\) that is \(4^{10}\approx1.05\times10^{6}\)—a million times the shots for the same relative accuracy. Note the trap in the word "relative": if the noise decayed as fast as the signal, the shot count would not change at all, and a plot of signal-to-noise would look flat while both quantities vanished. Always ask which of the two is decaying.

4.3.3 Regularisation, early stopping, and the prior you did not choose

A variational model with \(p\) angles fitted to \(N\) samples will interpolate whenever \(p\gtrsim N\), and interpolation is not generalisation. Three regularisers are available, and only one of them is usually discussed.

Early stopping is the cheapest: monitor a validation set and stop when its loss turns upward. Since the parameters move slowly near flat regions, the number of iterations acts as an implicit constraint on the distance travelled from initialisation, and that constraint is a real prior. Explicit penalties—adding \(\lambda\|\mathbf\theta\|^2\) to the loss, or constraining the parameter range—make the prior visible and tunable. The third and least discussed is the ansatz itself: choosing a circuit that conserves particle number, or that only entangles nearest neighbours, has already excluded most functions, and that exclusion is worth more than any amount of tuning in the small-sample regime.

The consequence for reporting is blunt. Two papers comparing quantum and classical models with different inductive biases are comparing the biases, not the hardware. State the ansatz, the parameter count, the regularisation, and the stopping rule, or the comparison cannot be interpreted. Chapter 7 develops the statistical side of this, and Chapter 8 turns it into a checklist for advantage claims.
