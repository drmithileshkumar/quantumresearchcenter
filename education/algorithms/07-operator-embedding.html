Operator Embedding

How can a unitary circuit represent a transformation that is not unitary?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 2 and 5; operator norms and normalization are developed through the embedding.

Anchor example: Embedding a scaled two-by-two matrix using an auxiliary qubit.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 7.1 Unitary Embeddings
  2. 7.2 Linear Combinations
  3. 7.3 Composing Encodings

7.1 Unitary Embeddings

We would like a circuit to multiply a vector by the matrix diag(1, 1/2). That transformation shrinks some vectors more than others, so it cannot be a deterministic unitary on the same register. An auxiliary system gives a precise way to make it a conditional part of a legal evolution.

7.1.1 Ancilla blocks and normalization

Let A=diag(1,1/2), B=diag(0,√3/2), and define, in ancilla block order 0 then 1,

\[U=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B&-A\end{pmatrix}.\]

Because A and B commute and \(A^2+B^2=I\), multiplication gives \(U^2=I\). The top-left block is A, so \(U\ket0\ket\psi=\ket0 A\ket\psi+\ket1 B\ket\psi\). More generally a block encoding has \((\bra{0^a}\otimes I)U(\ket{0^a}\otimes I)=A/\alpha\). The scale α must be at least the operator norm of A, defined as the largest length of \(A\ket\psi\) over unit input vectors. A sub-block of a unitary cannot increase that length.

7.1.2 Success probabilities and postselection

For \(\ket\psi=\ket+\), the ancilla-zero branch is \((\ket0+\ket1/2)/\sqrt2\), with squared length 5/8. Conditional on observing zero, the normalized output is \((2\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt5\). The other branch carries the missing probability 3/8. Postselection means keeping only the selected outcome, not pretending the others never occurred. Expected repetitions without amplification are 8/5 here; for a general input they are \(\alpha^2/\|A\psi\|^2\), which may be very large or infinite.

Problem 7.1. What happens for system input one in the diagonal example?

Worked solution

Selection probability is 1/4. Conditional output is still one; its amplitude was shrunk before renormalization. The input-dependent success distinguishes this from a deterministic linear operation.

7.2 Linear Combinations

7.2.1 Preparation and coherent selection

Suppose \(A=\sum_j a_jU_j\), with nonnegative real coefficients and unitary terms. Put \(\alpha=\sum_j a_j\) and prepare \(P\ket0=\sum_j\sqrt{a_j/\alpha}\ket j\). Define \(\mathrm{SELECT}=\sum_j\ket j\bra j\otimes U_j\). The circuit applies P, SELECT, then P† on the auxiliary register. Its zero block is A/α because projecting the auxiliary state multiplies each square-root coefficient by itself. Negative or complex coefficient phases can be absorbed into the selected unitaries, but controlled phase conventions then matter.

7.2.2 Constructing weighted sums

For \(A=(I+X)/2\), prepare an auxiliary plus state, use it to control X on the system, then apply H to the auxiliary. On system input zero, ancilla-zero postselection yields plus with probability 1/2. This coherent sum differs from randomly choosing I or X: the random procedure yields a mixture of zero and one, not plus. The linear-combination construction includes both preparation circuits, SELECT, and selection success in its cost.

Problem 7.2. Can an encoding of \(2I\) have α=1?

Worked solution

No. Its desired block would have norm two, but every block obtained by preparing and projecting normalized ancillas has norm at most one. α=2 permits the identity block.

7.3 Composing Encodings

7.3.1 Products and sums of encoded matrices

To encode AB, use independent zeroed auxiliary registers for the encodings of A/α and B/β. Apply the B encoding first and then the A encoding. Projecting both auxiliaries onto zero leaves AB/(αβ). Reusing the same auxiliary without a proof can let failure amplitudes return to the selected block and change the answer. Sums can be built by one more selection register; their scales depend on the combination coefficients.

7.3.2 Access costs and approximation errors

If the two encoded blocks differ from A/α and B/β by at most ε and δ in operator norm, their product error is bounded by ε+δ+εδ when the intended blocks have norm at most one. This follows by expanding \((A/\alpha+E)(B/\beta+F)\) and bounding the three extra terms. Large normalization factors and small selection probability can destroy a claimed advantage. A matrix specified by arbitrary classical entries still needs a justified loading or oracle construction; the word “encoding” does not provide that access.

Problem 7.3. Why does classical random selection fail to implement \((I+X)/2\) coherently?

Worked solution

Random selection adds density operators, not amplitudes. On zero it produces \(I/2\); coherent selected addition produces \(\ket+\), whose X measurement is deterministic.

Once a matrix is encoded in a unitary, repeated use and carefully chosen phases can transform its spectral values. The next chapter constructs the simplest such responses.

Sources and further study: Gilyén et al., Quantum singular value transformation.
