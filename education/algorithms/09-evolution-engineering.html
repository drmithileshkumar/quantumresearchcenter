Evolution Engineering

How can simple available evolutions reproduce a desired dynamics?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 4, 7, and 8; Hamiltonians and the needed series expansions are developed within the examples.

Anchor example: Alternating evolutions generated by X and Z, then tracking their error.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 9.1 Product Formulas
  2. 9.2 Simulation Strategies
  3. 9.3 Adiabatic Evolution

9.1 Product Formulas

Available gates can implement evolution generated by X and by Z separately. Suppose the desired generator is X+Z. Alternating the two seems natural, but the order matters. We must calculate the error before calling the sequence a simulation.

9.1.1 Commuting and noncommuting terms

For a Hermitian H, \(e^{-itH}\) is unitary: in an eigenvector direction it multiplies by a unit-magnitude phase. For \(H=X+Z\), anticommutation gives \(H^2=2I\), hence

\[e^{-it(X+Z)}=\cos(\sqrt2t)I-i\frac{\sin(\sqrt2t)}{\sqrt2}(X+Z).\]

If A and B commute, \(e^{-it(A+B)}=e^{-itA}e^{-itB}\). X and Z do not commute, so this factorization fails. Expand both exponentials to second order in a small δ: the product differs from the exact exponential by \(-\delta^2[A,B]/2+O(\delta^3)\). The bracket means AB−BA.

9.1.2 Error bounds and step selection

The first-order product \((e^{-itA/r}e^{-itB/r})^r\) has error bounded by \(t^2\|[A,B]\|/(2r)\) for bounded Hermitian A,B. One obtains the local bound by integrating the commutator and sums it over r steps; unitary prefixes and suffixes preserve operator norm. For X and Z, the commutator norm is two, giving \(t^2/r\). This is a sufficient worst-case bound, not an equality. The symmetric step \(e^{-i\delta A/2}e^{-i\delta B}e^{-i\delta A/2}\) cancels the second-order term and has global error of order \(t^3/r^2\) for fixed bounded generators. Rotation-synthesis error adds to this approximation.

Problem 9.1. For t=1, how many first-order steps does the stated X+Z bound require for error at most 0.01?

Worked solution

r≥100 is sufficient. Each step contains two elementary Pauli rotations before any compilation overhead. Actual error can be below this upper bound.

9.2 Simulation Strategies

9.2.1 Product formulas and spectral methods

Product formulas are attractive when each term’s exponential is easy. Pauli-string exponentials can be implemented by basis changes, parity computation, a Z rotation, and inverse parity computation. Block-encoding methods may improve query dependence on precision when suitable access is available, but their encodings can be expensive. Compare total gates and qubits at the same target error, not merely the degree of a polynomial against the number of product steps.

9.2.2 Access, time, and precision

The norm of an operator is its largest stretch of a unit vector; for Hermitian H it is the largest absolute eigenvalue. An operator-norm error ε bounds the vector error for every normalized input. Physical simulation also involves discretizing the original model, choosing units and parameters, and estimating observables. Those errors are separate from circuit approximation. Scaling H by a large constant while shortening t preserves Ht and does not remove the cost of the required coupling or encoded normalization.

Problem 9.2. If A=B=X, what product-formula error remains?

Worked solution

None in exact arithmetic: the two terms commute, so the product equals the desired exponential for any number of steps. Gate approximation or physical noise may still contribute errors.

9.3 Adiabatic Evolution

9.3.1 Following an evolving eigenspace

Another strategy changes H itself slowly. Consider \(H(s)=-(1-s)X-sZ\), with s from zero to one. Its lowest-energy state begins at plus and ends at zero. Its two energies are \(\pm\sqrt{(1-s)^2+s^2}\), so the gap is \(2\sqrt{(1-s)^2+s^2}\), minimized at s=1/2 with value √2. A slow schedule suppresses transitions to the other eigenstate. A gap is the energy separation, not the probability of an error.

9.3.2 Ground-state preparation and gap dependence

Adiabatic theorems give conditions on derivatives, boundary behavior, runtime, and minimum gaps that guarantee tracking of an eigenspace. We do not infer a universal runtime from the one-qubit example. In optimization, the final ground state can encode the desired solution, but the gap along the path may become extremely small. Preparing the initial state, implementing the path, controlling errors, and extracting the solution all count. Slow evolution is a design principle, not a general polynomial-time solver for difficult optimization problems.

Problem 9.3. Why does finding a final Hamiltonian with the correct ground state not complete an adiabatic algorithm?

Worked solution

It does not give an efficient path to that state. A small intervening gap, expensive Hamiltonian implementation, or costly readout can make the proposed algorithm ineffective.

When a guaranteed preparation is too expensive, one can restrict the state family and optimize its parameters. The next chapter distinguishes what that strategy proves from what it merely tries.

Sources and further study: Farhi et al., Quantum Computation by Adiabatic Evolution; Low and Chuang, Hamiltonian Simulation by Qubitization.
