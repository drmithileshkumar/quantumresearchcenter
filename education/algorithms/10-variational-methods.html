Variational Methods

What can be learned by repeatedly adjusting and measuring a small quantum circuit?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Measurement, circuit costs, and Hamiltonians from earlier chapters; optimization introduced here.

Anchor example: A parameterized two-qubit circuit minimizing a specified energy.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

10.1 Parameterized Circuits

Choose a two-qubit circuit with one adjustable angle. Can repeated measurement guide that angle toward a low-energy state? This small case lets us solve the objective exactly before relying on a classical optimizer.

10.1.1 Expressiveness and circuit structure

Apply \(R_y(\theta)=e^{-i\theta Y/2}\) to the first qubit of 00, then CNOT from the first to the second. The state is \(\ket{\psi(\theta)}=\cos(\theta/2)\ket{00}+\sin(\theta/2)\ket{11}\). This one-parameter family includes a Bell state but not every two-qubit state. Adding gates increases representational freedom and execution cost; a circuit family is an assumption about where useful answers may lie.

10.1.2 Objectives and measurement budgets

For \(H=-Z\otimes Z-X\otimes X\), the first expectation is one and the second is sin θ, so \(E(\theta)=-1-\sin\theta\). The minimum is −2 at θ=π/2. The two observables require different local measurement bases: computational basis for ZZ and Hadamards before measurement for XX. Each product outcome is ±1; averaging R independent outcomes has variance at most 1/R. For a sum \(\sum_j h_jP_j\), separately sampled estimates have total variance \(\sum_j h_j^2\operatorname{Var}(P_j)/R_j\). Measurement allocation is part of the algorithm.

Problem 10.1. Predict E at θ=0, π/2, and π.

Worked solution

The values are −1, −2, and −1. The middle circuit produces the Bell state with both ZZ and XX expectation one.

10.2 Optimization

10.2.1 Parameter derivatives

For a parameter entering once through \(e^{-i\theta P/2}\) with \(P^2=I\), the exact derivative satisfies

\[\frac{dE}{d\theta}=\frac{E(\theta+\pi/2)-E(\theta-\pi/2)}2.\]

Our objective gives −cos θ by this rule, agreeing with direct differentiation. A classical update \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta E^{\prime}(\theta)\) takes a small step downhill when the learning rate η is suitable. If the same parameter controls several gates, contributions must be handled correctly; the single-gate shift formula is not automatically valid for the whole circuit.

10.2.2 Noise, sampling, and trainability

Sampled gradients fluctuate. Stop when improvement is not distinguishable from uncertainty, rather than requesting meaningless extra decimal places. Hardware noise can bias the objective, so more shots do not necessarily recover the ideal answer. Some circuit families have gradients that become extremely small as size grows; this is a trainability issue, distinct from whether the family contains a good state. Multiple initializations and classical baselines are useful evidence but do not prove global convergence.

Problem 10.2. If the estimate of each of two independent Pauli means uses R shots, bound the variance of their sum.

Worked solution

It is at most \(2/R\). Four times as many shots halves this upper bound on standard deviation, not the variance itself.

10.3 Variational Algorithms

10.3.1 Variational eigensolvers

The variational principle follows by expanding \(\ket\psi=\sum_j c_j\ket{E_j}\): its energy is \(\sum_j|c_j|^2E_j\), an average no lower than the smallest eigenvalue. A variational eigensolver combines a trial circuit, an energy estimator, and a classical search over parameters. In our example the trial family contains the exact ground state, so the bound is saturated. In a general family the best achievable energy may be higher, and the optimizer may not reach even that family minimum.

10.3.2 QAOA and evidence for performance

QAOA alternates phases from a diagonal cost C with mixing generated by \(B=\sum_jX_j\): \(e^{-i\beta_pB}e^{-i\gamma_pC}\cdots e^{-i\beta_1B}e^{-i\gamma_1C}\ket+^{\otimes n}\). Cost phases alone cannot change computational-basis probabilities; the mixer turns phase differences into interference. For one edge in MaxCut, \(C=(I-ZZ)/2\) rewards unequal bits. Optimizing the expected cost and drawing a high-quality bit string are related but different tasks. Report distributions, sampling costs, optimization work, and comparisons at matched instance sizes. No general speedup follows merely from choosing QAOA.

Problem 10.3. Why can C alone not improve the MaxCut sampling distribution from the uniform state?

Worked solution

A diagonal cost evolution changes only phases of basis amplitudes. Their squared magnitudes stay uniform. A noncommuting mixer is needed to turn the phase information into different probabilities.

Classical optimization already uses measurement to decide later actions. The final chapter moves that feedback inside the computation and tracks its branches explicitly.

Sources and further study: Farhi et al., A Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm.
