A measurement need not be the last instruction. Its outcome is a classical bit that can choose the next gate, the next measurement basis, or whether to start again. This section sets up the bookkeeping for computations that branch on mid-circuit outcomes, shows when such branching can be replaced by coherent control and when it cannot, and introduces the simplest uses of measurement-dependent corrections.

A mid-circuit measurement on part of a register splits the computation into branches, one per outcome. For a computational-basis measurement of one qubit, the branch operators are the projectors \(M_0=\ket0\bra0\otimes I\) and \(M_1=\ket1\bra1\otimes I\). Outcome \(b\) occurs with probability

\[p_b=\|M_b\ket\psi\|^2,\]

and leaves the normalized state \(M_b\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_b}\). The outcome is stored as a classical record, a bit that later operations can read. Using it to choose a later gate \(V_b\) is feedforward: the branch with outcome \(b\) continues as \(V_bM_b\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_b}\).

Two features distinguish this from coherent computation. First, branches with different records never interfere again: the record is a which-path record held in a classical register, and classical registers do not go into superposition. Probabilities of branches add; their amplitudes do not. Second, if we ignore the record, the state is described by the mixture of branches, written as a density operator \(\sum_bV_bM_b\ket\psi\bra\psi M_b^\dagger V_b^\dagger\), a description that weights each branch by its probability.

Example: prepare \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})\), measure the first qubit, and if the outcome is \(1\) apply \(X\) to the second. Both branches end with the second qubit in \(\ket0\). Feedforward has turned a random outcome into a deterministic state, which is the essential trick of teleportation and of everything in Section 11.3.

  1. For \(\ket\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\ket{00}+\sqrt{\frac23}\ket{11}\), give \(p_0\), \(p_1\), and the post-measurement states when the first qubit is measured.

    \(p_0=\frac13\) leaving \(\ket{00}\), and \(p_1=\frac23\) leaving \(\ket{11}\).

  2. Measure the first qubit of \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})\ket0\), then apply \(H\) to the first qubit. Compare with applying \(H\) without measuring.

    Without measuring, \(H\ket+=\ket0\): outcome \(0\) with certainty. Measuring first gives \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\) at random; \(H\) then gives \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\), each a fair coin in the computational basis. The measurement destroyed the interference that \(H\) would have used.

  3. Why can a zero-probability outcome be ignored when writing the conditional states?

    If \(p_b=0\), the branch never occurs, and \(M_b\ket\psi=0\) has no normalized version. Any feedforward attached to it is irrelevant. Only branches with nonzero probability need a correction or a decision.

A measurement followed by a classically controlled gate can be replaced by a quantum-controlled gate with the measurement moved to the end. If a qubit is measured in the computational basis and the outcome controls \(V\) on other qubits, then using that qubit as the control of a controlled-\(V\), and measuring it afterwards, gives the same joint distribution of all final outcomes. This is the deferred measurement principle.

Why does it hold? The controlled gate acts as \(\ket0\bra0\otimes I+\ket1\bra1\otimes V\). It never changes the control qubit’s computational-basis value, so the final measurement of that qubit returns the same statistics it would have earlier, and on each value the target receives exactly the same operation as under feedforward. Since nothing afterwards acts on the control qubit, the two procedures cannot be distinguished by their outputs.

The principle is a statement about output statistics, not about resources. The deferred version keeps the measured qubit alive until the end, so it cannot be reset and reused; it needs a controlled gate, which may be more expensive than a classically controlled one; and it must remain coherent longer. The measured version can recycle the qubit immediately but requires fast classical feedback during the computation. Which is cheaper depends on the hardware. Section 11.2 uses the principle in reverse: it starts from a coherent circuit and replaces quantum controls by measurements and classical control, saving qubits.

  1. Apply the deferred version of the Bell example: CNOT from the first qubit to the second instead of measure-then-\(X\). What is the final state before measuring the first qubit?

    \[\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CNOT}}\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}+\ket{10})=\ket+\ket0\]. Measuring the first qubit gives a fair coin, and the second qubit is \(\ket0\) in both cases, matching feedforward.

  2. Does deferred measurement apply if, after the measurement, a gate acts on the measured qubit itself, for example a Hadamard?

    Not directly. The argument requires that nothing after the controlled gate acts on the control in a way sensitive to coherence. A Hadamard on the measured qubit after measurement produces a random state; on the unmeasured qubit it would interfere branches. The principle holds when the measured qubit is only used as a control and then measured.

  3. A device has 10 qubits and fast mid-circuit measurement. An algorithm in coherent form needs 20 qubits, 10 of which are used only as controls and then measured. How does deferred measurement in reverse help?

    Measuring each control qubit as soon as it has done its job, and replacing its quantum control by classical control, frees it to be reset and reused. If the controls are used one at a time, the 10 control qubits can be replaced by a single recycled qubit, and the algorithm fits in 11 qubits. Iterative phase estimation is exactly this transformation.

Feedforward is most useful when every outcome leads to the intended result after a cheap fix. Here is a small example. In the Hadamard-test circuit of Section 3.1.2, take the controlled unitary to be \(U=e^{i\theta X}\). The two outcomes apply

\[\text{outcome }0:\ \frac{I+U}{2}=\cos\tfrac\theta2\,e^{i\theta X/2},\qquad\text{outcome }1:\ \frac{I-U}{2}=-i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,e^{i\theta X/2}X.\]

Both branches apply the half-angle rotation \(e^{i\theta X/2}\), with an extra \(X\) on outcome \(1\). Since \(X\) commutes with \(e^{i\theta X/2}\), applying \(X\) again after outcome \(1\) gives \(e^{i\theta X/2}\) deterministically, for every input. The outcome probabilities are \(\cos^2\frac\theta2\) and \(\sin^2\frac\theta2\), independent of the input, so the measurement reveals nothing about the data. A fix that is a Pauli gate is a Pauli correction, and Pauli corrections are cheap in nearly every hardware and error-correction scheme.

A related pattern is repeat-until-success. Some circuits apply the desired gate on one outcome and, on the other, a known unitary that can be undone cheaply. On failure, undo and try again. The number of attempts is random, but the final result is exact, and the expected cost can be lower than a deterministic decomposition. For single-qubit rotations in Clifford+\(T\) circuits, repeat-until-success constructions reduce the expected \(T\) count below that of the best deterministic synthesis by a constant factor (Paetznick and Svore, 2014). Postselection without a way to undo failures, as in Section 7.1.3, is not of this kind: there a failure destroys the input.

  1. Verify \((I+e^{i\theta X})/2=\cos\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta X/2}\).

    Factor out \(e^{i\theta X/2}\): \[\frac12e^{i\theta X/2}(e^{-i\theta X/2}+e^{i\theta X/2})=e^{i\theta X/2}\cos(\theta X/2)=\cos\frac\theta2\,e^{i\theta X/2}\], since \(\cos(\theta X/2)=\cos\frac\theta2\,I\) when \(X^2=I\).

  2. For \(\theta=\pi/2\), what are the outcome probabilities, and what gate is applied after correction?

    Each outcome has probability \(\frac12\). The corrected gate is \(e^{i\pi X/4}\), a quarter-turn about the \(X\) axis.

  3. A repeat-until-success circuit succeeds with probability \(3/4\) per attempt and costs 4 \(T\) gates per attempt. What is the expected \(T\) count?

    The number of attempts is geometric with mean \(1/(3/4)=4/3\), so the expected \(T\) count is \(16/3\approx5.3\). The worst case is unbounded but exponentially unlikely: needing more than \(k\) attempts has probability \(4^{-k}\).

Phase estimation in Chapter 4 used many control qubits and a coherent inverse Fourier transform. Measurement and feedback do the same job with one control qubit. Beyond saving qubits, adaptivity opens a view of estimation as a sequence of experiments, each chosen using the results of the last, and of the fundamental limit on how precise a phase can be made with a given amount of evolution.

Let \(U\ket u=e^{2\pi i\phi}\ket u\), with \(\phi=0.b_1b_2\cdots b_m\) exactly in binary. We learn the bits one at a time, from the last to the first, with one control qubit that is measured and reused.

At stage \(k\) (running from \(m\) down to \(1\)), prepare the control in \(\ket+\) and apply controlled-\(U^{2^{k-1}}\). The kicked-back phase is \(2\pi\cdot2^{k-1}\phi\) modulo \(2\pi\), which is \(2\pi\,(0.b_kb_{k+1}\cdots b_m)\): multiplying by \(2^{k-1}\) shifts the binary point past the first \(k-1\) digits, which contribute whole turns. The bits after \(b_k\) are already known from earlier stages. Remove their contribution with a phase gate on the control,

\[P(-\omega_k),\qquad\omega_k=2\pi\,(0.0\,b_{k+1}\cdots b_m),\]

leaving phase \(2\pi\,(0.b_k)=\pi b_k\): the control is \(\ket+\) if \(b_k=0\) and \(\ket-\) if \(b_k=1\). A Hadamard and measurement return \(b_k\) with certainty. This is iterative phase estimation, the deferred-measurement inverse of the textbook circuit (Section 11.1.2).

The resources compare as follows. Qubits: one control instead of \(m\). Controlled-\(U\) uses: \(2^m-1\) in total, exactly as before, since stage \(k\) still needs \(U^{2^{k-1}}\). Coherence: each stage needs coherence only for its own controlled power, not for the whole computation. Classical feedback: the correction angle \(\omega_k\) depends on earlier outcomes, so the controller must compute it between stages. Iterative estimation saves width, not total evolution.

  1. For \(\phi=1/4=0.01_2\) with \(m=2\), carry out both stages.

    Here \(b_1=0\) and \(b_2=1\). Stage \(k=2\) uses \(U^2\): the phase is \(2\pi\cdot2\cdot\frac14=\pi\), no correction is needed, the control is \(\ket-\), and the outcome is \(b_2=1\). Stage \(k=1\) uses \(U\): the phase is \(2\pi\cdot\frac14=\pi/2\); subtracting \(\omega_1=2\pi\cdot(0.01_2)=\pi/2\) leaves \(0\), so the outcome is \(b_1=0\). The result is \(0.01_2=\frac14\).

  2. What happens at stage 1 if the correction is omitted, for \(\phi=1/4\)?

    The control is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0+i\ket1)\). After \(H\), outcome \(0\) has probability \(|1+i|^2/4=\frac12\). The bit is random: without feedback the earlier result cannot be used to remove the known part of the phase.

  3. Why are the bits measured from least significant to most significant, rather than the other way?

    The highest power \(U^{2^{m-1}}\) produces phase \(\pi b_m\) with no contamination from other bits, so \(b_m\) is read first without correction. Each lower power’s phase contains the already-known later bits, which feedback removes. Starting from \(b_1\) with \(U\) alone would give a phase mixing all bits, with nothing yet known to subtract.

Take \(\phi=3/8=0.011_2\), so \(b_1=0\), \(b_2=1\), \(b_3=1\), and \(m=3\).

  • Stage 3, with \(U^4\): phase \(2\pi\cdot4\cdot\frac38=2\pi\cdot\frac32\equiv\pi\). No correction is needed. The control is \(\ket-\); outcome \(b_3=1\).
  • Stage 2, with \(U^2\): phase \(2\pi\cdot\frac34\). Subtract \(\omega_2=2\pi\cdot(0.01)=2\pi\cdot\frac14\), leaving \(\pi\). Outcome \(b_2=1\).
  • Stage 1, with \(U\): phase \(2\pi\cdot\frac38\). Subtract \(\omega_1=2\pi\cdot(0.011)=2\pi\cdot\frac38\), leaving \(0\). Outcome \(b_1=0\).

The acquisition order is \(b_3,b_2,b_1\), but the phase is \(0.b_1b_2b_3=0.011\). Recording bits in the wrong order is an easy implementation error that the problems explore.

The same structure appears inside the textbook circuit. The inverse QFT’s controlled phase gates subtract the contributions of bits already determined, then a Hadamard reads the next bit. If each qubit of the QFT output is measured as soon as it is final, and the measured value classically controls the remaining phase gates, the output statistics are unchanged, by deferred measurement. This is the semiclassical Fourier transform of Griffiths and Niu (1996). It needs only single-qubit gates, measurements, and classical control, no two-qubit gates at all, and it is how Shor’s algorithm can run its \(2n\)-qubit control register on a single recycled qubit.

  1. Repeat the procedure for \(\phi=5/8=0.101_2\).

    Stage 3 (\(U^4\)): phase \(2\pi\cdot\frac{20}{8}\equiv\pi\), so \(b_3=1\). Stage 2 (\(U^2\)): phase \(2\pi\cdot\frac{10}{8}\equiv2\pi\cdot\frac14\); subtract \(\omega_2=2\pi\cdot(0.0b_3)=2\pi\cdot\frac14\), leaving \(0\), so \(b_2=0\). Stage 1 (\(U\)): phase \(2\pi\cdot\frac58\); subtract \(\omega_1=2\pi\cdot(0.0b_2b_3)=2\pi\cdot0.001_2=2\pi\cdot\frac18\), leaving \(2\pi\cdot\frac12=\pi\), so \(b_1=1\). The result is \(0.101_2=\frac58\).

  2. A program stores the bits in acquisition order and reports \(0.b_3b_2b_1\). What does it report for \(\phi=3/8\)?

    It reports \(0.110_2=\frac34\) instead of \(\frac38\). Tests with a symmetric bit pattern such as \(0.101\) would not catch this bug, since the pattern reads the same in both orders; test with asymmetric phases.

  3. How many two-qubit gates does the semiclassical inverse QFT on \(m\) qubits use, and what replaces them?

    None. Each controlled phase gate whose control has already been measured becomes a phase gate conditioned on a classical bit. The \(m(m-1)/2\) controlled rotations become classically controlled single-qubit rotations, combined into one rotation per stage.

The exact examples assumed \(\phi\) has \(m\) binary digits. A general phase does not, so each stage’s outcome is probabilistic, and a wrong early bit corrupts the correction for every later one. Practical adaptive schemes therefore repeat stages, use the outcomes as statistical evidence about \(\phi\), and choose later experiments to be informative given what is already known. Bayesian methods keep a probability distribution over \(\phi\) and update it after each outcome; robust phase estimation (Kimmel, Low, and Yoder, 2015) uses a fixed schedule of powers with enough repetitions per power to bound the error even with noise.

How precise can any such scheme be? Count resources as total uses of \(U\), \(N\). Repeating a single Hadamard test \(N\) times estimates \(\phi\) with error of order \(1/\sqrt N\): the standard quantum limit, the same \(1/\sqrt N\) as classical sampling. Using powers \(U^{2^j}\) and adaptive or Fourier processing reaches error of order \(1/N\): the Heisenberg limit. No strategy does better, for the same reason that amplitude estimation cannot beat \(1/\epsilon\) queries (Section 5.4.2). The improvement comes from coherence across many uses of \(U\), concentrated in long controlled powers.

In the presence of noise that limits how long a controlled power can stay coherent, the Heisenberg scaling holds only up to that coherence time; beyond it, repetition returns to standard scaling. Adaptive estimation is the setting in which this trade-off is managed: use the longest powers that remain reliable, and repeat them.

  1. To estimate a phase to \(10^{-4}\), roughly how many uses of \(U\) does each limit require?

    Standard quantum limit: error \(\sim1/\sqrt N\), so \(N\sim10^8\). Heisenberg limit: error \(\sim1/N\), so \(N\sim10^4\) (up to constants).

  2. Why does an error in an early bit (\(b_m\)) of iterative phase estimation damage every later stage?

    Each correction \(\omega_k\) is built from all later bits. A wrong \(b_m\) adds a wrong term \(2\pi b_m2^{k-1-m}\) to every correction, so each subsequent stage starts from a wrongly shifted phase and its outcome becomes random. Repeating each stage and taking a majority vote makes early bits reliable before they are used.

  3. A device keeps controlled powers coherent only up to \(U^{64}\). How should an estimate to \(10^{-5}\) be organized?

    Use powers up to \(64\) to resolve \(\phi\) to about \(1/64\) per measurement with Heisenberg-like efficiency, then repeat the highest-power experiment many times. Repetition reduces the error as \(1/\sqrt{R}\), so reaching \(10^{-5}\) from a per-experiment resolution of about \(10^{-2}\) needs about \(R\sim10^6\) repetitions of \(U^{64}\), roughly \(6\times10^7\) uses of \(U\), far more than the \(10^5\) the Heisenberg limit would allow with longer coherence.

Measurement can do more than read and steer: it can perform the computation. Entangle the data with prepared auxiliary qubits, measure in chosen bases, and correct the outcomes, and a gate has been applied. Chains of such steps give a model of computation driven entirely by measurement, and a related trick supplies the non-Clifford gates that error-corrected computers find hardest.

Take an input \(\ket\psi=a\ket0+b\ket1\) and an auxiliary qubit in \(\ket+\). Apply a controlled-\(Z\) between them, giving \(a\ket0\ket++b\ket1\ket-\). Now measure the input qubit in the basis

\[\ket{\pm_\alpha}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0\pm e^{i\alpha}\ket1\big).\]

Projecting onto \(\ket{\pm_\alpha}\) gives, for outcome \(m\in\{0,1\}\) (\(m=0\) for \(+\)), the unnormalized auxiliary state

\[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(a\ket++(-1)^me^{-i\alpha}b\ket-\big)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,X^mHP(-\alpha)\ket\psi,\qquad P(\beta)=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\beta}).\]

Each outcome has probability \(\frac12\), whatever \(\ket\psi\) is. The state has moved to the auxiliary qubit, and on the way the gate \(HP(-\alpha)\) has been applied, followed by a known Pauli \(X^m\) that depends on the outcome. This is one-bit teleportation, and the general idea of applying a gate by teleporting through a suitably prepared resource is gate teleportation.

The measurement basis chooses the gate: different \(\alpha\) give different rotations. The Pauli byproduct can be corrected immediately, or tracked classically and accounted for later. Nothing is cloned, since the input qubit is consumed by the measurement, and nothing is sent faster than light, since the byproduct cannot be interpreted without the outcome bit.

  1. For \(\alpha=0\), what gate is applied, and what correction does outcome \(m=1\) need?

    \(X^mH\ket\psi\). For \(m=0\) the result is \(H\ket\psi\). For \(m=1\) it is \(XH\ket\psi\); applying \(X\) gives \(H\ket\psi\).

  2. Verify the formula for \(\ket\psi=\ket0\), \(\alpha=0\), outcome \(m=0\).

    After CZ: \(\ket0\ket+\). Projecting the first qubit onto \(\ket{+}\) gives amplitude \(\braket{+}{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\), leaving \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket+\). The formula gives \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}HP(0)\ket0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\ket+\). They agree.

  3. Show that the outcome probabilities are independent of \(\ket\psi\), and explain why that matters.

    The unnormalized output is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\) times a unitary applied to \(\ket\psi\), so its squared length is \(\frac12\) for every input. The measurement therefore reveals nothing about \(\ket\psi\), which is necessary: a measurement that learned about the data would disturb it, and the gate could not be applied faithfully.

Chain the construction. Prepare a line of qubits in \(\ket+\), apply controlled-\(Z\) between neighbours, and place the input at one end. Measuring the first qubit with angle \(\alpha_1\) teleports \(HP(-\alpha_1)\ket\psi\) onto the second; measuring the second with \(\alpha_2\) teleports \(HP(-\alpha_2)HP(-\alpha_1)\ket\psi\) onto the third; and so on. Since \(H\) and \(P\) generate all single-qubit gates, a line can apply any single-qubit unitary. On a two-dimensional grid of such entangled qubits, a cluster state, measurements can also apply entangling gates. This is measurement-based quantum computation (Raussendorf and Briegel, 2001): all entanglement is prepared in advance, and the computation consists of single-qubit measurements in adaptively chosen bases.

Adaptivity is essential. A byproduct \(X\) from one step does not commute with the next step’s phase gate; instead \(P(\beta)X=e^{i\beta}XP(-\beta)\), so pushing the \(X\) through flips the sign of the next angle. The classical controller therefore chooses each measurement basis using earlier outcomes, flipping signs as needed, and keeps track of the remaining Pauli byproducts, a record called the Pauli frame, to reinterpret the final outcomes. Measurements whose bases do not depend on others can be done simultaneously, so the depth of the computation is set by the chain of classical dependencies.

The model is equivalent in power to the circuit model, not more powerful. Its appeal is architectural: photonic platforms, where two-qubit gates are probabilistic but entangled resource states can be built and checked in advance, use it naturally. Its costs are the size of the resource state, the precision of measurement angles, and fast classical feedforward.

  1. Two steps with angles \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2\) and outcomes \(m_1=0\), \(m_2=0\). What unitary is applied to \(\ket\psi\)?

    \(HP(-\alpha_2)HP(-\alpha_1)\). With no byproducts, no corrections are needed.

  2. Verify \(P(\beta)X=e^{i\beta}XP(-\beta)\) by applying both sides to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\).

    Left side on \(\ket0\): \(P(\beta)\ket1=e^{i\beta}\ket1\). Right side: \(e^{i\beta}XP(-\beta)\ket0=e^{i\beta}\ket1\). On \(\ket1\): left \(P(\beta)\ket0=\ket0\); right \(e^{i\beta}X(e^{-i\beta}\ket1)=\ket0\). Equal.

  3. If the first outcome is \(m_1=1\), how must the second measurement angle be changed to apply the intended \(HP(-\alpha_2)HP(-\alpha_1)\) up to Paulis?

    The state after step 1 is \(XHP(-\alpha_1)\ket\psi\). Step 2 applies \(HP(-\alpha_2')\) to it: \[HP(-\alpha_2')X=e^{-i\alpha_2'}HXP(\alpha_2')=e^{-i\alpha_2'}ZHP(\alpha_2')\]. Choosing \(\alpha_2'=-\alpha_2\) gives \(ZHP(-\alpha_2)\) acting on \(HP(-\alpha_1)\ket\psi\): the intended gate with a \(Z\) byproduct added to the Pauli frame. So the second angle is negated.

Error-correcting codes make Clifford gates (\(H\), \(S\), CNOT) comparatively easy to apply fault-tolerantly, but Clifford gates alone are not universal and can be simulated efficiently classically. The \(T\) gate supplies the missing power, and in many codes it cannot be applied directly. Gate teleportation solves this by moving the difficulty into a state.

Prepare the magic state \(\ket A=T\ket+=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0+e^{i\pi/4}\ket1)\). With data \(\ket\psi=a\ket0+b\ket1\), apply a CNOT from the data to the magic qubit, and measure the magic qubit:

\[\text{outcome }0:\ a\ket0+e^{i\pi/4}b\ket1=T\ket\psi,\qquad\text{outcome }1:\ e^{i\pi/4}\big(a\ket0+e^{-i\pi/4}b\ket1\big)\propto T^\dagger\ket\psi.\]

On outcome \(1\), apply \(S=T^2\): \(ST^\dagger=T\). Either way the data ends in \(T\ket\psi\), using only a CNOT, a measurement, and a classically controlled \(S\), all Clifford operations. This is state injection.

The non-Clifford difficulty has moved into preparing \(\ket A\) with very high fidelity. Magic-state distillation (Bravyi and Kitaev, 2005) takes many noisy copies of \(\ket A\) and, using only Clifford operations and measurements, outputs fewer copies with much lower error; for example, one standard protocol turns 15 copies with error \(p\) into one with error about \(35p^3\). Distillation dominates the cost of many fault-tolerant resource estimates, which is why algorithms are often costed in \(T\) gates or Toffolis (Section 2.3.1), and why the rotation-synthesis costs of Chapters 3, 4, and 9 matter.

This closes the technique sequence. Algorithm design, as Chapter 1 promised, has meant choosing compatible access, encoding the property that matters, engineering interference, constructing a readout, proving correctness, and counting every resource. Measurement and feedback, introduced here last, turn out to touch every stage: they shorten circuits, recycle qubits, and even supply the gates. The next course, Quantum Programming, turns these contracts and calculations into executable, tested circuits.

  1. Verify the outcome-\(0\) branch of state injection for \(\ket\psi=\ket1\).

    The joint state is \(\ket1\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket0+e^{i\pi/4}\ket1)\). CNOT flips the magic qubit: \(\ket1\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\ket1+e^{i\pi/4}\ket0)\). Outcome \(0\) leaves \(e^{i\pi/4}\ket1=T\ket1\), with probability \(\frac12\).

  2. Why is the correction on outcome \(1\) an \(S\) gate and not a Pauli?

    Outcome \(1\) applies \(T^\dagger\) instead of \(T\), and \(T(T^\dagger)^{-1}=T^2=S\). \(S\) is Clifford, so it is cheap fault-tolerantly, but it is not a Pauli; it cannot simply be tracked in a Pauli frame and must be applied (or tracked as a Clifford frame).

  3. A 15-to-1 distillation protocol maps input error \(p\) to about \(35p^3\). Starting from \(p=10^{-3}\), what error after one round and after two? How many raw states does two rounds consume per output?

    One round: \(35\times10^{-9}=3.5\times10^{-8}\). Two rounds: \(35\times(3.5\times10^{-8})^3\approx1.5\times10^{-21}\). Two rounds consume \(15\times15=225\) raw magic states per output (ignoring failed rounds).
