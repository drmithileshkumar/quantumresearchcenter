Interpretations of Quantum Mechanics

What the quantum formalism says, what it leaves open, and how to compare physical accounts

A detector records one result. A unitary calculation produces a correlated state. How should we connect those two descriptions? This advanced course follows that question through concrete experiments, mathematical constraints, and competing accounts of states, events, and probability.

Prerequisites and approach

Complete Quantum Foundations, or bring equivalent knowledge of state vectors, density operators, tensor products, projective measurement, and probability. Quantum Information Theory is useful preparation for channels and distinguishability, but is not required throughout. Chapter 6 also uses elementary gradients and continuity equations.

10 of 10 chapters contain draft lessons with worked examples and solved problems. These are teaching manuscripts in development; advanced results are stated with their assumptions and sources where a full proof lies beyond the course.

Ten draft chapters develop a common measurement model before comparing operational accounts, consistent histories, Everett, Bohmian mechanics, objective collapse, relational quantum mechanics, and QBism. Bell and contextuality constraints, quantum-state ontology, and quantum observers supply tests of what each account can claim. This is a parallel advanced path after Quantum Foundations; Quantum Error Correction is not a prerequisite.

Each chapter has three worked checks, ten exercises with hints and reasoned solutions, a summary, and linked primary-source or research-review readings. Explanations are conversational, but claims are tied to equations and explicit assumptions. No interpretation is presented as an experimentally selected winner. Modified collapse dynamics is distinguished from empirically equivalent readings of the usual formalism.

Conventions: finite-dimensional examples use normalized kets and left-to-right tensor order; |±⟩=(|0⟩±|1⟩)/√2; X, Y, and Z are the usual Pauli matrices; logarithms are natural unless specified. Conditional states require nonzero conditioning probability. Ideal coherent-control thought experiments are distinguished from practical macroscopic experiments. Continuous-variable models state their restricted physical scope.

Read with an assumption ledger: identify the calculation, the physical postulates, the theorem assumptions, and the interpretive conclusion. The final chapter offers a comparative research practicum. A reproducible numerical companion verifies the finite examples; it does not prove the general no-go theorems or select an interpretation.
