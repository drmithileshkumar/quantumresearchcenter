Imagine that someone hands you a qubit. They promise that it was prepared in one of two ways: either in \(|0\rangle\), or in \(|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\). You know the two possibilities, but you do not know which one they chose. You may perform any measurement you like, and then you must give an answer. How often can you be right?

This sounds like a small problem. Yet it forces us to say what it means for two quantum states to be different in a way we can use. Writing down two unequal matrices is easy. Designing an experiment that tells them apart is a different task. Quantum information theory connects these tasks: it turns a mathematical difference into a statement about what an observer can accomplish.

We will keep returning to this one experiment. First we will try a measurement whose behavior we can calculate immediately. Then we will ask whether a better measurement exists, and prove when we have found the best one. That proof will give us a useful measure of state distinguishability. Finally, we will ask what happens when the system passes through noise, or when one kind of wrong answer matters more than the other.

Before calculating. The two states are different. Does that mean some measurement can distinguish them with certainty? Try proposing a measurement, and identify which outcome would settle the question.

1.1 From matrices to decisions

1.1.1 The trace norm as a measurement advantage

Let us begin with a fair choice: each preparation occurs with probability one half. Measure in the computational basis \(\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}\). If the preparation was \(|0\rangle\), you certainly observe 0. If it was \(|+\rangle\), you observe 0 or 1 with equal probability. An outcome of 1 therefore identifies \(|+\rangle\) without ambiguity. An outcome of 0 leaves both preparations possible.

What should we guess when we see 0? Across many trials, half of all trials start with \(|0\rangle\) and produce 0. Another quarter start with \(|+\rangle\) and produce 0. Among the trials giving this outcome, \(|0\rangle\) is twice as common. So our decision rule should be: guess \(|0\rangle\) on outcome 0, and \(|+\rangle\) on outcome 1. Its success probability is

\[p_{\mathrm{success}}=\frac12\underbrace{(1)}_{\text{correct given }|0\rangle}+\frac12\underbrace{\left(\frac12\right)}_{\text{correct given }|+\rangle}=\frac34.\]

We have found a strategy that works 75% of the time. Have we found the best strategy? Nothing in this calculation establishes that. It only evaluates the measurement we happened to choose. A different measurement basis could trade some reliability on the first preparation for a larger improvement on the second. To find out, we need a way to describe all allowed decisions, not just computational-basis measurements.

We will eventually express the best success probability using a quantity called the trace norm. Rather than introducing it as a definition to memorize, let us first do the optimization that makes it necessary.

1.1.2 Writing down every possible decision

Replace our two particular pure states by arbitrary density operators \(\rho\) and \(\sigma\). The source chooses \(\rho\) with probability \(p\) and \(\sigma\) with probability \(1-p\). These are the prior probabilities: what we know before looking at the system. We receive one system, know the two candidate states and the prior, and must guess one of them. There is no “inconclusive” answer in this task.

Even if a measuring apparatus has many possible outcomes, our final answer has only two. We can group all outcomes leading to the guess “\(\rho\)” into one measurement effect M; the remaining outcomes form \(I-M\). Randomized decision rules can also be included in these effects. By the probability rule,

\[\Pr(\text{guess }\rho\mid\rho)=\tr(M\rho),\qquad \Pr(\text{guess }\sigma\mid\sigma)=\tr[(I-M)\sigma].\]

The allowed effects satisfy \(0\le M\le I\). These are operator inequalities: M and I−M must both be positive semidefinite. Equivalently, every eigenvalue of M lies between zero and one. This guarantees valid probabilities for every input state. M need not be a projector; restricting it to projectors at this stage would require justification.

Weighting the two ways to be correct by their priors gives

\[\begin{aligned}p_{\mathrm{success}}(M)&=p\tr(M\rho)+(1-p)\tr[(I-M)\sigma]\\&=1-p+\tr(MH),\qquad H=p\rho-(1-p)\sigma.\end{aligned}\]

Now the task is precise. The source fixes H. We choose M, subject to \(0\le M\le I\), to make \(\tr(MH)\) as large as possible. The constant 1−p is the success probability of always guessing \(\sigma\); the remaining term measures what our decision rule adds to that strategy.

1.1.3 Finding the best measurement

H is Hermitian, so choose an orthonormal eigenbasis and write \(H=\sum_i h_i|i\rangle\langle i|\). In this basis,

\[\tr(MH)=\sum_i h_i\langle i|M|i\rangle.\]

Look at one term. If \(h_i\) is positive, we want its coefficient \(\langle i|M|i\rangle\) as large as possible: one. If \(h_i\) is negative, we want that coefficient to be zero. The constraints on M allow neither values above one nor values below zero. Therefore every allowed effect obeys

\[\tr(MH)\le\sum_{h_i>0}h_i.\]

An upper bound is only half of an optimality proof. Can a single physical effect attain all these choices simultaneously? Yes: choose \(M_*\) to be the projector onto the positive-eigenvalue subspace of H. Its diagonal entries are exactly the zeros and ones we need, and it is a valid effect. Zero eigenvalues do not affect the score, so the choice there is immaterial. We have both a bound that applies to every measurement and a measurement that reaches it.

To put the result in a compact form, let \(a\) be the sum of positive eigenvalues of H and \(b\) the sum of the absolute values of its negative eigenvalues. Then \(\tr H=a-b=2p-1\). The trace norm of a Hermitian operator is the sum of its absolute eigenvalues, so \(\|H\|_1=a+b\). Adding these relations gives \(a=(\|H\|_1+2p-1)/2\). Substituting into our success probability yields

\[\boxed{p_{\mathrm{guess}}=\max_{0\le M\le I}p_{\mathrm{success}}(M)=\frac{1+\|p\rho-(1-p)\sigma\|_1}{2}.}\]

This is the binary minimum-error discrimination formula, often called the Helstrom bound. Here we have derived it, including the optimizing measurement. For a general matrix X, the trace norm is \(\|X\|_1=\tr\sqrt{X^\dagger X}\), the sum of its singular values. For Hermitian H, those singular values are \(|h_i|\), which is why that norm appeared in our calculation. The operator norm \(\|X\|_\infty\) instead keeps only the largest singular value. These norms answer different questions.

With equal priors, the formula becomes particularly transparent. Define the trace distance by

\[T(\rho,\sigma)=\frac12\|\rho-\sigma\|_1,\qquad p_{\mathrm{guess}}=\frac{1+T(\rho,\sigma)}2.\]

The factor one half normalizes T to lie between zero and one. Identical states give T=0 and no improvement over guessing. States with orthogonal supports give T=1 and can be identified perfectly. For intermediate values, T tells us exactly how much distinguishability the best one-shot measurement can extract: the success advantage over a random guess is T/2.

1.1.4 Returning to the qubit

Now we can settle the question we left open. In the ordered basis \((|0\rangle,|1\rangle)\), our original states and their difference are

\[\rho=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=\rho-\sigma=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Multiplying D by itself gives \(D^2=I/2\). Its eigenvalues therefore have magnitude \(1/\sqrt2\); because its trace is zero, one is positive and the other negative. Thus \(\|D\|_1=\sqrt2\), and

\[T=\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad p_{\mathrm{guess}}=\frac{1+1/\sqrt2}{2}\approx0.853553.\]

Our 75% strategy was useful, but it was not optimal. The positive-eigenvalue eigenvector is \(|m_\rho\rangle=\cos(\pi/8)|0\rangle-\sin(\pi/8)|1\rangle\). Measure in the orthonormal basis containing this vector, and guess \(\rho\) when it clicks. For \(|0\rangle\), that outcome occurs with probability \(\cos^2(\pi/8)\). For \(|+\rangle\), the same outcome occurs with probability \(\sin^2(\pi/8)\), so the complementary outcome correctly identifies \(|+\rangle\) with probability \(\cos^2(\pi/8)\). Both conditional success probabilities are approximately 0.853553.

The improved measurement gives up certainty on \(|0\rangle\) to gain more on \(|+\rangle\). The optimum still falls short of one. That remaining error is not a limitation of our chosen basis or experimental patience: the bound covers every allowed measurement on this one supplied system.

Worked check 1.1. Suppose the source instead gives the same state under both labels, but chooses label \(\rho\) with probability 0.8. What should you do? Check your answer using the bound.

Reasoned solution

The system carries no evidence about the label, so always guess the more likely label. This succeeds with probability 0.8. Algebraically, \(H=0.8\rho-0.2\rho=0.6\rho\). Positivity and unit trace give \(\|H\|_1=0.6\), hence \(p_{\mathrm{guess}}=(1+0.6)/2=0.8\). Unequal priors can make a good guess possible even when the states are identical.

1.2 Fidelity and physical processing

1.2.1 Why discarding cannot improve a test

Suppose we pass the supplied state through a noisy device before trying to identify it. Could the device make the task easier? It might improve the performance of a particular detector. But could it improve on the best possible measurement of the original input?

Think of the entire procedure—device followed by measurement—as one larger measuring apparatus. It is still a valid experiment on the original system. Our earlier optimization already allowed every such experiment. Therefore a fixed physical process cannot increase the optimal success probability. With equal priors, the relation between success and trace distance immediately gives

\[T(\mathcal N(\rho),\mathcal N(\sigma))\le T(\rho,\sigma),\]

where \(\mathcal N\) denotes the same quantum channel applied to either candidate. A channel is a deterministic physical operation on the system, possibly involving an ancilla and discarding part of the output. Chapter 2 will develop its mathematical descriptions.

We can also make the apparatus argument explicit. If M is an output effect, its input effect is \(\mathcal N^\dagger(M)\), where the adjoint is defined by \(\tr[M\mathcal N(\tau)]=\tr[\mathcal N^\dagger(M)\tau]\). A channel’s adjoint is positive and sends I to I. Consequently \(0\le M\le I\) implies \(0\le\mathcal N^\dagger(M)\le I\). Every output decision therefore corresponds to an allowed input decision, although the reverse need not hold.

The deterministic qualification matters. If we keep only a rare successful measurement outcome and discard the other trials, the surviving conditional states can become easier to distinguish. The success probability of that selection is then part of the accounting. The contraction inequality applies to the complete trace-preserving process, not to a selected branch after renormalization.

1.2.2 Overlap as a second comparison

Trace distance asks how well we can tell two states apart. Sometimes our question is framed the other way around: how well does a prepared state agree with a target? If the target is a pure state \(|\psi\rangle\), a natural test measures the projector \(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\). An actual state \(\sigma\) passes with probability \(\langle\psi|\sigma|\psi\rangle\).

This motivates a second quantity, fidelity. For general mixed states we use

\[F(\rho,\sigma)=\left\|\sqrt\rho\sqrt\sigma\right\|_1^2.\]

The square root of a positive matrix is obtained by taking square roots of its eigenvalues while keeping its eigenvectors. If \(\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\), its square root is itself. The product \(\sqrt\rho\sqrt\sigma\) then has only one possibly nonzero singular value, equal to \(\sqrt{\langle\psi|\sigma|\psi\rangle}\). Squaring its trace norm recovers the test probability above. For mixed targets the formula remains useful, but should not be interpreted as the pass probability of that same pure-state projector test.

We use squared fidelity throughout this course. Some references call \(\sqrt F\) fidelity instead. Neither convention changes the physics, but mixing them changes equations. For two pure states, our convention gives \(F=|\langle\psi|\phi\rangle|^2\). Identical states have fidelity one; orthogonal states have fidelity zero. Larger fidelity means more similarity, whereas larger trace distance means more distinguishability.

1.2.3 Connecting fidelity to distinguishability

For two pure states, we can derive the relationship directly. Choose their phases so that \(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle=c\) is real and nonnegative. For \(c<1\), choose a unit vector \(|\eta\rangle\) orthogonal to \(|\psi\rangle\) such that \(|\phi\rangle=c|\psi\rangle+\sqrt{1-c^2}|\eta\rangle\). In this two-dimensional span, the difference of their density matrices is

\[\begin{pmatrix}1-c^2&-c\sqrt{1-c^2}\\-c\sqrt{1-c^2}&-(1-c^2)\end{pmatrix}.\]

Its trace is zero and its determinant is \(-(1-c^2)\), so its eigenvalues are \(\pm\sqrt{1-c^2}\). All remaining eigenvalues are zero. Taking half the sum of absolute eigenvalues gives \(T=\sqrt{1-c^2}\). Since \(F=c^2\), we obtain \(T=\sqrt{1-F}\). The identical-state case c=1 gives T=0 directly.

For mixed states, equality need not hold. The general bounds are

\[1-\sqrt{F(\rho,\sigma)}\le T(\rho,\sigma)\le\sqrt{1-F(\rho,\sigma)}.\]

These are the Fuchs–van de Graaf inequalities, stated here without their full proof; see Watrous, Chapter 3 for the fidelity and distance results. They let us translate a fidelity guarantee into a bound on measurement distinguishability. For example, F≥0.99 implies T≤0.1. It does not imply T≤0.01. The square root matters when reporting an error budget.

Consider \(\rho=|0\rangle\langle0|\) and \(\sigma=I/2\). Here F=1/2, but subtracting the matrices gives eigenvalues ±1/2, hence T=1/2. This is smaller than \(\sqrt{1-F}=1/\sqrt2\). Our pure-state identity cannot be carried over just because one of the two states is pure.

Fidelity also obeys a processing theorem: \(F(\mathcal N(\rho),\mathcal N(\sigma))\ge F(\rho,\sigma)\). We state this result rather than infer it from the trace-distance inequalities, which do not by themselves prove it. The physical interpretation agrees with our earlier argument: noise can make states look alike by erasing the distinctions between them.

Worked check 1.2. Compare \(|+\rangle\) and \(|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2\) before and after complete dephasing, which sets off-diagonal entries to zero in the computational basis.

Reasoned solution

The input vectors have zero overlap, so F=0 and T=1. Their density matrices are \(\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\). Dephasing sends both to I/2, giving F=1 and T=0. Before noise, an X-basis measurement distinguishes them perfectly. Afterwards no measurement can distinguish them. Their identical initial diagonal entries never meant that their full input states were identical.

1.3 Asymmetric tests

1.3.1 Constraints instead of equal costs

So far, we have maximized the probability of giving the correct label. Both kinds of wrong answer enter the average error with their prior weights. But imagine that \(\rho\) describes a signal that a detector must rarely miss, while \(\sigma\) describes background noise. A false alarm and a missed signal may have quite different consequences. We should formulate the task to reflect that distinction.

Continue to let M mean “accept the signal hypothesis \(\rho\).” There are now two separate error probabilities:

\[\alpha=1-\tr(M\rho)\quad\text{(missed signal)},\qquad \beta=\tr(M\sigma)\quad\text{(false alarm)}.\]

Suppose the missed-signal probability must be at most \(\epsilon\). Among all tests meeting that requirement, we want the one with the smallest false-alarm probability. The problem is

\[\beta_\epsilon=\min_M\tr(M\sigma)\quad\text{subject to}\quad 0\le M\le I,\quad\tr(M\rho)\ge1-\epsilon.\]

Notice what changed. We no longer need a prior to state this task: each error is conditional on one of the two hypotheses, and the acceptable miss rate is supplied externally. The earlier minimum-average-error measurement need not be optimal for this constrained problem. A mathematical expression is useful only after we have checked that it answers the operational question being asked.

1.3.2 Spending a false-alarm budget

Let us solve a small case completely. Take commuting states whose diagonal probabilities are \(\rho=(0.8,0.2)\) and \(\sigma=(0.3,0.7)\). A measurement in their shared eigenbasis reveals an outcome, call it a or b. No off-diagonal entry of M contributes to either trace, so we can replace M by its diagonal without changing either error. Write those diagonal entries as \(m_a,m_b\), each between zero and one: the probabilities of accepting the signal after seeing a or b.

We then have \(\alpha=1-0.8m_a-0.2m_b\) and \(\beta=0.3m_a+0.7m_b\). If \(\epsilon=0.2\), choosing \(m_a=1,m_b=0\) accepts 80% of true signals and falsely accepts 30% of background trials. Could a mixture of the two outcomes do better?

Compare what each acceptance buys us. Increasing \(m_a\) by a small amount raises signal acceptance by 0.8 times that amount and raises false alarms by 0.3 times it. The false-alarm cost per unit of signal acceptance is therefore 0.3/0.8=0.375. For b it is 0.7/0.2=3.5. Outcome a supplies signal acceptance much more cheaply. An optimum must use a as much as possible before paying for b; otherwise we could shift acceptance from b to a, preserve signal acceptance, and reduce false alarms.

This is the likelihood-ratio principle in a two-outcome example. The ratios \(\rho_i/\sigma_i\) tell us which observations favor the signal most strongly. We have derived the ordering by comparing costs, rather than merely asserting a decision rule.

Worked check 1.3. Now tighten the requirement to \(\alpha\le0.1\). Find an optimal test and its false-alarm probability.

Reasoned solution

We need signal acceptance at least 0.9. Use a fully, giving 0.8, then obtain the remaining 0.1 from b. Thus \(0.8+0.2m_b=0.9\), so \(m_b=0.5\). The effect is \(M=\operatorname{diag}(1,0.5)\), and \(\beta=0.3+0.7(0.5)=0.65\). We accept every a outcome and toss a fair coin after b. The cost comparison proves optimality: replacing any acceptance of a by acceptance of b would increase the false-alarm rate. Tightening the miss requirement from 0.2 to 0.1 has forced a substantial increase in false alarms.

1.3.3 From a solved example to a general method

Our optimal effect \(\operatorname{diag}(1,0.5)\) is not a projector. This illustrates why we allowed general effects from the beginning. A valid test can include deliberate randomness. For the minimum-average-error problem, the spectral argument showed that a projector always suffices. A constrained test has a different optimum, so we must not impose that earlier conclusion without checking it.

For states that do not commute, there may be no shared eigenbasis that turns the problem into classical outcome probabilities. Nevertheless, the matrix optimization remains well defined. It is a semidefinite program: the quantity to minimize and the scalar constraint are linear in the entries of a Hermitian matrix, while positivity restricts the allowed matrices. These features give us a systematic route beyond hand-solvable examples.

Why is convexity useful here? If \(M_1\) and \(M_2\) both meet the miss constraint, then \(tM_1+(1-t)M_2\), with \(0\le t\le1\), also meets it. Physically, we can flip a coin to choose between the two tests. Their errors average with the same weights. But feasibility is not optimality. A numerical measurement with false-alarm probability b shows that \(\beta_\epsilon\le b\). To certify the optimum, we also need a matching lower bound applying to all feasible measurements, just as the spectral proof supplied a bound for our first task.

We have moved from a concrete guessing experiment to two general ways of assessing a test: its average success, or one error minimized under a constraint on the other. Both concern one supplied system. If we are given many independently prepared copies, we can ask how rapidly the error decreases with their number; Chapter 3 will connect that question to relative entropy. First, Chapter 2 will examine the physical processes that carry, alter, or discard the distinctions we have learned to measure.
