1.1

Mathematical Proof

1.1.1 Prime-producing polynomials§

Try a few more values of \(n\). For \(n=3,4,5\), the expression gives \(53,61,71\), again all prime. In fact, it gives a prime for every whole number from zero through thirty-nine. Forty successful cases make the pattern look promising.

Then the next value breaks it:

\[40^2+40+41=1681=41\cdot41.\]

This number has a divisor other than one and itself, so it is not prime. There was no mistake in the earlier calculations. The mistake would have been to conclude from them that the expression must always produce a prime.

1.1.2 Counterexamples and universal claims§

The claim “this expression produces a prime for every nonnegative integer” includes numbers we have not tried. Checking more values can give us more evidence, but there will always be another value beyond the ones we checked.

A single failure has a different effect. The value \(n=40\) is enough to disprove the claim, because the claim said every value would work. We call such a failure a counterexample.

Examples are still useful. They help us notice patterns and formulate conjectures—claims we suspect are true. But establishing a conjecture requires an argument that covers every case it makes a claim about. If there are only finitely many cases, checking them all may suffice. When there are infinitely many, we need a reason that applies without examining them one at a time.

1.1.3 Proof of closure under addition for even integers§

Consider another familiar pattern: adding two even integers gives an even integer. We could try \(2+4\), \(6+8\), and many more sums, but we would face the same difficulty. How could those examples establish the claim for every pair?

The definition of an even integer gives us a way forward. Any two even integers can be written as \(2m\) and \(2n\), where \(m\) and \(n\) are integers. Their sum is

\[2m+2n=2(m+n).\]

Since \(m+n\) is an integer, the sum is twice an integer and therefore even. We did not choose particular values for \(m\) and \(n\), so the argument applies to every pair of even integers. This is a proof.

The proof works because each step has a justification. We used the meaning of “even,” the distributive law to factor out two, and the fact that a sum of integers is an integer. A reader can inspect these steps and see why the conclusion follows.

1.2

Formal Reasoning

The even-number argument relies on familiar arithmetic. A longer or less familiar proof may be harder to judge. To check it reliably, we need to know which assumptions it uses and which steps are allowed.

1.2.1 Axioms and inference rules§

If we ask why the distributive law is valid, we can appeal to properties of arithmetic. But an argument cannot keep appealing to earlier arguments without ever having a starting point. At some stage, we must state the assumptions from which we are reasoning. These starting assumptions are called axioms.

We must also say how conclusions may be drawn. For example, if we have established a statement \(P\), and also established that \(P\) implies \(Q\), we may conclude \(Q\). A rule of this kind is called a rule of inference.

A formal theory specifies a language for writing statements, a collection of axioms, and rules of inference. A formal proof is a finite sequence of statements, each justified by an axiom or by a rule applied to earlier statements.

In ordinary mathematical writing, we leave out many routine steps so that the main idea is easier to follow. Formalization makes those steps explicit. Its purpose is to make it possible to check whether an argument follows the rules, even when finding that argument required considerable insight.

1.2.2 Hilbert’s program§

In the early twentieth century, David Hilbert sought to put mathematics on secure foundations by making its axioms and proof rules explicit. An important part of his program was to establish that the resulting rules could not lead to contradictions.

A theory is consistent if it cannot prove both a statement and its negation. Without this requirement, the rules could support conflicting conclusions. In ordinary classical logic, a contradiction would even allow every statement to be derived.

A further ambition is that the axioms should settle every statement in their language: either the statement has a proof, or its negation does. A theory with this property is called complete. Consistency and completeness make different demands. A theory might never contradict itself and still leave some questions unanswered.

There is also a question about methods. Given a statement, could we always determine whether it has a proof by following a fixed set of instructions? If such a procedure always stops with the correct answer, provability in the theory is decidable. This related decision problem brings computation into the discussion. It asks how to obtain an answer, rather than only whether the axioms supply one.

1.2.3 Proof checking, proof search, and decidability§

Suppose someone hands us a proposed proof. We can inspect each line and check its justification. If we find an invalid step, we have rejected that particular argument. We have not shown that the conclusion has no proof; another argument might succeed.

There is a simple, if impractical, way to search for a proof. Assume proofs are written as finite strings over a finite alphabet and can be checked mechanically. List all candidate strings in order of length, using a fixed order for strings of the same length, and test them. If a proof exists, it will eventually appear.

If no proof exists, however, the search has no reason to stop. After any finite number of failed attempts, longer candidates remain. This is why searching does not automatically give a decision procedure. It also does not prove that deciding is impossible: a different method might detect the absence of a proof without searching through candidates.

Completeness would help. We could alternate between searching for a proof of the statement and searching for a proof of its negation. In a complete theory, one search must eventually succeed. In a consistent theory, they cannot both succeed. Together with mechanically checkable proofs, these properties would give us a way to settle each question.

1.3

Models of Computation

A proof search follows instructions, but we have not yet said precisely what counts as an instruction or a procedure. A familiar calculation gives us a concrete starting point.

1.3.1 Euclid’s algorithm§

To find the greatest common divisor of \(48\) and \(18\), we could list their divisors and compare the lists. Euclid’s algorithm avoids that work. Divide the larger number by the smaller one, then replace the pair by the smaller number and the remainder:

\[48=2\cdot18+12,\qquad18=1\cdot12+6,\qquad12=2\cdot6+0.\]

The pairs are therefore

\[(48,18)\longrightarrow(18,12)\longrightarrow(12,6)\longrightarrow(6,0).\]

When the second number is zero, the first is the answer: six.

There are two things to justify. First, the replacement must preserve the common divisors. If \(a=qb+r\), any divisor of both \(a\) and \(b\) also divides \(r=a-qb\). Conversely, a divisor of both \(b\) and \(r\) divides \(a=qb+r\). Thus the greatest common divisor stays the same.

Second, the process must stop. Each nonzero remainder is smaller than the preceding positive divisor. A decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers cannot continue indefinitely. The procedure therefore reaches zero and returns the correct answer.

1.3.2 Configurations and transitions§

Imagine pausing the calculation after the first division. To resume it, we need the current pair, \((18,12)\), and the instruction to repeat the division step. The earlier pair, \((48,18)\), is no longer needed to continue.

A configuration is a record of everything needed to resume a calculation. What belongs in it depends on how closely we describe the procedure. At the beginning of Euclid’s repeated step, the two numbers are enough because we already know which instruction comes next. If we pause halfway through a division, we may also need temporary values and a record of our place in the instructions.

A transition takes one configuration to the next. A particular execution, or run, is a sequence of these transitions. The four pairs above describe one run of Euclid’s algorithm. The algorithm itself is the rule that governs every allowed input pair, together with the condition telling it when to stop.

1.3.3 Turing machines§

Euclid’s algorithm uses instructions such as “divide and find the remainder.” To describe computation in general, it helps to break instructions into simpler operations. A Turing machine provides one way to do this.

The machine has a tape divided into cells, a head that reads one cell at a time, and a finite set of internal states. A rule specifies what to do for each combination of the current state and the symbol being read: write a symbol, move the head one cell, and enter a new state.

For example, a machine could scan a block of ones by repeatedly moving right while it reads a one, then halt when it reaches a blank. More elaborate rule tables can carry out arithmetic. A configuration records the tape contents, the head position, and the internal state, so it determines which rule applies next.

The tape has no fixed limit on available space. This does not mean that a finite calculation uses infinite memory: after finitely many steps, the head has visited only finitely many cells. The model allows more space whenever it is needed, while keeping each individual step simple and precisely specified.

1.4

Limits of Computation

A machine follows a finite list of rules. Once that list is written down, another machine can read it as input. This makes it possible for programs to simulate—and ask questions about—other programs.

1.4.1 Program encodings and universal computation§

A Turing machine’s rule table is finite, so we can write it as a string of symbols. That string can be placed on the tape of another machine, along with an input. The second machine can read the rules and simulate the first machine, one step at a time.

A universal machine does this for any correctly encoded machine and input. There is no need to build a different physical device for every calculation. Changing the encoded instructions changes the computation. The same idea underlies a stored-program computer.

The Church–Turing thesis says that any calculation carried out by an effective mechanical procedure can be performed by a Turing machine. Different proposed accounts of computation, including systems based on function substitution, have turned out to describe the same computable functions. This agreement supports the thesis. It is not a proof covering an independently defined collection of every conceivable procedure.

Universality concerns what can be computed, not how efficiently. In the standard model, quantum computers do not make uncomputable functions computable. Their importance lies in the possibility of changing the resources required for tasks that are already computable.

1.4.2 The halting problem§

Some programs halt; others run forever. Euclid’s algorithm halts because its positive remainders keep decreasing. A program instructed simply to repeat the same action forever does not. Can one procedure determine which behavior occurs for any supplied program and input?

Suppose such a procedure exists. Call it \(H(P,x)\), where \(P\) is the description of a program and \(x\) is its input. We require \(H\) itself to halt on every input and give the correct answer.

Using \(H\), construct a new program \(D\). On input \(p\), it asks whether the program described by \(p\) halts when given \(p\) itself as input. If \(H(p,p)\) says yes, \(D\) enters an endless loop. If it says no, \(D\) halts.

Now give \(D\) its own description, \(d\), as input. If \(H(d,d)\) predicts that this run halts, the instructions of \(D\) make it run forever. If \(H(d,d)\) predicts that it runs forever, the instructions make it halt. Either prediction is wrong.

The contradiction comes from assuming that \(H\) always gives the right answer. No such general procedure exists. This does not prevent us from proving that a particular program halts, as we did for Euclid’s algorithm. It rules out one method that correctly settles every case and always finishes.

1.4.3 Incompleteness and undecidability§

The halting argument gives a precise example of a question that no algorithm can settle in every case. It also helps clarify what it means for Hilbert’s ambitions to have limits.

Gödel’s incompleteness results concern a fixed theory. Suppose its axioms can be generated by an algorithm, it is consistent, and it includes enough arithmetic to reason about integer calculations. Then some sentences can be neither proved nor refuted from those axioms. Adding a new axiom may settle a particular sentence, but if the enlarged theory still meets the conditions, it too has unsettled sentences. Under the usual additional conditions, such a theory also cannot prove its own consistency.

A different question concerns logical validity. First-order logic lets us write statements about objects and their relations using phrases such as “for every” and “there exists.” A statement is valid if it holds under every permitted interpretation of those objects and relations. For example, in a nonempty collection, “if every object has a property, then some object has that property” is valid. Reversing the implication is not.

Church and Turing showed that no algorithm always decides whether an arbitrary first-order statement is valid. Gödel’s completeness theorem for first-order logic does guarantee that every valid statement has a logical proof. There is no contradiction: finding proofs of valid statements does not tell a search when to stop on invalid ones. Nor does proving every logically valid statement mean that a chosen set of arithmetic axioms settles every statement about integers.

These results limit general methods without undermining the proofs and algorithms that do work. For a task we can solve, further questions remain: how should we represent the input, how much work will the calculation take, and what information will its steps preserve? Those questions lead from computability to the design of computations.
