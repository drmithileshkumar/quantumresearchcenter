5.1

Principles of the Model

The postulates specify allowed states and changes. A computational model also specifies how inputs are prepared, which steps are elementary, and how outputs and costs are assessed.

5.1.1 Registers and initialization§

A classical computation stores a bit string and updates it according to a program. In the quantum circuit model, the working memory is a register of qubits. An \(n\)-qubit register has computational-basis states \(\ket{x}\), one for every \(n\)-bit string \(x\), and a pure state

\[\ket\psi=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\alpha_x\ket x,\qquad\sum_x|\alpha_x|^2=1.\]

The amplitudes describe one joint state. They are not a list of classical answers that can all be read out. A computational-basis measurement returns one string, with probability \(|\alpha_x|^2\).

A computation begins with a specified preparation, often \(\ket{0}^{\otimes n}\). For a classical input \(x\), applying \(X\) to the positions where \(x\) has a one prepares \(\ket x\). Extra working qubits, called ancillas, are usually initialized to \(\ket0\). Preparing a more general state takes operations too; its cost is part of the computation.

5.1.2 Gates, locality, and interference§

A gate is a specified unitary operation on a small number of qubits. The gate preserves normalization for every input, as required by Chapter 4. A one-qubit gate acts on its chosen wire and as the identity on the other wires. For two qubits, a gate on the first is \(U\otimes I\); a gate on the second is \(I\otimes U\).

The program is a sequence of gates \(U_1,\ldots,U_T\). Before the final measurement, the state is

\[\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{out}}}=U_T\cdots U_2U_1\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}.\]

The first gate appears on the right because it acts on the input first. Local gates build a global transformation by composition. We do not assume that an arbitrary operation on all \(n\) qubits is available at unit cost.

Linearity applies the same gate to every component of a superposition. When different paths arrive at the same configuration, their amplitudes add and can reinforce or cancel. A useful algorithm arranges this interference so that the final measurement is likely to give a useful result. Merely creating a superposition does not ensure an advantage.

5.1.3 Measurement, control, and computational cost§

Measurement converts part of the quantum state into a classical record. For a final computational-basis measurement,

\[p(y\mid x)=\left|\bra yU_T\cdots U_1\ket{x,0\ldots0}\right|^2.\]

This expression describes measuring the entire register. If only designated output qubits are read, sum the probabilities of all full strings consistent with that output. A single run samples an outcome; it does not reveal the amplitudes themselves.

Measurements can also occur during a computation. Their recorded bits may choose later gates through classical control. A measurement updates the state conditional on the observed result, so it can change the interference available to later gates. It cannot generally be inserted into a circuit without changing what the circuit computes.

A quantum algorithm specifies how a classical procedure constructs circuits for different input sizes and interprets their outputs. For efficiency, that construction and the required quantum resources must scale appropriately with input size. Useful resources include qubit count, gate count, circuit depth, preparation, and the number of repeated runs. A probabilistic algorithm must also state its success criterion; for example, a decision procedure may be required to answer correctly with probability at least \(2/3\) on every valid input. This is a standard bounded-error threshold, not a claim that every circuit achieves it.

5.2

Circuits and Worked Examples

A circuit turns an ordered list of operations into a readable diagram. We first learn its symbols, then follow the amplitudes through one- and two-qubit examples.

5.2.1 Reading a quantum circuit§

A circuit draws time from left to right. Each horizontal wire tracks a qubit, with its initial state written at the left. A labeled box on a wire applies a gate to that qubit. A wire with no gate carries the identity during that step. The drawing represents the ordering of operations, not the physical path of a particle.

Boxes on separate wires can act in parallel when the hardware permits. A gate spanning several wires acts jointly on those qubits. A filled control dot connected to a target \(\oplus\) denotes controlled-NOT, or CNOT: it flips the target exactly when the control basis label is one.

\[\operatorname{CNOT}\ket{a,b}=\ket{a,b\oplus a},\qquad a,b\in\{0,1\}.\]

This rule extends linearly to superpositions. The control dot does not measure the control qubit. A measurement box, labeled \(M\) here, produces a classical bit; a double line represents that classical record. When a later gate depends on a measured bit, its control is classical and must be distinguished from a coherent quantum control.

5.2.2 A one-qubit interference circuit§

Start with \(\ket0\), apply \(H\), then \(Z\), then \(H\), and finally measure. Follow the amplitudes through the circuit:

\[\ket0\xrightarrow{H}\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\xrightarrow{Z}\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}\xrightarrow{H}\ket1.\]

The measurement therefore returns one with certainty. The \(Z\) gate changes only a relative sign, leaving the immediate computational-basis probabilities unchanged. The final \(H\) makes that sign observable by combining paths: the amplitudes for zero cancel, and those for one reinforce.

If we omit \(Z\), the circuit instead applies \(HH=I\) and returns zero with certainty. If we measure between the first \(H\) and \(Z\), either observed basis state yields equal final probabilities after the last \(H\). The intermediate measurement removes the coherence needed for the original deterministic result. A circuit is therefore a precise prescription for both the gates and the locations of measurements.

5.2.3 A two-qubit circuit and its joint state§

Prepare two qubits in \(\ket{00}\). Apply \(H\) to the first, then CNOT with the first as control and the second as target:

\[\ket{00}\xrightarrow{H\otimes I}\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{CNOT}}\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.\]

The first gate creates a superposition in the control qubit. CNOT leaves the \(00\) component fixed and sends \(10\) to \(11\). Measuring both qubits gives either \(00\) or \(11\), each with probability \(1/2\). These are the amplitudes of the entangled state introduced by the composite-state postulate.

CNOT has not made two independent copies of an arbitrary input. For a control \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) and target \(\ket0\), its output is \(a\ket{00}+b\ket{11}\), which generally differs from \((a\ket0+b\ket1)\otimes(a\ket0+b\ket1)\). The circuit creates a joint state, not a pair of separately readable amplitude lists.

These examples give the basic model: prepare a register, compose local unitary gates, and obtain classical output through specified measurements. The next chapter develops composite systems and entanglement in detail. The later chapter on circuits and universality asks how larger operations are constructed and how much they cost.
