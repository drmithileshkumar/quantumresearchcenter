4.1

Vectors and Matrices

Path sums describe every operation, but they grow quickly: an operation on two qubits already has sixteen arrows. A compact language groups the amplitudes of a state into one object and the weights of an operation into another, so that the sum and product rules become a single multiplication. The conditions found for the weights then become one equation, which names the operations quantum mechanics allows.

4.1.1 State vectors and ket notation§

A state vector collects the amplitudes into one object. Choose an order for the basis labels, then write the coefficients in that order:

\[\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\ket0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The ket brackets name a vector; they do not perform an operation. The computational-basis probabilities are \(|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\beta|^2\), which add to one.

For two qubits ordered \(00,01,10,11\), the same convention gives four coordinates. Keep that order beside a calculation until it becomes familiar: swapping two coordinates changes the represented state unless you also change the basis labels.

Superposition is basis-dependent. The vector \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) combines two computational-basis vectors, but can itself be one vector of another basis. The physical state stays the same when we change how we describe it.

4.1.2 Operations as matrices§

Applying an operation by path sums means writing out every arrow. Is there a more compact way? Collect the weights in a table whose entry in row \(j\) and column \(i\) is the weight of the arrow \(i\to j\). This table is a matrix \(U\), with \(U_{ji}=w(i\to j)\).

One step changes the state by \(v^\prime_j=\sum_iw(i\to j)\,v_i\). In matrix language this is \(v^\prime_j=\sum_iU_{ji}v_i\), the matrix–vector product: \(\ket{\psi^\prime}=U\ket\psi\). The sum and product rules for one step are exactly matrix multiplication.

Column \(i\) of \(U\) holds the weights of the arrows leaving \(i\), so it is the state \(U\ket i\). For the operations we have met, writing \(X\) for NOT and \(\operatorname{diag}(a,b)\) for the matrix with diagonal entries \(a,b\) and zeros elsewhere,

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1),\qquad H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Two steps, first \(U\) and then \(V\), sum over the intermediate configuration \(j\): \((VU)_{ki}=\sum_jV_{kj}U_{ji}\). This is the matrix product, with the rightmost matrix acting first. For example, \(HH=I\), the identity matrix.

Why is a matrix determined by what it does to the basis states? Because \(U(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)=\alpha\,U\ket0+\beta\,U\ket1\): each configuration’s amplitude is carried along separately and the results add. This property, linearity, is the sum rule in another form.

Which matrices are valid operations? The two conditions on the weights of an operation decide, and their compact form needs one more tool.

4.1.3 Inner products, bras, and adjoints§

The conditions on an operation were \(\sum_j|w(i\to j)|^2=1\) and \(\sum_jw(i\to j)^*\,w(k\to j)=0\) for \(i\neq k\). Since \(w(i\to j)=U_{ji}\), these are statements about the columns of \(U\): each column has squared magnitudes adding to \(1\), and for two different columns, the conjugated entries of one times the entries of the other add to \(0\). Such sums deserve a name.

The inner product of two vectors is

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j.\]

Why the conjugate? So that \(\braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\psi_j|^2\) is real and nonnegative. Without it, the one-coordinate vector \((i)\) would have “squared length” \(i^2=-1\). The norm \(\|\psi\|=\sqrt{\braket\psi\psi}\) is the length of \(\ket\psi\); a state vector has norm \(1\). Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is \(0\), and a basis of orthogonal unit vectors is orthonormal.

The bra \(\bra\phi\) is the row of conjugated coordinates of \(\ket\phi\), so that the row \(\bra\phi\) times the column \(\ket\psi\) is the inner product \(\braket\phi\psi\). For example, \(\bra0=(1,0)\), and \(\braket0\psi=\alpha\) picks out the first coordinate.

The conditions on an operation now say that the columns of \(U\) form an orthonormal basis. To write this as one equation, define the adjoint \(U^\dagger\) of a matrix as its conjugate transpose: \((U^\dagger)_{ij}=U_{ji}^*\). Then \((U^\dagger U)_{ik}=\sum_jU_{ji}^*U_{jk}\) is the inner product of columns \(i\) and \(k\), and the conditions say exactly

\[U^\dagger U=I.\]

A matrix with this property is unitary, and \(U^\dagger\) is its inverse: reverse each arrow and conjugate its weight.

Is the adjoint always the inverse? No. For the matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\), which is not unitary, the adjoint is \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\) itself, while the inverse is \(\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1)\). In general the adjoint is characterized by \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\): it moves a matrix from one side of an inner product to the other.

4.2

Postulates

We now collect the rules into a model for finite quantum systems. States describe preparations, unitaries describe closed-system evolution, and measurements connect the calculation to outcomes. The new ingredient is the state left after a measurement. We will also distinguish repeating a measurement from preparing a fresh state for each run.

4.2.1 State and evolution postulates§

The experiments in Chapter 3 motivated a mathematical description. We now state its rules explicitly for finite-dimensional systems. They are physical postulates, tested through their predictions, rather than deductions from classical probability.

Pure states. A pure state is a unit vector in a complex inner-product space. Vectors differing only by a global phase represent the same physical state.

Closed-system evolution. Evolution is unitary: \(\ket\psi\mapsto U\ket\psi\), with \(U^\dagger U=I\).

For a qubit, remove a global phase so its first coordinate is real and nonnegative. Then

\[\ket\psi=\cos\frac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\ket1,\qquad0\leq\theta\leq\pi.\]

One angle fixes the two magnitudes; the other fixes their relative phase. At a pole, where one component vanishes, that relative phase has no effect.

A pure-state vector is not the description of every preparation or every subsystem. Classical uncertainty about preparation and entanglement with another system call for density matrices, introduced when needed and developed in Appendix D. We begin with pure states so the calculations remain visible.

4.2.2 The measurement postulate§

The measurement rule gives the probability of each outcome. What is the state after the measurement?

Perform an ideal computational-basis measurement on a qubit and obtain \(0\), then measure it again immediately. Experiments find \(0\) again, every time. Which state gives \(0\) with certainty? Only \(\ket0\), up to a global phase. So after the outcome \(0\), the qubit is in the state \(\ket0\).

Measurement. Measuring the state \(\sum_j\alpha_j\ket j\) in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\alpha_j|^2\), and leaves the system in the state \(\ket j\).

For \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\), the outcome is \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\); after the outcome \(1\), the state is \(\ket1\). A single run gives a single outcome; the probabilities describe how often each outcome occurs.

This also explains why measuring between two applications of \(H\) destroys their return to the start: after the measurement the qubit is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and \(H\) applied to either gives each outcome with probability \(1/2\).

Is a measurement a closed-system unitary evolution? No. When the outcome is \(0\), it sends both \(\ket0\) and \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) to \(\ket0\). Different states merge, so a measurement cannot be undone and is not unitary.

4.2.3 Repeated measurements on independently prepared states§

One measurement produces one outcome. To test a predicted probability, prepare the state afresh on each run and measure every copy in the same way.

If the outcome probability is \(p\), its observed fraction over \(N\) independent runs has standard deviation

\[\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}N}.\]

For \(p=1/2\), 100 runs give a standard deviation of 0.05; 10,000 give 0.005. Reducing this typical fluctuation tenfold costs a hundredfold increase in samples. This is an uncertainty scale, not a guarantee that every experiment lands within it.

Measuring one qubit repeatedly is a different experiment. Write \(\ket+=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Once a computational-basis measurement of \(\ket+\) gives zero, an immediate repeat gives zero again, assuming no intervening change. A freshly prepared \(\ket+\) is still equally likely to give either outcome. Always specify which preparation is being repeated.

4.3

Measurement in Other Bases

The measurement postulate refers to one basis, the configurations of the system. A qubit has many other orthonormal bases, and every state can be written in any of them. A unitary applied before a measurement measures the original state in another basis, so choosing the unitary means choosing the question. Can a well-chosen unitary also make two states easier to tell apart? No: unitaries preserve inner products, and with them every overlap between states.

4.3.1 Changes of basis§

Is the computational basis special? Consider

\[\ket+=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2},\qquad\ket-=\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

Both are unit vectors, and \(\braket+-=\tfrac12(1-1)=0\), so they form another orthonormal basis.

Every state can be written in this basis, and its coordinates are inner products: the coefficient of \(\ket+\) in \(\ket\psi\) is \(\braket+\psi\). For example, \(\braket+0=\braket-0=1/\sqrt2\), so

\[\ket0=\frac{\ket++\ket-}{\sqrt2}.\]

Has anything physical changed? No. Writing \(\ket0\) in the \(\pm\) basis changes its coordinates, not the state. Applying a gate does change the state. The two can look alike on paper, so it matters to say which one is meant.

The same holds for any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\): \(\ket\psi=\sum_j\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}\).

4.3.2 Measurement in an orthonormal basis§

Apply \(H\) to a qubit and then measure it. Which property of the original state does the outcome reveal?

\(H\) sends \(\ket+\) to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket-\) to \(\ket1\). Write the original state as \(a\ket++b\ket-\); after \(H\) it is \(a\ket0+b\ket1\). The measurement gives \(0\) with probability \(|a|^2=|\braket+\psi|^2\) and \(1\) with probability \(|\braket-\psi|^2\). “Apply \(H\), then measure” therefore measures the state in the \(\pm\) basis.

Does this work for any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\)? Let \(U\) be the unitary whose columns are the \(\ket{u_j}\), so that \(U\ket j=\ket{u_j}\). Its inverse \(U^\dagger\) sends \(\ket{u_j}\) to \(\ket j\). Applying \(U^\dagger\) and then measuring gives outcome \(j\) with probability

\[|\braket{j}{U^\dagger\psi}|^2=|\braket{U j}\psi|^2=|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2,\]

and applying \(U\) afterward turns the resulting state \(\ket j\) into \(\ket{u_j}\).

Measurement in a basis. Measuring \(\ket\psi\) in the orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\) gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\), and leaves the state \(\ket{u_j}\).

Geometrically, \(\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}\) is the projection of \(\ket\psi\) onto the direction \(\ket{u_j}\). With the projector \(P_j=\ket{u_j}\bra{u_j}\), the probability is \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\). More generally, a projective measurement is given by projectors \(P_j\) onto orthogonal subspaces with \(\sum_jP_j=I\): outcome \(j\) has probability \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\) and leaves the state \(P_j\ket\psi/\|P_j\ket\psi\|\). When a subspace has more than one dimension, the outcome identifies the subspace, not a single vector.

4.3.3 Overlaps under unitary evolution§

A unitary preserves more than total probability. It preserves every pairwise overlap:

\[\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\braket\phi{U^\dagger U\psi}=\braket\phi\psi.\]

It can rotate two states together, but cannot turn a nonorthogonal pair into an orthogonal pair. That will limit both discrimination and copying. A useful calculation has revealed a constraint on every possible unitary device.

Could we use any matrix and normalize its output afterwards? Generally not. The normalization factor would depend on the input, so the resulting map would no longer be linear. A conditional measurement update does involve normalization, but it is accompanied by a probabilistic outcome. It is not a deterministic gate disguised as a formula.

Unitary evolution describes the closed system. Measurement, discarded records, and environmental noise require a broader account. Keeping the scope clear prevents us from attributing all physical processes to one kind of map.

4.4

Observables and Distinguishability

So far a measurement has returned a label. A physical measurement usually reports a number, such as an energy, and we want an operator to describe the numbers just as projections describe the outcomes. The states that an operator only rescales, its eigenvectors, make the connection: an observable is an operator whose eigenspaces are the outcomes and whose eigenvalues are the numbers reported. We then return to the limit set by overlaps and ask how well any measurement can tell apart two states that are not orthogonal.

4.4.1 Eigenvectors and eigenspaces§

To attach numbers to measurement outcomes, we need the states that an operator only rescales. \(Z\) leaves \(\ket0\) alone and multiplies \(\ket1\) by \(-1\). A vector \(\ket v\neq0\) with \(A\ket v=\lambda\ket v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\): \(A\) keeps its direction and multiplies it by \(\lambda\).

For \(Z\), the vectors \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) are eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\). For \(X\), which exchanges \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\), we find \(X\ket+=\ket+\) and \(X\ket-=-\ket-\), with the same eigenvalues.

What can the eigenvalues of a unitary be? A unitary preserves length, so \(|\lambda|=1\). An eigenvector of a unitary only gains a phase.

If several independent eigenvectors share an eigenvalue, every combination of them is again an eigenvector with that eigenvalue. Together they form an eigenspace.

The outcome subspaces of a projective measurement will be the eigenspaces of the operator we are looking for.

4.4.2 Hermitian operators and spectral projections§

A measurement distinguishes outcomes; an observable also assigns them numbers. For orthogonal outcome projectors \(P_j\), choose real labels \(a_j\) and form

\[A=\sum_j a_jP_j.\]

This operator is Hermitian: \(A^\dagger=A\). Conversely, a Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenspaces. Its spectral projectors define a measurement, with probability \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\) for the reported eigenvalue \(a_j\).

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,5)\), we measure in the computational basis and report 2 or 5. Changing those labels changes the average reported number without changing which basis directions the apparatus distinguishes.

There is one qualification: if distinct projectors receive the same number, the operator groups their subspaces into one eigenspace. Recording only that number loses the finer outcome distinction. The standard spectral measurement of \(A\) refers to these grouped eigenspaces.

4.4.3 Nonorthogonal state discrimination§

Someone hands us one qubit, prepared either in \(\ket0\) or in \(\ket+\). Can we always tell which?

Suppose a measurement could. Some outcome would then occur with certainty for \(\ket0\) and never for \(\ket+\). By the measurement postulate, \(\ket0\) would lie in that outcome’s subspace and \(\ket+\) would be orthogonal to it, so \(\braket0+=0\). But \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\). No measurement identifies the two states with certainty.

Could a cleverer procedure help, such as an operation before measuring, or an interaction that writes a record into another system? Any such operation is unitary and preserves the overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), so the records cannot become orthogonal either.

The limit is structural, not a weakness of detectors. Two pure states can be distinguished perfectly exactly when they are orthogonal. A measurement can trade wrong answers for answers that report “inconclusive,” but it cannot always be right.
