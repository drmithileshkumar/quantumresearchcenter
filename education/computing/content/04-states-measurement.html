4.1

Vectors and Matrices

In Chapter 3, we represented the state of a system as a weighted sum of configuration kets, \(\sum_i v_i\ket{i}\), and described each computational step \(S\) by the weights \(\bra{j}S\ket{i}\) on its transition arrows. To find the amplitude arriving at any final configuration, we multiplied incoming amplitudes by arrow weights along each path and added the results across all paths meeting at the same destination. In this section, we keep those exact path calculations intact and recognize them as the operations of linear algebra: states become column vectors, transition amplitudes between states become inner products, and the arrow weights of a step become the entries of a transition matrix.

4.1.1 State vectors and ket notation§

At any stage of a quantum computation, each classical configuration \(i\) carries a complex amplitude \(v_i\), whose squared magnitude \(|v_i|^2\) gives the probability of observing configuration \(i\) upon measurement. Naming the complete state \(\ket{\psi}\) (read "ket psi"), we write it as a weighted sum of configuration kets:

\[\ket{\psi} = \sum_i v_i\ket{i}.\]

In Chapter 3, this sum served as a bookkeeping device for our path rules: contributions reaching the same configuration add their weights together, while contributions to distinct configurations remain separate. Those two bookkeeping rules—scaling weights and adding like terms—are precisely the axioms of a vector space. From now on, we treat \(\ket{\psi}\) not merely as a formal sum, but as a geometric object: a state vector.

For a system with two distinguishable configurations, \(0\) and \(1\), the state takes the form \(\ket{\psi} = v_0\ket{0} + v_1\ket{1}\). Such a weighted combination of configurations is called a superposition, and a two-state quantum system of this kind is called a qubit (short for quantum bit).

Once we fix a standard ordering for the configurations—placing \(0\) first and \(1\) second—we can list the amplitudes in a vertical column. Each configuration ket then corresponds to a column with a \(1\) in its own position and \(0\) elsewhere:

\[\ket{0} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{1} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_0\ket{0} + v_1\ket{1} = v_0\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} + v_1\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}v_0\\v_1\end{pmatrix}.\]

An ordered column of numbers like this is a vector, and its entries \(v_0, v_1\) are its coordinates. The reference kets \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\) form the computational basis.

Written as columns, our two path-bookkeeping rules become entrywise vector operations. Combining two sets of path contributions adds their matching coordinates, and scaling a state by a factor \(c\)—as happens when a transition step weights all arrows leaving configuration \(i\) by its incoming amplitude—multiplies every coordinate by \(c\):

\[\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}b_0\\b_1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a_0+b_0\\a_1+b_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad c\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}ca_0\\ca_1\end{pmatrix}.\]

These operations are vector addition and scalar multiplication (multiplication by a number). Notice that a column vector built from only a subset of paths holds only part of each final amplitude; the normalization condition \(\sum_i |v_i|^2 = 1\) applies to the complete state vector after all paths have been summed, not to individual path contributions.

In the real-amplitude example shown above, the probabilities of observing \(0\) and \(1\) are \((3/5)^2 = 9/25\) and \((4/5)^2 = 16/25\), which sum to \(1\). The two amplitudes \(3/5\) and \(4/5\) are coordinates of a single state vector, not two separate outcomes occurring simultaneously.

In column notation, the action of the Hadamard step, \(H\ket{0} = (\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\) and \(H\ket{1} = (\ket{0}-\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\), translates directly into a mapping of basis columns:

\[H:\ \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} \mapsto \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Each entry in the output column is the corresponding arrow weight \(\bra{j}H\ket{i}\), with the minus sign in the second column recording the negative transition weight from \(1\) to \(1\).

This coordinate representation extends naturally to registers of any size. For a two-qubit register with four configurations ordered lexicographically as \(00, 01, 10, 11\),

\[\frac{\ket{00}-\ket{11}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The column lists all four amplitudes in order—\(1/\sqrt{2}\) for \(00\), \(0\) for \(01\) and \(10\), and \(-1/\sqrt{2}\) for \(11\)—whereas Dirac's ket notation explicitly labels the nonzero configurations so we never have to count row positions by hand.

A state vector tells us the amplitude for reaching each basis configuration. How do we compute the amplitude to go from one general state vector to another?

4.1.2 Inner products and transition amplitudes§

Suppose a system is prepared in the state \(\ket{\psi} = \sum_i \psi_i\ket{i}\), where we now label the amplitudes \(\psi_i\) to match the name of the state (so a second state \(\ket{\phi}\) has amplitudes \(\phi_i\)). Just as we wrote \(\bra{j}S\ket{i}\) with the final configuration on the left and the initial configuration on the right, we write the amplitude to find the state \(\ket{\psi}\) in the basis configuration \(j\) as

\[\braket{j}{\psi} = \psi_j.\]

If the initial state is itself a single basis configuration \(\ket{i}\), its only nonzero coordinate is \(1\) at position \(i\), so

\[\braket{j}{i} = \begin{cases}1, & j=i,\\ 0, & j\ne i.\end{cases}\]

Notice that these are simply the weights \(\bra{j}I\ket{i}\) of the do-nothing identity step \(I\); when no step acts between the initial and final states, we omit the \(I\) and close the bracket into \(\braket{j}{i}\).

Now suppose the target is also a superposition, \(\ket{\phi} = \sum_j \phi_j\ket{j}\). What is the transition amplitude \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\) to go from the initial state \(\ket{\psi}\) to the final state \(\ket{\phi}\)? Every path from \(\ket{\psi}\) to \(\ket{\phi}\) passes through some basis configuration \(j\): it leaves \(\ket{\psi}\) into configuration \(j\) with amplitude \(\braket{j}{\psi}\), and then enters \(\ket{\phi}\) from \(j\) with amplitude \(\braket{\phi}{j}\). Multiplying along each path and summing over all intermediate configurations \(j\) gives

\[\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \sum_j \braket{\phi}{j}\,\braket{j}{\psi}.\]

This has the exact same structure as our two-step composition rule,

\[\bra{k}TS\ket{i} = \sum_j \bra{k}T\ket{j}\,\bra{j}S\ket{i},\]

with the step-free amplitudes \(\braket{j}{\psi}\) and \(\braket{\phi}{j}\) playing the roles of the first and second arrow weights.

What value should we assign to \(\braket{\phi}{j}\), the amplitude to enter \(\ket{\phi}\) from configuration \(j\)? The probability rule from Chapter 3 dictates our choice. Because the probability of configuration \(j\) in state \(\ket{\psi}\) is \(|\psi_j|^2 = \psi_j^*\psi_j = \braket{j}{\psi}^*\braket{j}{\psi}\), total probability normalization requires

\[\sum_j \braket{j}{\psi}^*\,\braket{j}{\psi} = 1.\]

Comparing this with the path sum for a state transitioning into itself, \(\braket{\psi}{\psi} = \sum_j \braket{\psi}{j}\,\braket{j}{\psi}\), we see that the two expressions match term by term provided the amplitude to enter a state from \(j\) is the complex conjugate of the amplitude to leave that state into \(j\):

\[\braket{\phi}{j} = \braket{j}{\phi}^* = \phi_j^*.\]

Adopting this definition guarantees that \(\braket{\psi}{\psi} = \sum_j |\psi_j|^2 = 1\) for every normalized state: any normalized state transitions into itself with amplitude \(1\). If we omitted the complex conjugate, the perfectly valid normalized state \((\ket{0}+i\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\) would yield \(\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{i^2}{2} = 0\), falsely suggesting zero overlap with itself! Substituting \(\braket{\phi}{j} = \phi_j^*\) into our path sum yields

\[\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \sum_j \phi_j^*\psi_j.\]

This sum is the inner product of the vectors \(\ket{\phi}\) and \(\ket{\psi}\) (read "bra phi ket psi"), and it defines the transition amplitude from initial state \(\ket{\psi}\) to final state \(\ket{\phi}\), \(\mathcal{A}(\text{final}\leftarrow\text{initial}) = \braket{\phi}{\psi}\). When both states are basis configurations, it reduces to \(\braket{j}{i}\). What \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\) means operationally when an experiment tests for a general state \(\ket{\phi}\) will become clear in Section 4.2 when we state the measurement postulate.

In matrix notation, the conjugated row vector associated with \(\ket{\phi}\) is called its bra, written \(\bra{\phi}\):

\[\ket{\phi} = \begin{pmatrix}\phi_0\\\phi_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bra{\phi} = \begin{pmatrix}\phi_0^* & \phi_1^*\end{pmatrix}.\]

The row \(\bra{\phi}\) lists the incoming amplitudes \(\braket{\phi}{j}\), while the column \(\ket{\psi}\) lists the outgoing amplitudes \(\braket{j}{\psi}\). Multiplying the row \(\bra{\phi}\) by the column \(\ket{\psi}\) carries out the sum over \(j\) and produces the single complex number \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\)—turning a "bra" and a "ket" into a "bra(c)ket."

What happens if we swap the initial and final states? Conjugating each term in the sum shows that reversing the direction of a transition conjugates its amplitude:

\[\braket{\psi}{\phi} = \sum_j \psi_j^*\phi_j = \left(\sum_j \phi_j^*\psi_j\right)^* = \bigl(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\bigr)^*.\]

This property is conjugate symmetry: exchanging initial and final states preserves the magnitude \(|\braket{\phi}{\psi}|\) while reversing the phase. For instance, with \(\ket{\psi} = (\ket{0}+i\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\), we have \(\braket{1}{\psi} = i/\sqrt{2}\), whereas \(\braket{\psi}{1} = -i/\sqrt{2}\). When all amplitudes are real, the inner product is symmetric and the order does not matter.

An inner product condenses two state vectors into a single complex number. How can we visualize what that number tells us about the geometric relationship between the two states?

4.1.3 Geometric view§

When amplitudes are real, a qubit state is an arrow in a two-dimensional plane, and the inner product has an immediate geometric meaning in terms of lengths and angles. Every algebraic feature we see in this plane carries straight over to complex state vectors.

First, the inner product of a vector with itself measures length. The norm (or length) of a vector \(\ket{\psi}\) is defined as \(\|\psi\| = \sqrt{\braket{\psi}{\psi}}\), which means that normalized quantum states (\(\braket{\psi}{\psi}=1\)) are unit vectors—vectors of length \(1\). Two vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular) when their inner product is zero, \(\braket{\phi}{\psi} = 0\), and a collection of mutually orthogonal unit vectors is called orthonormal. Our computational basis is orthonormal because \(\braket{j}{k}\) equals \(1\) when \(j=k\) and \(0\) when \(j\ne k\).

How large can the transition amplitude \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\) between two unit vectors be? We can decompose any unit vector \(\ket{\psi}\) into a component parallel to \(\ket{\phi}\) plus a remainder \(\ket{\psi_\perp}\) that is orthogonal to \(\ket{\phi}\):

\[\ket{\psi} = \braket{\phi}{\psi}\,\ket{\phi} + \ket{\psi_\perp},\qquad \braket{\phi}{\psi_\perp} = 0.\]

Taking the inner product of both sides with \(\bra{\phi}\) and using \(\braket{\phi}{\phi}=1\) confirms that when \(\ket{\psi_\perp}\) is orthogonal to \(\ket{\phi}\), the coefficient along \(\ket{\phi}\) must be \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\). Because the two parts meet at a right angle, their squared lengths obey the Pythagorean identity:

\[1 = \|\psi\|^2 = |\braket{\phi}{\psi}|^2 + \|\psi_\perp\|^2.\]

Since \(\|\psi_\perp\|^2 \ge 0\), the squared magnitude \(|\braket{\phi}{\psi}|^2\) always lies between \(0\) and \(1\). It equals \(1\) only when \(\ket{\psi_\perp}=0\) (meaning \(\ket{\psi}\) and \(\ket{\phi}\) differ at most by a global phase), and it equals \(0\) when \(\ket{\psi}\) and \(\ket{\phi}\) are orthogonal. For real unit vectors separated by an angle \(\theta\) in the plane, the inner product is simply \(\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \cos\theta\); for complex unit vectors, \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\) plays the exact same role as a complex-valued directional overlap.

Performing this orthogonal decomposition along all computational basis directions simultaneously recovers the coordinate expansion of \(\ket{\psi}\). Each coordinate \(\braket{j}{\psi}\) is the component of \(\ket{\psi}\) along the basis vector \(\ket{j}\), and because the basis vectors are mutually orthogonal, their squared lengths add up to the squared length of \(\ket{\psi}\):

\[\ket{\psi} = \sum_j \ket{j}\,\braket{j}{\psi},\qquad \braket{\psi}{\psi} = \sum_j |\braket{j}{\psi}|^2.\]

Geometrically, the outcome probabilities \(|\braket{j}{\psi}|^2\) are the squared lengths of the state vector's perpendicular projections onto the basis axes.

As a concrete example, take \(\ket{\phi} = \ket{0}\) and \(\ket{\psi} = (\sqrt{3}/2)\ket{0} + (1/2)\ket{1}\), which makes an angle of \(\theta = 30^\circ\) with \(\ket{0}\). The transition amplitude is \(\braket{0}{\psi} = \cos 30^\circ = \sqrt{3}/2\), whose square \(3/4\) is the probability of observing \(0\), while the orthogonal remainder \((1/2)\ket{1}\) has squared length \(1/4\).

4.1.4 Transition matrices§

Now that states are column vectors, how do we represent a computational step \(U\)? From Chapter 3, we know how a step acts on each basis configuration: it sends \(\ket{i}\) to the weighted sum of its successors, \(U\ket{i} = \sum_j \bra{j}U\ket{i}\,\ket{j}\), and it carries a superposition \(\ket{\psi} = \sum_i v_i\ket{i}\) to the corresponding weighted combination of those images. Geometrically, the operation \(U\) transforms each basis vector \(\ket{i}\) into a new vector \(U\ket{i}\) and recombines them with the original coordinates \(v_i\).

For a single qubit in the state \(\ket{\psi} = v_0\ket{0} + v_1\ket{1}\), this rule reads

\[U\ket{\psi} = v_0 U\ket{0} + v_1 U\ket{1}.\]

This fundamental property is linearity: applying an operation to a linear combination of input states produces the exact same linear combination of their individual outputs. For any two states \(\ket{\phi}, \ket{\psi}\) and any complex numbers \(a, b\),

\[U(a\ket{\phi} + b\ket{\psi}) = aU\ket{\phi} + bU\ket{\psi}.\]

Because a linear operation is completely determined by where it sends the basis vectors, we can package all of its transition weights into a rectangular array of numbers called a transition matrix (or simply a matrix). Its entry \(U_{ji}\) in row \(j\) and column \(i\) is the transition amplitude from initial configuration \(i\) to final configuration \(j\):

\[U_{ji} = \bra{j}U\ket{i}.\]

Notice how index order matches our right-to-left bracket notation: the column index \(i\) on the right names the starting configuration, and the row index \(j\) on the left names the destination configuration. Consequently, column \(i\) of the matrix \(U\) is simply the column vector \(U\ket{i}\) listing the outgoing arrow weights from configuration \(i\).

When \(U\) acts on a general input state \(\ket{\psi} = \sum_i v_i\ket{i}\), the amplitude \(v'_j\) arriving at configuration \(j\) is obtained by multiplying each incoming coordinate \(v_i\) by the arrow weight \(U_{ji}\) and summing over \(i\):

\[v'_j = \sum_i \bra{j}U\ket{i}\,v_i = \sum_i U_{ji}v_i,\qquad \ket{\psi'} = U\ket{\psi}.\]

Standard matrix–vector multiplication—taking the dot product of row \(j\) of \(U\) with the column vector \(\ket{\psi}\)—automatically performs the sum rule across all incoming arrows to configuration \(j\).

Not every matrix represents a valid physical step of a closed quantum system. To preserve the probability interpretation, the step must send every normalized input vector to a normalized output vector:

\[\sum_i |v_i|^2 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_j \left|\sum_i U_{ji}v_i\right|^2 = 1 \qquad\text{for every normalized input state}.\]

In Chapter 3, we saw that preserving total probability for all superpositions requires two conditions on the arrow weights leaving the basis configurations. First, outgoing normalization, \(\sum_j |\bra{j}U\ket{i}|^2 = 1\), means that each column of \(U\) is a unit vector. Second, the cross-sum condition, \(\sum_j \bigl(\bra{j}U\ket{i}\bigr)^*\bra{j}U\ket{k} = 0\) for \(i\ne k\), means that distinct columns of \(U\) are mutually orthogonal. Written in terms of matrix entries, both requirements combine into a single equation:

\[\sum_j U_{ji}^* U_{jk} = \delta_{ik},\]

where the Kronecker delta \(\delta_{ik}\) equals \(1\) when \(i=k\) and \(0\) when \(i\ne k\). A square matrix whose columns form an orthonormal set of vectors is called unitary. Every valid linear evolution of a closed quantum system must be represented by a unitary matrix.

To see a unitary matrix in action, consider the real transition matrix

\[U = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}4 & -3\\ 3 & 4\end{pmatrix}.\]

Its first column rotates \(\ket{0}\) to \((4/5)\ket{0} + (3/5)\ket{1}\), and its second column rotates \(\ket{1}\) to \(-(3/5)\ket{0} + (4/5)\ket{1}\). Both columns have length \(\sqrt{(4/5)^2 + (\pm 3/5)^2} = 1\), and their inner product is \((4/5)(-3/5) + (3/5)(4/5) = 0\), confirming that \(U\) is unitary. When \(U\) acts on \(\ket{\psi} = (3/5)\ket{0} + (4/5)\ket{1}\), the horizontal components \((3/5)(4/5)\) and \((4/5)(-3/5)\) cancel out while the vertical components \((3/5)(3/5) + (4/5)(4/5) = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1\) reinforce, rotating \(\ket{\psi}\) cleanly onto \(\ket{1}\).

The single-qubit operations we met in Chapter 3 also have simple transition matrices and geometric meanings. The bit-flip gate \(X\) (quantum NOT) swaps \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\); the phase-flip gate \(Z\) leaves \(\ket{0}\) unchanged and multiplies \(\ket{1}\) by \(-1\) (which we can also write compactly as the diagonal matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\)); and the Hadamard step \(H\) maps \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\) to the orthogonal diagonal states \((\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\) and \((\ket{0}-\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\):

\[X = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}.\]

After a step \(U\) transforms \(\ket{\psi}\) into \(U\ket{\psi}\), the transition amplitude into a final state \(\ket{\phi}\) is the inner product of \(\ket{\phi}\) with the transformed vector \(U\ket{\psi}\):

\[\mathcal{A}(\phi\leftarrow\psi; U) = \bra{\phi}U\ket{\psi} = \sum_{i,j} \braket{\phi}{j}\,\bra{j}U\ket{i}\,\braket{i}{\psi}.\]

In path terms, every route leaves \(\ket{\psi}\) through some configuration \(i\) with amplitude \(\braket{i}{\psi}\), crosses the transition arrow \(i\to j\) with weight \(\bra{j}U\ket{i}\), and enters \(\ket{\phi}\) from \(j\) with amplitude \(\braket{\phi}{j}\).

Finally, applying two steps in succession—first \(U\), then \(V\)—moves the state vector twice, producing \(V(U\ket{\psi}) = VU\ket{\psi}\). Written in matrix entries, our two-step sum rule across intermediate configurations \(j\),

\[\bra{k}VU\ket{i} = \sum_j \bra{k}V\ket{j}\,\bra{j}U\ket{i},\qquad (VU)_{ki} = \sum_j V_{kj}U_{ji},\]

is the exact definition of the matrix product \(VU\). Because vectors stand on the right, the rightmost matrix \(U\) acts first and \(V\) acts second. For instance, two consecutive Hadamard steps satisfy \(HH = I\), where \(I = \operatorname{diag}(1,1)\) is the identity matrix that leaves every state vector unchanged.

4.1.5 Reversibility of transitions§

Every quantum step we have examined so far is reversible, and several familiar gates—such as \(X\), CNOT, \(Z\), and \(H\)—happen to be their own inverses. What if a step does not undo itself? Is there a universal recipe that reverses any valid quantum transition?

For a deterministic permutation of configurations, the recipe is obvious: reverse the direction of every arrow. If a step sends configuration \(i\) to configuration \(j\), its inverse sends \(j\) back to \(i\). What happens if we try simply reversing the arrows of a quantum step with complex weights, such as the square root of NOT (\(\sqrt{X}\)), whose stay-weight is \(a = (1+i)/2\) and whose switch-weight is \(b = (1-i)/2\)? Because the stay-arrows and switch-arrows of \(\sqrt{X}\) already carry identical weights in both directions, reversing the arrows leaves the matrix unchanged—and applying \(\sqrt{X}\) twice gives \(\sqrt{X}\sqrt{X} = X\), flipping the bit instead of restoring it!

Why did simply reversing the arrows fail? Trace the two paths that start at \(0\) and return to \(0\) over two steps: staying at \(0\) twice contributes \(a\cdot a\), and switching to \(1\) and back contributes \(b\cdot b\). Their sum is

\[a^2 + b^2 = \frac{i}{2} - \frac{i}{2} = 0.\]

Because of their complex phases, the two return paths destructively interfere to zero when we need them to reinforce to \(1\)! To make the return paths add up to \(|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\), the second step must use the complex conjugates \(a^*\) and \(b^*\). With conjugated weights on the reversed arrows, the return paths contribute \(a^*a + b^*b = |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\), while the paths that switch from \(0\) to \(1\) cancel out:

\[b^*a + a^*b = \frac{i}{2} - \frac{i}{2} = 0.\]

Reversing every arrow and taking the complex conjugate of its weight completely undoes \(\sqrt{X}\).

This general operation is called the adjoint (or conjugate transpose) of \(U\), denoted \(U^\dagger\) (read "U dagger"). The arrow weight of \(U^\dagger\) from \(j\) to \(i\) is the complex conjugate of the arrow weight of \(U\) from \(i\) to \(j\):

\[\bra{i}U^\dagger\ket{j} = \bigl(\bra{j}U\ket{i}\bigr)^*,\qquad (U^\dagger)_{ij} = U_{ji}^*.\]

Notice that this is the exact same backward-conjugation rule we found for inner products (\(\braket{\phi}{j} = \braket{j}{\phi}^*\)), now applied to the transition arrows of a step. In matrix form, we obtain \(U^\dagger\) by transposing \(U\) (turning column \(i\) into row \(i\)) and conjugating every entry.

Does \(U^\dagger\) undo every unitary matrix \(U\)? Let us apply \(U\) followed by \(U^\dagger\) and compute the matrix entry from configuration \(k\) to configuration \(i\) using the sum rule:

\[\bra{i}U^\dagger U\ket{k} = \sum_j \bra{i}U^\dagger\ket{j}\,\bra{j}U\ket{k} = \sum_j \bigl(\bra{j}U\ket{i}\bigr)^*\bra{j}U\ket{k} = \sum_j U_{ji}^* U_{jk}.\]

Look at the right-hand side: it is the exact inner product between column \(i\) and column \(k\) of \(U\) that defines a unitary matrix! When \(i=k\), outgoing normalization makes the sum equal to \(1\); when \(i\ne k\), column orthogonality makes the sum equal to \(0\). Therefore, a matrix \(U\) is unitary if and only if its adjoint undoes it:

\[U^\dagger U = I.\]

Because a square matrix whose left inverse is \(U^\dagger\) also satisfies \(UU^\dagger = I\), every unitary operation is automatically reversible, with its inverse given immediately by its adjoint, \(U^{-1} = U^\dagger\). When a unitary matrix equals its own adjoint (\(U^\dagger = U\), as is true for \(X\), \(Z\), and \(H\)), applying the gate a second time undoes the first.

Geometrically, what does a unitary transformation do to state space? For any matrix \(A\), moving a vector \(\ket{\psi}\) forward with \(A\) and taking its inner product with \(\ket{\phi}\) gives the same result as moving \(\ket{\phi}\) backward with \(A^\dagger\) and taking its inner product with \(\ket{\psi}\):

\[\bra{\phi}A\ket{\psi} = \braket{A^\dagger\phi}{\psi}.\]

When \(U\) is unitary, we can apply this identity to two states \(\ket{\chi}\) and \(\ket{\psi}\) that both evolve under \(U\): since \(U^\dagger U = I\), their inner product after the step is \(\braket{U\chi}{U\psi} = \bra{\chi}U^\dagger U\ket{\psi} = \braket{\chi}{\psi}\). That is, a unitary step preserves the inner product between every pair of state vectors—keeping all vector lengths and all angles (and phases) between states unchanged. It moves the entire state space rigidly, like a rotation or reflection, which is why it can always be reversed without loss of information.

By contrast, while the adjoint \(A^\dagger\) is defined for every matrix \(A\), it does not equal \(A^{-1}\) when \(A\) is not unitary. Consider the non-unitary horizontal shear

\[A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Here \(A\) slides points horizontally in proportion to their vertical coordinate, whereas its adjoint \(A^\dagger\) (which is simply the transpose since the entries are real) slides points vertically in proportion to their horizontal coordinate. Acting on the unit vector \(\ket{\psi} = \tfrac{3}{5}\ket{0} + \tfrac{4}{5}\ket{1}\), both \(A\) and \(A^\dagger\) distort its length and tilt it in different directions.

Consequently, applying \(A\) followed by \(A^\dagger\)—or \(A^\dagger\) followed by \(A\)—does not return \(\ket{\psi}\) to its starting state:

To undo the horizontal shear \(A\), one must slide horizontally in the opposite direction using \(A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}\), which is not \(A^\dagger\). The adjoint \(A^\dagger\) always shifts an operator from one side of an inner product to the other (\(\bra{\phi}A\ket{\psi} = \braket{A^\dagger\phi}{\psi}\)), but it reverses the operation if and only if the operation preserves lengths and inner products—that is, when the matrix is unitary.

4.2

Postulates

Up to this point, our rules for vectors, inner products, and unitary matrices have grown out of path bookkeeping. How does this mathematical apparatus connect to physical systems in the laboratory? Four fundamental questions must be settled: which physical preparations are represented by state vectors, how an isolated system evolves in time, what happens when a system is observed (including the operational meaning of \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\) when \(\ket{\phi}\) is a superposition), and how multiple subsystems combine into a single composite system. The postulates of quantum mechanics answer these questions. Unlike algebraic identities, each postulate is a physical law—grounded in experiment rather than derived from arithmetic.

4.2.1 State and evolution postulates§

Do distinct unit vectors always describe physically distinguishable preparations? Not quite. As we saw in Section 3.4, multiplying every coordinate of a state vector by a common phase factor \(e^{i\theta}\) leaves all outcome probabilities unchanged—both immediately and after any sequence of subsequent unitary steps, because by linearity every path sum simply factors out the same \(e^{i\theta}\). Two unit vectors that differ only by an overall global phase cannot be distinguished by any physical experiment, so they represent the exact same physical state.

State postulate. An isolated state of a finite-dimensional quantum system is completely described by a unit vector of complex amplitudes in a vector space equipped with the inner product \(\braket{\phi}{\psi} = \sum_j \phi_j^*\psi_j\). Unit vectors that differ only by a global phase \(e^{i\theta}\) represent the same physical state.

How many independent real parameters are left to specify the state of a single qubit, \(\ket{\psi} = v_0\ket{0} + v_1\ket{1}\)? Normalization (\(|v_0|^2 + |v_1|^2 = 1\)) constrains the two magnitudes so that we can write \(|v_0| = \cos(\theta/2)\) and \(|v_1| = \sin(\theta/2)\) for an angle \(0 \le \theta \le \pi\). Factoring out a global phase allows us to make the first nonzero coordinate real and nonnegative, leaving only a single relative phase \(\phi\) on the second coordinate:

\[\ket{\psi} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{0} + e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\ket{1},\qquad 0 \le \theta \le \pi.\]

When \(\theta = 0\) (\(\ket{\psi}=\ket{0}\)) or \(\theta = \pi\) (\(\ket{\psi}=\ket{1}\) up to a global phase), one of the coordinates vanishes and the relative phase \(\phi\) becomes irrelevant.

How does a quantum state change when the system is isolated from outside disturbance? In Section 4.1, we proved that any linear step preserving total probability across all superpositions must be represented by a unitary matrix—an operation that moves state space rigidly and is undone by its adjoint. Our second postulate asserts that nature's closed-system dynamics obey this exact rule.

Evolution postulate. Over any time step during which a quantum system remains closed (isolated from measurement or external entanglement), its state vector evolves linearly via a unitary operator \(U\):

\[\ket{\psi} \longmapsto U\ket{\psi},\qquad U^\dagger U = I.\]

When the amplitudes are real, we can picture unitary evolution as a rigid rotation (or reflection) in the state plane. Every normalized state vector has its tip on the unit circle, and a rotation matrix

\[U(\delta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta & -\sin\delta\\ \sin\delta & \cos\delta\end{pmatrix}\]

turns every state vector around the origin by the same angle \(\delta\), preserving the unit length of each vector and the angle between any pair of vectors:

In a general complex state space, the geometric invariant preserved by unitary evolution is the complex inner product itself, \(\braket{U\chi}{U\psi} = \braket{\chi}{\psi}\). Keep in mind that these vectors live in an abstract state space of complex amplitudes, not in ordinary three-dimensional physical space.

4.2.2 The measurement postulate§

While unitary evolution carries all amplitude components forward reversibly, an observation—a measurement—behaves very differently: it yields a single definite classical outcome and alters the state of the system. Which outcome appears, with what probability, and what state remains afterward?

Measurement postulate. Given a system in the normalized state \(\ket{\psi} = \sum_j v_j\ket{j}\), an ideal measurement in the computational basis yields a single outcome \(j\) with probability \(p(j) = |\braket{j}{\psi}|^2 = |v_j|^2\) (the Born rule). Conditional on observing outcome \(j\), the state of the system immediately becomes \(\ket{j}\).

The probability formula \(|v_j|^2\) is the rule we have used since Chapter 3; the measurement postulate adds the post-measurement state update (often called state collapse). Both parts are physical assumptions. For instance, measuring \(\ket{\psi} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\ket{0} + \frac{1}{2}\ket{1}\) in the computational basis yields \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\). If a run produces the outcome \(1\), the post-measurement state is \(\ket{1}\), so repeating the exact same measurement immediately afterward is guaranteed to yield \(1\) again with probability \(1\).

Geometrically, measurement acts as an orthogonal projection followed by renormalization. Recall from Section 4.1.3 that any state vector can be resolved into perpendicular components along the basis axes, \(\ket{\psi} = \sum_j \ket{j}\,\braket{j}{\psi}\). Measurement selects one axis \(j\) with probability equal to the squared length of that component, discards all other orthogonal components, and rescales the surviving component back to unit length. The linear operator that extracts the component of a vector along \(\ket{j}\) is the projector \(P_j = \ketbra{j}{j}\), defined so that \(P_j\ket{\psi} = \ket{j}\braket{j}{\psi}\): the ket–bra outer product takes the inner product of the input state with \(\bra{j}\) and multiplies the vector \(\ket{j}\) by that scalar. In matrix form, \(\ketbra{j}{j}\) is a column vector multiplied by a row vector—for example, \(\ketbra{0}{0} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\). Using projectors, the Born rule and post-measurement state take the compact form

\[p(j) = \|P_j\ket{\psi}\|^2,\qquad \ket{\psi_j} = \frac{P_j\ket{\psi}}{\sqrt{p(j)}}\quad\bigl(p(j) > 0\bigr).\]

Predict before measuring

Prepare \(\ket{+} = (\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\). Measuring in the computational basis gives two equally likely outcomes. Must every measurement of this state be uncertain?

Compare your prediction

No. A measurement in a basis containing \(\ket{+}\) gives its corresponding outcome with certainty. The preparation is unchanged; the measurement asks a different question.

Is there anything physically privileged about the computational basis \(\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}\)? Not at all: we can measure a quantum system in any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{b_j}\}\). If a unitary operation \(U\) rotates the orthonormal basis vectors \(\ket{b_j}\) into the computational basis vectors, \(U\ket{b_j} = \ket{j}\) (so that \(\ket{b_j} = U^\dagger\ket{j}\)), we can perform a measurement in the \(\{\ket{b_j}\}\) basis by applying \(U\) and then measuring in the computational basis. By the Born rule and the adjoint identity, the probability of obtaining outcome \(j\) is

\[|\bra{j}U\ket{\psi}|^2 = |\braket{U^\dagger j}{\psi}|^2 = |\braket{b_j}{\psi}|^2.\]

This gives the concrete operational meaning of the transition amplitude \(\braket{\phi}{\psi}\) promised in Section 4.1.2: if we measure a system prepared in state \(\ket{\psi}\) using any orthonormal basis that includes the unit vector \(\ket{\phi}\), we observe the outcome corresponding to \(\ket{\phi}\) with probability \(|\braket{\phi}{\psi}|^2\), and conditional on that outcome the state collapses to \(\ket{\phi}\).

To visualize basis choice in a real state-vector plane, consider the state \(\ket{\psi} = \cos\alpha\ket{0} + \sin\alpha\ket{1}\) measured in an orthonormal basis rotated by an angle \(\beta\):

\[\ket{b_0} = \cos\beta\ket{0} + \sin\beta\ket{1},\qquad \ket{b_1} = -\sin\beta\ket{0} + \cos\beta\ket{1}.\]

The transition amplitudes are \(\braket{b_0}{\psi} = \cos(\alpha-\beta)\) and \(\braket{b_1}{\psi} = \sin(\alpha-\beta)\), so the outcome probabilities are \(\cos^2(\alpha-\beta)\) and \(\sin^2(\alpha-\beta)\). Choosing \(\beta = 0^\circ\) gives the computational (or \(Z\)) measurement along \(\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}\). Choosing \(\beta = 45^\circ\) gives the diagonal (or \(X\)) measurement along \(\ket{+} = (\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\) and \(\ket{-} = (\ket{0}-\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\) (where the overall minus sign on \(\ket{b_1} = -\ket{-}\) leaves the projector \(\ketbra{-}{-}\) and outcome probability unchanged). If we prepare \(\ket{+}\) (\(\alpha = 45^\circ\)) and measure in the \(X\) basis (\(\beta = 45^\circ\)), we obtain outcome \(+\) with probability \(\cos^2(0^\circ) = 1\) and leave \(\ket{+}\) undisturbed; if we measure that exact same state \(\ket{+}\) in the \(Z\) basis (\(\beta = 0^\circ\)), we obtain \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(\cos^2(45^\circ) = 1/2\) each. Changing the measurement basis changes the question we ask of the system, not the state that was prepared.

A complete quantum run interleaves these two distinct modes of change: smooth, reversible unitary evolution between observations, punctuated by irreversible projective updates whenever a measurement is performed.

This explains cleanly why observing a qubit mid-flight between two Hadamard steps destroys interference. Starting from \(\ket{0}\), the first Hadamard produces \(\ket{+} = (\ket{0}+\ket{1})/\sqrt{2}\). An intermediate \(Z\)-basis measurement projects the state onto either \(\ket{0}\) or \(\ket{1}\) with probability \(1/2\) each, discarding the other branch so that the second Hadamard produces \(0\) and \(1\) with equal probability \(1/2\)—whereas without the intermediate measurement, the second Hadamard rotates \(\ket{+}\) deterministically back to \(\ket{0}\).

Unlike unitary evolution, a projective measurement update is fundamentally irreversible on the measured system alone: if a \(Z\)-basis measurement reports outcome \(0\), both an initial \(\ket{0}\) and an initial \(\ket{+}\) leave the exact same post-measurement state \(\ket{0}\), erasing all trace of the prior superposition. (As we saw in Section 3.4.3, if we model the measuring apparatus itself as a second quantum register that records the path via a unitary gate like CNOT, the combined system evolves unitarily, and the conditional state of the first register on each recorded branch matches the measurement postulate.)

4.2.3 The composite-state postulate§

A single qubit is described by two complex amplitudes. How many amplitudes are needed to describe a pair of qubits, \(A\) and \(B\)? It might be tempting to keep two separate two-component vectors—one for qubit \(A\) and one for qubit \(B\)—comprising four numbers in total. We already saw in Section 3.4.3 why separate lists are not enough: a classical configuration of the combined system specifies a definite bit value for each qubit simultaneously. Two qubits have four joint basis configurations—\(00, 01, 10, 11\), where the first bit labels qubit \(A\) and the second labels qubit \(B\)—and a general quantum state of the pair assigns an independent complex amplitude to each of the four joint configurations:

\[\ket{\Psi} = c_{00}\ket{00} + c_{01}\ket{01} + c_{10}\ket{10} + c_{11}\ket{11},\qquad \sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}} |c_{ij}|^2 = 1.\]

Notice how rapidly the dimension of the state space grows with the number of qubits. Writing down separate two-component vectors for \(n\) qubits would require only \(2n\) amplitudes, whereas specifying an amplitude for every joint bit string in \(\{0,1\}^n\) requires \(2^n\) amplitudes.

Under what circumstances do two separate state vectors suffice to describe the pair? Suppose qubit \(A\) is prepared independently in the state \(\ket{\psi} = a\ket{0} + b\ket{1}\) and qubit \(B\) is prepared independently in the state \(\ket{\phi} = c\ket{0} + d\ket{1}\). By the product rule for independent steps, the amplitude to find \(A\) in configuration \(i\) and \(B\) in configuration \(j\) is the product of their individual amplitudes—for instance, the amplitude of \(\ket{01}\) is \(ad\). Forming all four pairwise products constructs the four-component joint state vector from the two two-component vectors:

\[\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket{i}\otimes\ket{j} = \ket{ij}.\]

This operation is called the tensor product (read "tensor"). Because each joint coordinate multiplies an amplitude of \(A\) by an amplitude of \(B\), the joint probability of observing \(01\) is \(|ad|^2 = |a|^2|d|^2\)—the product of the individual probabilities, exactly as statistical independence demands. Any composite state that can be written as a single tensor product \(\ket{\psi}\otimes\ket{\phi}\) is called a product state.

Our final postulate states that the state space of a composite system contains not only product states, but all normalized superpositions of the joint basis configurations.

Composite-state postulate. The state space of a composite quantum system is the tensor product of the state spaces of its component subsystems. If the subsystems have orthonormal bases \(\{\ket{i}\}\) and \(\{\ket{j}\}\), the composite system has the orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{ij} = \ket{i}\otimes\ket{j}\}\), and every normalized vector in this tensor-product space is a valid physical state.

When subsystems of dimensions \(m\) and \(n\) are combined, their state-space dimensions multiply to \(mn\). Crucially, because any normalized linear combination of the \(mn\) basis vectors is allowed, most states in the composite space cannot be factored into a tensor product of individual subsystem states.

For a simple example, consider the two-qubit state \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}\), which we generated using a Hadamard and a CNOT in Section 3.4.3. Could this state be written as a product \((a\ket{0}+b\ket{1})\otimes(c\ket{0}+d\ket{1})\)? Matching coordinates would require \(ac = 1/\sqrt{2}\) and \(bd = 1/\sqrt{2}\), which forces all four numbers \(a, b, c, d\) to be nonzero—yet that makes it impossible for the cross terms \(ad = 0\) (for \(\ket{01}\)) and \(bc = 0\) (for \(\ket{10}\)) to vanish! A composite state vector that cannot be factored into a product state is called entangled, and this non-factorizability is called entanglement.

What does entanglement signify physically? If we measure both qubits of \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}\) in the computational basis, we obtain \(00\) with probability \(1/2\) and \(11\) with probability \(1/2\)—the two bits always agree. Of course, flipping a classical fair coin and writing down either \(00\) or \(11\) produces the exact same \(50/50\) correlated statistics in the computational basis, so correlation in a single basis does not by itself prove entanglement. The deeper difference lies in the coherent superposition of the joint state vector: neither qubit possesses an individual state vector of its own, yet the pair as a whole occupies a single definite unit vector in the four-dimensional state space. In Chapter 5, we will assemble our four postulates into the formal model of quantum computation, and in Chapter 6 we will explore multi-qubit gates and measurements that distinguish quantum entanglement from any possible classical correlation.
