4.1

Vectors and Matrices

In our transition calculations, a state was a weighted sum of configuration kets, and a step \(S\) carried a weight \(\bra jS\ket i\) on each arrow. To find the amplitude at an endpoint, we multiplied the incoming amplitudes by these weights and added the contributions. We keep this calculation intact and give it the structure of vectors and matrices: states become vectors, and the arrow weights of a step become the entries of a matrix.

4.1.1 State vectors and ket notation§

At any step of a computation, each configuration \(i\) carries a complex amplitude \(v_i\), the amplitude for the computation to be in configuration \(i\); its squared magnitude \(|v_i|^2\) is the probability of that configuration. We have written the state as the weighted sum of configuration kets. Naming the whole state \(\ket\psi\),

\[\ket\psi=\sum_iv_i\ket i.\]

So far the sum has been bookkeeping: terms for the same configuration add their weights, and terms for different configurations stay apart. These are exactly the rules of vector arithmetic, and from now on we treat \(\ket\psi\) as a vector.

For one bit, \(\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\). The weighted sum is a superposition, \(\ket\psi\) is a state vector, and a quantum system with two distinguishable configurations is a qubit.

Fix an order for the configurations, \(0\) first, and list the amplitudes in that order. The state becomes a column, and each configuration ket becomes a column with a single one:

\[\ket0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1=v_0\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}+v_1\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_0\\v_1\end{pmatrix}.\]

An ordered list like this is a vector, and its entries are its coordinates. The kets \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) form the computational basis.

In columns, the two bookkeeping rules become entrywise operations. Adding two weighted sums adds matching coordinates, and multiplying a sum by a number, as a step does when it weights the successors of configuration \(i\) by \(v_i\), multiplies every coordinate:

\[\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}b_0\\b_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a_0+b_0\\a_1+b_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad c\begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ca_0\\ca_1\end{pmatrix}.\]

These are vector addition and multiplication by a number. A column built from only some of the paths holds part of each final amplitude, so normalization applies to the complete state, not to its parts.

For the vector drawn above, the probabilities of the two configurations are \(9/25\) and \(16/25\), which add to one. The amplitudes are coordinates of one description, not two outcomes obtained at once.

The Hadamard step, \(H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(H\ket1=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\), becomes a statement about columns:

\[H:\ \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Each coordinate of an image is an arrow weight \(\bra jH\ket i\); the minus sign is the negative arrow from \(1\) to \(1\).

Nothing here is limited to two configurations. With the four configurations \(00,01,10,11\), taken in that order,

\[\frac{\ket{00}-\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The column lists the amplitudes in that order: \(1/\sqrt2\) for \(00\), zero for \(01\) and \(10\), and \(-1/\sqrt2\) for \(11\). The ket form names each configuration, so we need not remember its position in the column.

A state gives the amplitude for each configuration. What is the amplitude to go from one state to another?

4.1.2 Inner products and transition amplitudes§

A state \(\ket\psi=\sum_i\psi_i\ket i\) gives the amplitude \(\psi_j\) for configuration \(j\). From here on we name the amplitudes after their state, so that a second state \(\ket\phi\) has amplitudes \(\phi_j\). As with arrow weights, we write the amplitude to go from the initial state \(\ket\psi\) to the final configuration \(j\) with the final one on the left:

\[\braket j\psi=\psi_j.\]

When the initial state is itself a configuration \(\ket i\), its only nonzero amplitude is a one at \(i\), so

\[\braket ji=\begin{cases}1,&j=i,\\0,&j\ne i.\end{cases}\]

These are the weights \(\bra jI\ket i\) of the identity step: with no step acting between the two configurations, we leave out the \(I\).

Now let the end be a state too, \(\ket\phi=\sum_j\phi_j\ket j\). What is the amplitude to go from \(\ket\psi\) to \(\ket\phi\)? A path passes through one configuration \(j\): it reaches \(j\) with amplitude \(\braket j\psi\), then continues into \(\ket\phi\) with amplitude \(\braket\phi j\). The product rule multiplies along each path, and the sum rule adds the paths:

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\braket\phi j\,\braket j\psi.\]

This has the shape of the sum rule

\[\bra kTS\ket i=\sum_j\bra kT\ket j\,\bra jS\ket i,\]

with the step-free amplitudes \(\braket j\psi\) and \(\braket\phi j\) in place of arrow weights.

What is \(\braket\phi j\), the amplitude to go from configuration \(j\) into \(\ket\phi\)? A normalized state must arrive at itself with amplitude one: \(\braket\phi\phi=\sum_j\braket\phi j\,\phi_j=1\) whenever \(\sum_j|\phi_j|^2=1\). Taking \(\braket\phi j=\phi_j\) works for real amplitudes but fails for \((\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), where it gives \(\tfrac12+\tfrac{i^2}2=0\). The conjugate \(\braket\phi j=\phi_j^*\) works for every state, because \(\sum_j\phi_j^*\phi_j=\sum_j|\phi_j|^2\), and for a final configuration it gives back the values \(1\) and \(0\) above. Therefore

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j.\]

This sum is the inner product of the two vectors, and it gives the transition amplitude from \(\ket\psi\) to \(\ket\phi\), written \(\mathcal A(\text{final}\leftarrow\text{initial})=\braket\phi\psi\). When both states are configurations, it is \(\braket ji\) again. For the same state on both sides, \(\braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\psi_j|^2\), so normalization reads \(\braket\psi\psi=1\).

The conjugated row belonging to \(\ket\phi\) is called its bra:

\[\ket\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_0\\\phi_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bra\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_0^*&\phi_1^*\end{pmatrix}.\]

Its entries are the amplitudes \(\braket\phi j\) into the final state, and the column \(\ket\psi\) holds the amplitudes \(\braket j\psi\) out of the initial one. Multiplying the row by the column performs the path sum and gives \(\braket\phi\psi\).

What happens if we exchange the initial and final states? The conjugate moves to the other factor in each product, so the amplitude is conjugated:

\[\braket\psi\phi=\sum_j\psi_j^*\phi_j=\left(\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j\right)^*=\bigl(\braket\phi\psi\bigr)^*.\]

This is conjugate symmetry. The magnitude is unchanged; only the phase reverses. For \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), \(\braket1\psi=i/\sqrt2\), while \(\braket\psi1=-i/\sqrt2\). For real vectors the order does not matter.

An inner product turns two state vectors into one complex number. What does that number say about the vectors themselves?

4.1.3 Geometric view§

With real amplitudes, a qubit state is an arrow in a plane, and the inner product can be read off a picture of two arrows. What we find there carries over to complex amplitudes.

The first thing the inner product measures is length. The norm, or length, of a vector is \(\|\psi\|=\sqrt{\braket\psi\psi}\), so normalized states are unit vectors. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero, and a set of mutually orthogonal unit vectors is orthonormal. The computational basis is orthonormal: \(\braket jk\) is one when \(j=k\) and zero otherwise.

How large can a transition amplitude between unit vectors be? Split \(\ket\psi\) into a part along \(\ket\phi\) and a part orthogonal to it:

\[\ket\psi=\braket\phi\psi\,\ket\phi+\ket{\psi_\perp},\qquad \braket\phi{\psi_\perp}=0.\]

Taking the inner product of both sides with \(\ket\phi\) confirms that the coefficient of the first part is \(\braket\phi\psi\). Because the two parts are orthogonal, their squared lengths add up to the squared length of \(\ket\psi\):

\[1=|\braket\phi\psi|^2+\|\psi_\perp\|^2.\]

So \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\) lies between zero and one. It is one when the two vectors differ only by a global phase, and zero when they are orthogonal. For real unit vectors in a plane, \(\braket\phi\psi=\cos\theta\), where \(\theta\) is the angle between them; complex vectors extend this geometry while keeping phase in the inner product.

The same split, done along every basis direction at once, gives the coordinates. Each \(\braket j\psi\) is the component of \(\ket\psi\) along \(\ket j\). Because the basis directions are orthonormal, these components rebuild the vector, and their squared lengths add up to its squared length:

\[\ket\psi=\sum_j\ket j\,\braket j\psi,\qquad \braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\braket j\psi|^2.\]

The probabilities of the configurations are the squared lengths of these perpendicular components.

For example, with \(\ket\phi=\ket0\) and \(\ket\psi=(\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\), the transition amplitude is \(\braket0\psi=\sqrt3/2\), and its squared magnitude \(3/4\) is the probability of configuration \(0\). The remaining part, \((1/2)\ket1\), is orthogonal to \(\ket0\).

4.1.4 Transition matrices§

We already know how a step acts on a state: each configuration goes to the weighted sum of its successors, \(U\ket i=\sum_j\bra jU\ket i\,\ket j\), and a state goes to the same combination of these images. Geometrically, the operation moves each basis direction and combines the moved components to give the new vector.

For a qubit state \(\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\), this means

\[U\ket\psi=v_0U\ket0+v_1U\ket1.\]

The coefficients weight the images of the original basis vectors. Their sum is the output state, whose coordinates in the fixed computational basis will generally differ from the input coordinates. This is linearity: an operation carries a sum of input contributions to the same sum of their outputs. More generally,

\[U(a\ket\phi+b\ket\psi)=aU\ket\phi+bU\ket\psi.\]

A transition matrix records the amplitudes for this motion between basis configurations. Its entry \(U_{ji}\) is the amplitude to arrive at \(j\) from \(i\):

\[U_{ji}=\bra jU\ket i.\]

This is the arrow weight we have used since our first transition diagrams, now placed in a table.

We use column \(i\) for the initial configuration and row \(j\) for the final one, so column \(i\) lists the coefficients of \(U\ket i\). The matrix is thus a coordinate description of the same transition graph.

For a general input, the amplitude arriving at \(j\) is found by carrying each \(v_i\) along the arrow \(i\to j\) and adding:

\[v'_j=\sum_i\bra jU\ket i\,v_i=\sum_iU_{ji}v_i,\qquad \ket{\psi'}=U\ket\psi.\]

Matrix–vector multiplication performs these path sums. The resulting coordinates describe where the state vector has moved.

We want to allow only operations that preserve the probability interpretation of the amplitudes. For a closed system evolving within the same state space, every normalized input must therefore give a normalized output: the probabilities of all possible configurations must still add to one.

\[\sum_i|v_i|^2=1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_j\left|\sum_iU_{ji}v_i\right|^2=1\qquad\text{for every input state}.\]

We have met this requirement before, as two conditions on the arrow weights of a step. Outgoing normalization, \(\sum_j|\bra jU\ket i|^2=1\), says that each column has unit length. The cross-sum condition, \(\sum_j\bigl(\bra jU\ket i\bigr)^*\bra jU\ket k=0\) for \(i\ne k\), says that distinct columns are orthogonal. Together,

\[\sum_j U_{ji}^*U_{jk}=\delta_{ik},\]

where \(\delta_{ik}\) is one when \(i=k\) and zero otherwise. A square matrix with these orthonormal columns is called unitary. Linear operations on a closed system must be unitary to preserve the probability interpretation for every state.

For example, consider the real transition

\[U=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}4&-3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}.\]

It rotates \(\ket0\) to \((4/5)\ket0+(3/5)\ket1\) and \(\ket1\) to \(-(3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\). The two directions remain perpendicular and retain their lengths. For \(\ket\psi=(3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\), the weighted images have horizontal components that cancel and vertical components that add to one. The vector therefore moves to \(\ket1\).

The familiar gates also have simple geometric actions. \(X\), or NOT, exchanges the two basis directions. \(Z\) changes the sign of the component along \(\ket1\). \(H\) sends the basis vectors to the two perpendicular diagonal directions \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \((\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Their transition matrices record these actions:

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Here \(Z\) can also be written \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\). The drawings use real vectors; the same linear description applies when amplitudes and transition weights are complex.

After the operation, comparison with a final state \(\ket\phi\) uses the inner product:

\[\mathcal A(\phi\leftarrow\psi;U)=\bra\phi U\ket\psi=\sum_{i,j}\braket\phi j\,\bra jU\ket i\,\braket i\psi.\]

The operation moves the initial vector, and the final bra selects its component along the final direction. In path terms, every route enters some configuration \(i\), crosses the arrow to some \(j\), and continues into \(\ket\phi\). For definite initial and final configurations, this is the arrow weight \(U_{ji}\).

Two successive operations move the vector twice. If \(U\) acts first and \(V\) second, the final state is \(VU\ket\psi\). The amplitude between two configurations is our two-step sum rule, read in matrix entries:

\[\bra kVU\ket i=\sum_j\bra kV\ket j\,\bra jU\ket i,\qquad (VU)_{ki}=\sum_jV_{kj}U_{ji}.\]

This defines the matrix product. The rightmost matrix acts first. For example, two Hadamard steps restore every input vector, so \(HH=I\), where \(I\) is the identity matrix.

4.1.5 Reversibility of transitions§

A transition matrix \(U\) carries an initial state \(\ket\psi\) to \(U\ket\psi\). To reverse this change, we need an operation that restores every initial state, including every superposition. An inverse \(U^{-1}\), when it exists, does exactly this: \(U^{-1}U\ket\psi=\ket\psi\).

For unitary quantum transitions, we can construct this inverse by reversing each transition arrow and conjugating its amplitude. The resulting matrix is called the adjoint, written \(U^\dagger\). We will see why it undoes a unitary operation; for a general matrix, the adjoint need not be the inverse.

To define the adjoint precisely, consider the overlap of \(U\ket\psi\) with a final direction \(\ket\phi\). The same overlap can be calculated by leaving the initial vector fixed and applying \(U^\dagger\) to the final vector:

\[\bra\phi U\ket\psi=\braket{U^\dagger\phi}\psi.\]

On the left, \(U\) acts on the initial vector \(\ket\psi\). On the right, \(U^\dagger\) acts on the comparison vector \(\ket\phi\). The inner product has the same value in both descriptions. The transformed comparison vector need not be normalized when \(U\) is a general matrix.

The reversal property of the inner product gives the corresponding relation between basis transitions:

\[\bra iU^\dagger\ket j=\bigl(\bra jU\ket i\bigr)^*,\qquad (U^\dagger)_{ij}=U_{ji}^*.\]

In a transition graph, the adjoint reverses each arrow and conjugates its amplitude. In matrix notation, this is the conjugate transpose.

For closed-system evolution, normalization must survive for every state. Geometrically, the images of an orthonormal basis must remain unit vectors and remain mutually orthogonal. Otherwise, some combination of input components would acquire a different total squared length.

The overlap of the two images \(U\ket i\) and \(U\ket k\) can be written using the adjoint:

\[\braket{Ui}{Uk}=\bra iU^\dagger U\ket k.\]

The left side tests whether the transformed directions retain the geometry of the original basis: it must be one for the same basis vector and zero for distinct ones. The right side is also the amplitude to go from \(\ket k\) to \(\ket i\) by applying \(U\) and then \(U^\dagger\). Those requirements make every input return to itself:

\[U^\dagger U=I.\]

By the sum rule,

\[\bra iU^\dagger U\ket k=\sum_j\bra iU^\dagger\ket j\,\bra jU\ket k=\sum_j\bigl(\bra jU\ket i\bigr)^*\bra jU\ket k.\]

For \(i=k\) this is the outgoing normalization of column \(i\); for \(i\ne k\) it is the cross sum. So \(U^\dagger U=I\) states both conditions at once.

An operation satisfying this equation is unitary. Its adjoint is its inverse. It preserves lengths and inner products, so the orthogonal components of a state remain orthogonal as they move. For example, \(H^\dagger=H\), and applying it after \(H\) restores the original state.

The adjoint is defined more generally, even when the transformation does not preserve this geometry. For a real matrix, conjugation changes nothing, so the adjoint is the transpose. Consider

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The action of \(A\) is a horizontal shear: it slides each point horizontally by an amount set by its vertical coordinate. Its adjoint is a vertical shear. The initial state \(\ket\psi=(3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\) is therefore sent in different directions by the two transformations. The angle arcs compare those changes directly.

Applying one shear after the other moves the vector again. The result depends on their order: \(AA^\dagger\) applies the vertical shear first, while \(A^\dagger A\) applies the horizontal shear first. Neither sequence restores this initial vector.

To undo \(A\), we must slide horizontally in the opposite direction. Its inverse is \(A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), rather than the vertical shear \(A^\dagger\). The adjoint transfers the action across an inner product; it undoes the action only when the operation is unitary.

These shears illustrate general linear maps, whose images need not have unit length. They are not closed-system quantum gates. The inner-product identity defining the adjoint still holds:

\[\bra\phi A\ket\psi=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi.\]

4.2

Postulates

The vector and matrix notation expresses the amplitude rules without changing them. To apply those rules to physical systems, we specify the state, evolution, and measurement postulates. A measurement gives both a probability for its result and a state to use in the next calculation.

4.2.1 State and evolution postulates§

The amplitude rules have led to normalized vectors and unitary matrices. To use them as a theory of physical systems, we must specify which preparations and processes they describe. These physical assumptions are the postulates of the model.

State postulate. A pure state of a finite quantum system is represented by a unit vector in a complex inner-product space. Vectors differing only by a global phase represent the same physical state.

For a qubit, this means \(\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\), with \(|v_0|^2+|v_1|^2=1\). A common phase can make the first nonzero coordinate real and nonnegative. When both coordinates are nonzero, the remaining freedom is their relative magnitude and phase. It is convenient to write

\[\ket\psi=\cos\frac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\ket1,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi.\]

The angle \(\theta\) sets the two magnitudes, whose squares add to one; \(\phi\) sets their relative phase. At \(\theta=0\) or \(\pi\), one amplitude vanishes, so no relative phase remains.

Evolution postulate. The evolution of a closed system is unitary:

\[\ket\psi\longmapsto U\ket\psi,\qquad U^\dagger U=I.\]

The equation ensures that normalization is preserved, and \(U^\dagger\) reverses the evolution. It is the matrix form of the transition conditions derived from the proposed probability rule. Identifying such evolution with the behavior of a closed physical system is a postulate.

Think first of states with real amplitudes. The condition \(v_0^2+v_1^2=1\) puts their arrow tips on a circle of radius one. A rotation moves each tip around that circle without stretching the arrow. For example,

\[U(\delta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta&-\sin\delta\\\sin\delta&\cos\delta\end{pmatrix},\qquad U(\delta)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\\\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha+\delta)\\\sin(\alpha+\delta)\end{pmatrix}.\]

The direction changes by \(\delta\), while the squared length remains \(\cos^2(\alpha+\delta)+\sin^2(\alpha+\delta)=1\). Apply the same rotation to two states and their angle stays the same as well. This is the geometry behind unitary evolution: for complex amplitudes, the corresponding preserved quantity is the inner product,

\[\braket{U\chi}{U\psi}=\bra\chi U^\dagger U\ket\psi=\braket\chi\psi.\]

Setting \(\ket\chi=\ket\psi\) gives preservation of unit length. A real rotation is one example of a unitary; in general the geometry lives in complex state space. The arrows below represent amplitude lists, not directions in physical space or on the Bloch sphere.

A pure-state vector describes a preparation with definite amplitudes. If a preparation is selected at random, or a subsystem is correlated with another system, a single ket need not describe the available state. Such situations require density matrices, developed later and in Appendix D. For now, the state and evolution postulates apply to pure states of finite closed systems.

4.2.2 The measurement postulate§

Unitary evolution carries all amplitudes forward. An ideal measurement instead reports one outcome and leaves a state conditional on that outcome. The computational-basis measurement rule applies to any finite set of configurations.

Measurement postulate. For a normalized state

\[\ket\psi=\sum_jv_j\ket j,\]

an ideal measurement in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|v_j|^2\). Conditional on that outcome, the state becomes \(\ket j\).

The outcome probabilities and the conditional state update are physical postulates, not consequences of path arithmetic. The probability rule determines how often each result occurs across independently prepared systems. The state update determines what happens in a later operation on the measured system. For example, measuring

\[\ket\psi=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\ket0+\frac12\ket1\]

gives \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\). If the result is \(1\), a second measurement in the same basis immediately gives \(1\) again, since the state is now \(\ket1\). This repeatability is a property of the ideal measurement described by the postulate.

Geometrically, an ideal measurement resolves the state into orthogonal projections onto the measurement directions. Write \(P_j=\ket j\bra j\) for the projector onto direction \(\ket j\). The component \(P_j\ket\psi=v_j\ket j\) has squared length \(p(j)=\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2=|v_j|^2\). When outcome \(j\) occurs, retain that component and normalize it:

\[\ket{\psi_j}=\frac{P_j\ket\psi}{\sqrt{p(j)}},\qquad p(j)>0.\]

This gives \(\ket j\) up to an unobservable global phase. The projections determine the probabilities; a single run selects just one outcome. In the real-coordinate example above, the two projected lengths are \(\sqrt3/2\) and \(1/2\), so their squares are \(3/4\) and \(1/4\).

Predict before measuring

Prepare \(\ket+=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Measuring in the computational basis gives two equally likely outcomes. Must every measurement of this state be uncertain?

Compare your prediction

No. A measurement in a basis containing \(\ket+\) gives its corresponding outcome with certainty. The preparation is unchanged; the measurement asks a different question.

The same projection rule applies to any orthonormal measurement basis. To see this in a real state-vector plane, write \(\ket\psi=\cos\alpha\ket0+\sin\alpha\ket1\) and choose

\[\ket{b_0}=\cos\beta\ket0+\sin\beta\ket1,\qquad \ket{b_1}=-\sin\beta\ket0+\cos\beta\ket1.\]

The two directions have unit length and zero inner product. Taking their inner products with the state gives \(\braket{b_0}\psi=\cos(\alpha-\beta)\) and \(\braket{b_1}\psi=\sin(\alpha-\beta)\). Their squared magnitudes are the probabilities. Conditional on outcome \(j\), the state becomes \(\ket{b_j}\), up to global phase.

At \(\beta=0\), these are the computational, or Z, measurement directions. At \(\beta=45^\circ\), they give the X measurement: \(\ket{b_0}=\ket+\) and \(\ket{b_1}=-\ket-\), where \(\ket-=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\). The minus sign multiplying the whole second ket does not change its projector. Preparing \(\ket+\) and measuring X therefore gives + with certainty; measuring Z on the same preparation gives probabilities one half and one half.

Changing the measurement setting changes which projections are tested. It does not rotate the prepared state. Merely rewriting a fixed state and a fixed measurement in new coordinates would leave their probabilities unchanged. The angles here describe directions in the real state-vector plane, not angles in physical space or on the Bloch sphere.

The full history alternates between two kinds of change. Between measurements, continuous unitary evolution carries the state along a smooth path. At each measurement time \(t_k\), the observed outcome \(j_k\) selects a projection, which is normalized to give the next state. Evolution then resumes from that state until the next measurement.

The update also explains the effect of measuring between two Hadamard steps. Starting from \(\ket0\), the first \(H\) gives \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). A measurement leaves either \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\). Applying \(H\) to either state then gives equal probabilities, whereas two uninterrupted Hadamard steps return to \(\ket0\).

The conditional measurement update is not a unitary operation on the measured system. After outcome \(0\), both an initial \(\ket0\) and an initial \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) leave the same state \(\ket0\). That state alone cannot recover the input. This does not prevent a larger system, including the measuring apparatus and its record, from evolving unitarily; the conditional description keeps only the reported branch.

4.2.3 The composite-state postulate§

To describe two systems together, we must specify a configuration of each part. If qubit \(A\) can be \(0\) or \(1\), and qubit \(B\) can also be \(0\) or \(1\), the pair has four configurations: \(00,01,10,11\). The first label belongs to \(A\), and the second to \(B\); we met such two-label configurations when a second bit recorded which path a computation took. A joint state therefore assigns an amplitude to every pair of labels, rather than keeping just one amplitude list for each part.

Composite-state postulate. The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the state spaces of its parts:

\[\mathcal H_{AB}=\mathcal H_A\otimes\mathcal H_B,\qquad \ket{ij}=\ket i_A\otimes\ket j_B.\]

Here \(\mathcal H_A\) and \(\mathcal H_B\) denote the vector spaces for the two systems. Intuitively, \(\otimes\) builds a space with one basis direction for every possible pair of basis labels. If the parts have \(m\) and \(n\) basis states, the joint space has \(mn\) basis states. Dimensions multiply: two qubits have four joint amplitudes, and three have eight.

When the parts are independently prepared in pure states, their amplitudes multiply. For \(\ket\psi_A=a\ket0_A+b\ket1_A\) and \(\ket\phi_B=c\ket0_B+d\ket1_B\),

\[\ket\psi_A\otimes\ket\phi_B=ac\ket{00}+ad\ket{01}+bc\ket{10}+bd\ket{11}.\]

For example, the amplitude for \(01\) is \(ad\), so its probability is \(|ad|^2=|a|^2|d|^2\), as expected for independent preparations. The expression is called a product state. If both individual states are normalized, the joint state is normalized too.

The postulate allows more than product states. Every normalized superposition of joint basis states is an allowed pure state:

\[\ket\Psi_{AB}=\sum_{i,j}c_{ij}\ket{ij},\qquad\sum_{i,j}|c_{ij}|^2=1.\]

The joint amplitudes need not factor into separate lists for the parts. Consider \(\ket\Psi_{AB}=(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). Measuring both qubits in the computational basis gives \(00\) or \(11\), each with probability \(1/2\). It never gives \(01\) or \(10\). No product of two pure qubit states has these amplitudes: nonzero \(ac\) and \(bd\) would force \(a,b,c,d\) all to be nonzero, making \(ad\) and \(bc\) nonzero as well. Such a pure joint state is entangled; this failure to factor is called entanglement.

The distinction concerns the amplitudes of the whole state, not just correlated measurement results; a classical mixture can also produce matching outcomes. In an entangled pure state, neither part has a pure state vector that can be combined with the other to reconstruct the joint state. The composite-state postulate gives us the space in which these joint states live. Chapter 6 develops their operations, measurements, and correlations. First, Chapter 5 uses these postulates to define quantum computation and its circuit representation.
