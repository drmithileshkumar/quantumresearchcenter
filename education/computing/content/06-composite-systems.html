6.1

Registers and Operations

Chapter 5 treated the overall computation model and its single-qubit case. We now extend it to multiple qubits. We first build joint states from the states of the parts, and find that a superposition of joint configurations need not be anything new: it is often a product of individual states. Only then do we ask which joint states the theory admits.

6.1.1 Tensor products and product bases§

The memory of a multi-qubit computation is a register. For \(n\) qubits, its basis labels are the \(n\)-bit strings; a pure state assigns an amplitude to each label. The amplitudes have total squared magnitude one. A specified input can occupy some qubits while extra working qubits, called ancillas, start in \(\ket0\). Their initialization and later operations are part of the computation.

A register of two qubits has four configurations, \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\), so its state has four amplitudes. Suppose we prepare the first qubit in \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) and, independently, the second in \(c\ket0+d\ket1\). What are the four joint amplitudes?

Reaching the configuration \(01\) means that the first qubit is \(0\) and the second is \(1\). The two preparations are independent, so by the product rule the amplitude is the product of the two local amplitudes, \(ad\). Ordering the configurations with the first qubit as the leftmost bit,

\[\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix}.\]

This combination is the tensor product. For basis states it gives \(\ket a\otimes\ket b=\ket{ab}\); for example, \(\ket0\otimes\ket1=\ket{01}\). The four vectors \(\ket{ab}\) form the product basis. Two qubits need four amplitudes and three qubits need eight: dimensions multiply rather than add.

In a circuit we now draw one wire for each qubit, with the first qubit above the second. Separate gate boxes act independently on their own wires; a box spanning both wires specifies a joint operation. Later diagrams may combine several wires into one register wire, marked by a diagonal slash. That shorthand still represents all of the qubits, not one larger-amplitude qubit.

6.1.2 Product states§

Which joint states arise from independent preparations? A joint state of the form \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi\) is a product state. Its amplitudes are products of local amplitudes, so its probabilities are products of local probabilities: the two qubits behave independently.

Does a superposition of several joint configurations signal something more? Not necessarily. The state \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) has two nonzero amplitudes, yet

\[\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\ket0:\]

the first qubit is in an equal superposition, and the second is certainly \(0\).

The real question is whether one local vector for each qubit reproduces every joint amplitude. When it does, the state is a product state.

6.1.3 Joint amplitudes§

Chapter 4 introduced the composition postulate. Here it gives the allowed memory states of a computation. The product rule tells us how independently prepared systems combine. Must every joint state be a product? Quantum mechanics does not require it. It takes the whole tensor-product space as the state space of the pair.

Composition. The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the state spaces of its parts. Its basis vectors are the products of basis vectors of the parts.

For two qubits we write \(\ket\Psi=\alpha_{00}\ket{00}+\alpha_{01}\ket{01}+\alpha_{10}\ket{10}+\alpha_{11}\ket{11}\), where \(\sum_{a,b}|\alpha_{ab}|^2=1\). These four amplitudes need not have the product form \(ac,ad,bc,bd\). Gates update the joint list, and measurement samples a label from it.

A register of \(n\) qubits therefore has \(2^n\) basis vectors, and a general state needs \(2^n\) amplitudes. Does a measurement reveal \(2^n\) numbers? No: measuring all \(n\) qubits gives a single \(n\)-bit label.

Storing a general \(n\)-qubit state on a classical computer takes up to \(2^n\) amplitudes, which quickly becomes impractical. Does that by itself make quantum computers powerful? No. An advantage needs a task, and a procedure that makes its answer observable efficiently.

6.1.4 Local and controlled gates§

How does an operation on the first qubit alone act on the pair? It changes the first label and leaves the second unchanged: each arrow of \(U\) from \(i\) to \(j\) becomes, for each value \(b\) of the second label, an arrow from \(ib\) to \(jb\) with the same weight, \(\bra{jb}U\otimes I\ket{ib}=\bra jU\ket i\). We write this operation \(U\otimes I\). On a product state, \((U\otimes I)(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi)=(U\ket\psi)\otimes\ket\phi\).

A multi-qubit circuit has one wire for each qubit. A gate on one wire acts on that qubit and as the identity on the others; gates on separate qubits can act in parallel when the hardware permits. A joint gate connects the wires on which it acts.

Controlled-NOT, or CNOT, leaves the control label unchanged and flips the target exactly when the control label is one. With the first qubit as control,

\[\operatorname{CNOT}\ket{a,b}=\ket{a,b\oplus a},\qquad a,b\in\{0,1\}.\]

Here \(\oplus\) is exclusive OR, or addition modulo two. The circuit symbol is a filled dot on the control wire joined vertically to a circled plus on the target wire. The basis-state rule extends linearly to a superposition; the dot is not a measurement.

In the order \(00,01,10,11\), CNOT exchanges the last two configurations:

\[\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{00}\\\alpha_{01}\\\alpha_{10}\\\alpha_{11}\end{pmatrix}\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{00}\\\alpha_{01}\\\alpha_{11}\\\alpha_{10}\end{pmatrix}.\]

No amplitude is lost, and applying the exchange twice restores the input. CNOT is unitary and its own inverse. More generally, a controlled \(U\) leaves the target unchanged on the control-zero alternatives and applies \(U\) on the control-one alternatives.

The operations on individual wires are called local gates. For a first calculation, start with \(\ket{00}\), apply \(H\) on the first wire and \(X\) on the second. The result is

\[(H\otimes X)\ket{00}=\frac{\ket{01}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.\]

Measuring both wires now returns 01 or 11, each with probability one half. Apply CNOT before measuring instead: 01 stays 01 while 11 becomes 10, so the answers are now 01 and 10. The circuit has changed a definite second bit into a bit that is always opposite the first.

The complete unitary is \(U=\operatorname{CNOT}(H\otimes X)\): the rightmost operation acts first. Its implementation uses two qubits and three gates. The independent \(H\) and \(X\) gates can share a time step, followed by CNOT, giving two gate layers before measurement. This number of successive layers is the circuit depth, assuming these gates are available directly. An arbitrary operation on the whole register is not counted as one elementary gate.

6.2

Entangled States

Joint amplitudes allows joint states that are not products. Can a circuit actually produce one? Two gates suffice: a Hadamard and a CNOT turn \(\ket{00}\) into a Bell state, and comparing amplitudes proves that no product of individual states equals it. Its measurement outcomes are perfectly correlated, but so are those of two bits that are both \(0\) or both \(1\) at random. Measuring in another basis tells the two apart.

6.2.1 Bell-state preparation§

Start from \(\ket{00}\), apply \(H\) to the first qubit, then CNOT with the first qubit as control. What happens?

\(H\) gives \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\), still a product state. CNOT acts on each configuration: it leaves \(00\) alone and sends \(10\) to \(11\):

\[\ket{00}\to\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}\to\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.\]

The result is a Bell state, written \(\ket{\Phi^+}\).

The circuit drawing records this sequence, with time running from left to right. Does the vertical line of CNOT mean that the first qubit is measured and the result used to decide whether to flip the second? No. CNOT acts on both configurations of the superposition at once, without any measurement. That is why the result is a superposition of \(00\) and \(11\), rather than one of them.

The final pure state is entangled: its joint amplitudes do not factor into two single-qubit states. Its probabilities alone would not distinguish it from a classical mixture.

6.2.2 Nonfactorization of amplitudes§

Try to factor the Bell state as \((a\ket0+b\ket1)\otimes(c\ket0+d\ket1)\). Matching coefficients requires

\[ac=bd=1/\sqrt2,\qquad ad=bc=0.\]

The first pair makes all four factors nonzero; the second pair then cannot hold. The state is entangled: this pure joint state is not a product of pure local states.

We can still predict every measurement on either qubit by using the joint amplitudes. What fails is assigning each qubit its own pure-state vector and multiplying the two vectors to recover the pair. We do not need a new mathematical representation to carry out the computations here.

Do correlated zero–one results establish entanglement? No. Preparing \(00\) or \(11\) according to a fair classical coin gives the same computational-basis counts. We need a measurement that distinguishes that mixture from the coherent Bell superposition.

6.2.3 Joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities§

Although neither qubit of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) has an individual pure-state vector, each still has well-defined local measurement probabilities. How do we read probabilities from a joint state? With amplitudes \(\alpha_{ab}\), the joint probability is \(p(a,b)=|\alpha_{ab}|^2\). The probability that the first qubit gives \(a\), whatever the second gives, is the marginal \(p(a)=\sum_bp(a,b)\), and the conditional probability of \(b\) given \(a\) is \(p(b\mid a)=p(a,b)/p(a)\) when \(p(a)\gt 0\).

For \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), \(p(00)=p(11)=1/2\); each qubit alone gives \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\); and once the first result is known, the second is certain.

If only the first qubit is measured and its outcome is \(a\), keep the terms with that first label and divide by \(\sqrt{p(a)}\). For example, measuring the first qubit of \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{11})/\sqrt3\) gives 0 with probability \(2/3\); conditional on that outcome the state becomes \((\ket{00}+\ket{01})/\sqrt2\). Measuring part of a register therefore uses the same projection and normalization rule as Chapter 4.

To test coherence, apply \(H\) to each qubit before measuring. Recall \(\ket+=H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket-=H\ket1=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\). This procedure is called measurement in the \(\pm\) basis: an input \(\ket+\) gives 0 certainly, and \(\ket-\) gives 1 certainly.

For the Bell state, the two contributions are

\[(H\otimes H)\ket{00}=\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11}}2,\qquad (H\otimes H)\ket{11}=\frac{\ket{00}-\ket{01}-\ket{10}+\ket{11}}2.\]

Adding them with the initial factor \(1/\sqrt2\) cancels 01 and 10, leaving \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) again. The results still agree with certainty. For the classical mixture, each run starts in either 00 or 11, and either preparation gives all four results with probability \(1/4\) after the two Hadamards. We average those probabilities; there is no addition of amplitudes between different runs.

The output depends on both the joint state and the gates before readout. A single basis measurement misses the coherence that this second circuit exposes.

6.3

Quantum Information Constraints

Entangled states show that the joint state carries more than the states of the parts. What does this description allow us to do with quantum information? Two natural tasks turn out to be impossible. CNOT copies basis states, but linearity forbids any operation that copies every state. And acting on one half of an entangled pair changes the joint state but not the statistics of the other half, so entanglement alone cannot carry a message.

6.3.1 Basis-state copying§

Can a quantum state be copied? CNOT seems to copy. With a blank target, \(\ket0\ket0\to\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket1\ket0\to\ket1\ket1\).

What does it do to a superposition? By linearity,

\[(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\ket0\to\alpha\ket{00}+\beta\ket{11}.\]

Two copies would instead be

\[(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\otimes(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)=\alpha^2\ket{00}+\alpha\beta\ket{01}+\alpha\beta\ket{10}+\beta^2\ket{11}.\]

These differ whenever \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are both nonzero. CNOT copies the labels \(0\) and \(1\), not superpositions of them.

6.3.2 The no-cloning theorem§

Could some cleverer unitary copy every state? Suppose \(U\ket\psi\ket0=\ket\psi\ket\psi\) for every state \(\ket\psi\). Take two states \(\ket\psi\) and \(\ket\phi\). A unitary preserves inner products, and the inner product of two product states is the product of the inner products of their parts, so

\[\braket\phi\psi\braket00=\braket\phi\psi^2,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad\braket\phi\psi=\braket\phi\psi^2.\]

The overlap must therefore be \(0\) or \(1\). For two different states that are not orthogonal, such as \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) with overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), this fails. No unitary copies every state. This is the no-cloning theorem.

What does the theorem not forbid? States known to belong to an orthogonal set, such as basis labels, can be copied, and a state that we know how to prepare can simply be prepared again. What is impossible is a single fixed operation that perfectly copies an arbitrary unknown state.

6.3.3 Local states and no-signalling§

Alice and Bob share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\). If Alice measures her qubit in the computational basis, her outcomes occur equally often. Given her outcome, Bob has the matching basis state. Without her message, his probability of 0 is still \(\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac12(0)=1/2\). Alice cannot choose which result she obtains.

Checking only this one measurement would leave a loophole: perhaps Bob could apply a gate before readout and detect her choice. We can close it using the same amplitude rules. Write any joint state as \(\sum_{a,b}\alpha_{ab}\ket{a,b}\). For each fixed Bob label \(b\), his probability is the squared length of the amplitude list \((\alpha_{0b},\alpha_{1b})\). An Alice-only unitary multiplies this list by her unitary, preserving its squared length. Thus it leaves every Bob probability unchanged.

If Bob first applies any local gate \(V\), perform that step first in the calculation. Alice’s and Bob’s gates act on different labels, so their order does not matter. The same norm argument then proves that Alice cannot change his probabilities for this readout either.

An unreported computational-basis measurement by Alice also leaves those probabilities unchanged: it separates the two values of \(a\) into alternatives, and averaging their probabilities gives the same sum \(\sum_a|\alpha_{ab}|^2\). A measurement in another basis is a local unitary followed by this measurement, so it cannot help her signal. The conclusion concerns Bob’s unconditioned statistics; a message reporting Alice’s outcome lets him select a conditional branch.

This is no-signalling for the local gates and projective measurements used here. Correlations become useful for communication only when supplemented by an ordinary message. Appendix D extends the argument to more general local operations.

6.4

Quantum Teleportation

No-cloning and no-signalling say what entanglement cannot do. Teleportation shows what it can: with a shared Bell pair and two classical bits, an unknown state moves from sender to receiver without the qubit that holds it travelling. We follow the joint state through the sender’s operations and measurement, find the corrections the receiver must apply, and check that neither limit is violated.

6.4.1 Entanglement resources§

Alice holds a qubit in an unknown state \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) and wants Bob to have it. She cannot copy it, and measuring it would reveal only one bit. What can she do if they share a Bell pair?

The three qubits start in \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket{\Phi^+}\); Alice holds the first two and Bob the third. Alice applies CNOT from her input qubit to her half of the Bell pair, then \(H\) to her input qubit. The Bell pair must already have been distributed; no qubit is sent during the subsequent protocol. In the order input, Alice’s half, Bob’s half, the initial expansion is \((\alpha\ket{000}+\alpha\ket{011}+\beta\ket{100}+\beta\ket{111})/\sqrt2\). CNOT changes it to \((\alpha\ket{000}+\alpha\ket{011}+\beta\ket{110}+\beta\ket{101})/\sqrt2\). Applying \(H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(H\ket1=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\) on the first label now gives the four branches below.

6.4.2 Measurement and conditional corrections§

Write Alice’s unknown input as \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\). After her CNOT and Hadamard, grouping the three-qubit state by her two basis labels gives

\[\frac12\bigl(\ket{00}\ket\psi+\ket{01}X\ket\psi+\ket{10}Z\ket\psi+\ket{11}XZ\ket\psi\bigr).\]

For example, the 01 branch contains \(\alpha\ket1+\beta\ket0=X\ket\psi\); the 11 branch contains \(\alpha\ket1-\beta\ket0=XZ\ket\psi\). The first label records Alice’s input-wire measurement, and the second records her Bell-half measurement.

This identity is the protocol. Every branch has squared norm \(1/4\), regardless of \(\alpha,\beta\). Alice measures her two qubits, sends their values \((m,n)\), and Bob receives the conditional state \(X^nZ^m\ket\psi\).

To check the identity, expand the initial state \((\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\), apply Alice’s two gates to each term, and collect equal first-two-bit labels. The next problems let you test the same mechanism on specific inputs.

6.4.3 State transfer and classical communication§

Before the classical bits arrive, Bob averages probabilities over the four equally likely states \(\ket\psi,X\ket\psi,Z\ket\psi,XZ\ket\psi\). His probability of 0 is \((|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2+|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2)/4=1/2\). In fact every normalized test state \(\ket v=c\ket0+d\ket1\) has probability \(1/2\): pairing the two opposite signs in these four branches cancels cross terms, leaving \((|c|^2+|d|^2)(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2)/2\). No choice of local readout reveals the input. His qubit is already present, but he cannot use it as the recovered input or learn the input’s amplitudes from it. The useful transfer waits for the message.

Nor is there a second copy left with Alice. Her measured qubits are basis states, and the shared Bell pair has been consumed. Conditioned on the communicated outcome and Bob’s correction, the unknown input is now at Bob.

Keep three descriptions separate: the full joint state, Bob’s state given Alice’s result, and Bob’s state when that result is unknown. Confusing them makes teleportation look like instantaneous communication or cloning. Tracking them turns it into an explicit calculation with the rules we already have.

We now have the multi-qubit model in working form: initialize a register, update its joint amplitudes with local and controlled gates, measure selected wires, and use classical records for later decisions. So far the register has carried states from one place to another. Can it also compute?

6.5

Quantum Parallelism

A register of qubits carries an amplitude for every bit string, and a gate on one wire updates all of them together. A reversible evaluation of a function should then act on every input string of the register in one run. We follow that run, ask how much of its result a measurement can deliver, and look for a question it might answer instead of the full table of values.

6.5.1 Evaluation on every input§

Teleportation used a register to move a state. Can a register also compute? Reversible computation already gave us a way to evaluate any Boolean function \(f\) without discarding information: keep the input \(x\) and add \(f(x)\) to a target bit. On the basis states of a quantum register this is the gate

\[U_f\ket x\ket y=\ket x\ket{y\oplus f(x)}.\]

For each input it either leaves the target alone or flips it, so it only rearranges configurations. It is unitary, and it can be built from Toffoli and CNOT gates at about the cost of evaluating \(f\) once, provided any workspace is cleared by uncomputation.

What does \(U_f\) do when the input register is not a single string? Take two input qubits, both \(\ket0\), and apply \(H\) to each:

\[(H\otimes H)\ket{00}=\tfrac12\bigl(\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11}\bigr).\]

Two gates spread equal amplitude over all four inputs. With \(n\) input qubits, \(n\) Hadamard gates give all \(2^n\) strings, each with amplitude \(2^{-n/2}\). The number of gates grows by one per qubit; the number of configurations doubles.

Now add a target qubit in \(\ket0\) and apply \(U_f\). The gate is defined by one arrow from each basis configuration, and each configuration carries its amplitude along its own arrow. So

\[U_f\Bigl(\tfrac12\sum_x\ket x\Bigr)\ket0=\tfrac12\sum_x\ket x\ket{f(x)}.\]

For a concrete case, let \(f\) be AND, so \(f(x)=1\) only for \(x=11\):

\[\tfrac12\bigl(\ket{00}\ket0+\ket{01}\ket0+\ket{10}\ket0+\ket{11}\ket1\bigr).\]

One application of \(U_f\) has produced a state in which every input appears beside its own value. This is quantum parallelism. Has the computer evaluated \(f\) four times for the price of once? The state holds four input–value pairs, but holding them is not the same as reading them.

6.5.2 What a measurement reveals§

Suppose we measure every qubit of the AND state. Each configuration \(\ket x\ket{f(x)}\) has amplitude \(1/2\), so each of the four pairs \((00,0)\), \((01,0)\), \((10,0)\), and \((11,1)\) appears with probability \(1/4\). A run returns one input, chosen at random, together with its value. Afterwards the register is in the observed configuration, and the other three pairs are gone.

To learn the whole table, we prepare and measure again. The inputs arrive at random, so four runs are the minimum and repeats make more runs likely. Evaluating \(f\) on the four inputs one at a time takes exactly four evaluations. With \(n\) inputs, the table has \(2^n\) entries, while a measurement of the \(n+1\) qubits yields \(n+1\) bits.

Could we copy the state before measuring it and measure each copy? A device that copied every such state would copy states that are not orthogonal, which the no-cloning theorem forbids. Two different functions can give overlapping parallel states: for AND and for the constant zero function, the states agree in three of their four terms.

What if we measure only the target? For AND it gives \(0\) with probability \(3/4\), leaving the inputs in \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10})/\sqrt3\), and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\), leaving \(\ket{11}\). The target alone tells us one value that \(f\) takes, and the inputs that remain are exactly the strings with that value.

That conditional state shows something else. Unless \(f\) is constant, the parallel state is entangled: it cannot be written as an input state times a target state, because the target is \(\ket0\) beside some inputs and \(\ket1\) beside others. The target keeps a record of which input occurred. Parallel evaluation alone produces a random sample of the table, not the table.

6.5.3 Global properties through interference§

If a run cannot deliver the table, what could it deliver? Many questions need much less than the table. Is \(f\) constant? Does it take the value \(1\) anywhere? Each answer is a single bit, but it depends on all the values together. Such an answer is a global property of \(f\).

Interference combines contributions from all the inputs, so it is the natural tool. After \(U_f\), apply \(H\) to each input qubit again and measure the inputs. Each input string \(x\) sends amplitude \(1/2\) to the readout \(00\), so the readout \(00\) collects contributions from all four branches.

For a constant function \(f(x)=c\), the parallel state is \(\tfrac12\sum_x\ket x\ket c\), a product of the uniform input state and \(\ket c\). Applying \(H\otimes H\) again returns the inputs to \(\ket{00}\). The readout is \(00\) with certainty.

For AND, the contributions to \(00\) do not all end at the same configuration. Three branches carry target \(0\) and contribute \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\) each to \(\ket{00}\ket0\); the branch \(x=11\) carries target \(1\) and contributes \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\) to \(\ket{00}\ket1\). Amplitudes add only at a common configuration, so

\[p(00)=\Bigl(\tfrac34\Bigr)^2+\Bigl(\tfrac14\Bigr)^2=\tfrac58.\]

The readout \(00\) no longer separates the two cases reliably. The cause is the record the target keeps: branches with different target values sit at different configurations, and their contributions cannot cancel or reinforce one another. This is the path-information effect of interference, now produced by the computation itself.

Quantum parallelism is therefore a resource only when a later interference step turns many values into one useful bit, and that step needs a target that keeps no record of the branch. Which global properties can be read out this way, and how must the target be prepared? Functions that can be consulted only through queries make these questions precise.
