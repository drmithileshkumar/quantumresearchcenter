8.1

Circuit Representation

We have used circuit diagrams since our first single-qubit programs in Chapter 5. When a circuit spans multiple wires and mixes unitary gates with measurements, three foundational questions arise: when can two gates execute in parallel, what is hidden inside a macroscopic subroutine box, and how does coherent quantum control differ from classical control driven by a measurement outcome?

8.1.1 Wires and gate ordering§

In a multi-qubit quantum circuit, each horizontal wire tracks one qubit over time from left to right, and each box represents a unitary gate or measurement applied to the wire(s) it touches. Remember that a wire labels a subsystem index, not an isolated state vector: as soon as multi-qubit gates entangle the wires, individual wires no longer possess standalone state vectors, and we track the joint state vector of the entire register.

Notice the reversal between visual reading order and algebraic matrix multiplication: if a circuit applies gate \(U\) first (on the left) and gate \(V\) second (on the right) to an input state \(\ket{\psi}\), the output state is \(V(U\ket{\psi}) = (VU)\ket{\psi}\), so the matrix product \(VU\) is written from right to left.

When can two gates in a circuit run at the same time? Whenever they act on completely disjoint sets of wires! If gate \(U\) acts on the first wire and gate \(V\) acts on the second wire, applying \(U\) then \(V\) or applying \(V\) then \(U\) routes every product-basis state \(\ket{ij}\) along the exact same transition arrows with product weight \(\bra{i'}U\ket{i}\bra{j'}V\ket{j}\):

\[(U\otimes I)(I\otimes V) = (I\otimes V)(U\otimes I) = U\otimes V.\]

Because operations on disjoint wires commute, they can be drawn in the same vertical column (a single parallel layer) and executed simultaneously if the hardware allows. By contrast, two gates that share a wire generally do not commute—for example, \(XZ = -ZX\) and \(HX = ZH\)—so their left-to-right order along the shared wire must be strictly respected.

8.1.2 Circuits for query and search algorithms§

When we draw a high-level algorithm as a circuit diagram, a single large box often stands for an entire subroutine. In Deutsch's algorithm from Chapter 7, the two initial \(H\) gates prepare \(\ket{+}\ket{-}\), the box labeled \(U_f\) kicks back the phase \((-1)^{f(x)}\), and the final \(H\) interferes the two branches. Drawing \(U_f\) as a single box specifies its unitary input–output behavior, but conceals the internal network of elementary gates needed to compute \(f\).

In Chapter 9, we will build Grover's search algorithm by repeating a two-box cycle: a phase-oracle box \(O_f\) that flips the sign of marked bit strings, followed by a diffusion box \(D\) that reflects amplitudes about the uniform superposition. Before we can judge the true cost of running such an algorithm on hardware, we must open both boxes and see how they are constructed out of elementary one- and two-qubit gates.

8.1.3 Measurement and classical control§

Does it make a physical difference whether a target gate is controlled coherently by an unmeasured qubit or controlled classically by the bit obtained from measuring that qubit? Let us compare both on the exact same two-qubit input state \(\ket{+}\ket{0} = \bigl(\ket{00}+\ket{10}\bigr)/\sqrt{2}\).

First, apply a coherent controlled gate—the unitary CNOT gate—directly to \(\ket{+}\ket{0}\). Because CNOT acts linearly on the superposition without measuring the control wire, it produces the pure entangled Bell state \(\ket{\Phi^+} = (\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt{2}\). Second, suppose instead that we measure the control wire in the computational basis, record the classical outcome bit \(m\in\{0,1\}\), and apply \(X\) to the target wire if \(m=1\)—a classically controlled gate. On half the runs the measurement yields \(m=0\) and leaves \(\ket{00}\); on the other half it yields \(m=1\) and flips the target to leave \(\ket{11}\). Instead of the coherent Bell state \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), the classically controlled procedure produces a \(50/50\) classical mixture of \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket{11}\)! As we saw in Section 6.2.3, measuring both qubits in the \(X\) basis immediately exposes the difference: \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) yields perfectly correlated \(X\)-basis outcomes (\(++\) or \(--\)), whereas the classical mixture yields completely uncorrelated \(X\)-basis outcomes.

Circuit notation keeps this distinction unmistakable: single lines carry coherent quantum states, whereas double lines (or dashed lines) emerging from a measurement box \(\mathrm{M}\) carry classical bits that trigger classically controlled operations.

Quantum teleportation from Section 6.4 uses both types of control in their proper places: Alice's CNOT gate is a coherent two-qubit gate acting before measurement, whereas Bob's recovery gates \(X^n\) and \(Z^m\) are classically controlled by the two bits Alice measures and transmits. Replacing Alice's coherent CNOT with a measurement-and-classical-control step would destroy entanglement and ruin teleportation, while replacing Bob's classical control with coherent quantum control would require Alice to ship her physical qubits to Bob's lab!

8.2

Gate Constructions

Every quantum circuit is assembled from a small repertoire of elementary gates. In this section, we review the standard single-qubit gates—the Pauli matrices, the Hadamard gate, continuous Pauli rotations, and the discrete phase gates \(S\) and \(T\)—express two-qubit controlled gates algebraically using projectors, and construct the multi-qubit phase-marking and reflection circuits that we will use for quantum search in Chapter 9.

8.2.1 Pauli, Hadamard, and rotation gates§

Let us gather our elementary single-qubit gates in one place. We have already worked extensively with the Pauli matrices \(I, X, Y, Z\) and the Hadamard gate

\[H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}.\]

While \(X, Y, Z,\) and \(H\) perform fixed discrete reflections and permutations of amplitudes, general single-qubit transformations require adjustable angles. In Section 5.3.2, we built the continuous rotation \(R_x(\theta)\) from \(X\). Because every Pauli matrix \(P\in\{X,Y,Z\}\) is Hermitian (\(P^\dagger = P\)) and squares to the identity (\(P^2 = I\)), the exact same formula defines a unitary rotation gate around any Pauli axis \(P\):

\[R_P(\theta) = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,I - i\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,P.\]

We can verify unitarity in one line: since \(P^\dagger = P\) and \(P^2 = I\), multiplying \(R_P(\theta)^\dagger = \cos(\theta/2)I + i\sin(\theta/2)P\) by \(R_P(\theta)\) cancels the \(\pm i\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)P\) cross terms and leaves \(\bigl(\cos^2(\theta/2) + \sin^2(\theta/2)\bigr)I = I\).

When quantum hardware—especially fault-tolerant hardware—restricts us to a finite catalog of discrete gates, two special members of the phase gate family \(R_\phi = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{i\phi})\) play a central role. They are the phase gates \(S\) (the quarter-turn phase gate, \(\phi = \pi/2\)) and \(T\) (the eighth-turn phase gate, \(\phi = \pi/4\)):

\[S = R_{\pi/2} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & i\end{pmatrix},\qquad T = R_{\pi/4} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & e^{i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix}.\]

Because multiplying diagonal phase gates adds their phase angles, squaring \(T\) gives \(T^2 = S\), and squaring \(S\) gives \(S^2 = Z\) (so \(S\) is a square root of \(Z\), distinct from \(\sqrt{X}\)). Note also that since \(S\) and \(T\) have complex diagonal entries, their inverses are their adjoints \(S^\dagger = \operatorname{diag}(1, -i)\) and \(T^\dagger = \operatorname{diag}(1, e^{-i\pi/4})\).

8.2.2 Controlled and entangling gates§

How do we write a general two-qubit controlled-\(U\) gate using the projector notation from Section 4.2.2? On branches where the control wire is in \(\ket{0}\), the target wire undergoes the identity \(I\); on branches where the control wire is in \(\ket{1}\), the target wire undergoes \(U\). Because the projectors \(\ketbra{0}{0}\) and \(\ketbra{1}{1}\) select the \(\ket{0}\) and \(\ket{1}\) components of the control wire respectively, we can write controlled-\(U\) compactly as a sum of two tensor products:

\[\text{controlled-}U = \ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + \ketbra{1}{1}\otimes U.\]

Here each tensor product \(A\otimes B\) acts independently on the first and second qubits, with transition amplitude \(\bra{jk}(A\otimes B)\ket{il} = \bra{j}A\ket{i}\,\bra{k}B\ket{l}\).

Setting \(U = X\) gives \(\operatorname{CNOT} = \ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + \ketbra{1}{1}\otimes X\). Setting \(U = Z\) gives the controlled-\(Z\) gate (\(\operatorname{CZ}\)), which leaves \(\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}\) unchanged and multiplies only \(\ket{11}\) by \(-1\):

\[\operatorname{CZ} = \ketbra{0}{0}\otimes I + \ketbra{1}{1}\otimes Z = \operatorname{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1).\]

Notice that \(\operatorname{CZ}\) is completely symmetric between the two qubits—it flips the phase if and only if both qubits are \(1\). Even better, because conjugation by \(H\) turns \(X\) into \(Z\),

\[HXH = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix} = Z,\]

we can build controlled-\(Z\) directly from CNOT by placing a Hadamard gate on the target wire immediately before and after the CNOT: \((I\otimes H)\operatorname{CNOT}(I\otimes H) = \operatorname{CZ}\).

Remember that a controlled gate is an entangling gate because it can create entanglement from suitable product states—for instance, \(\operatorname{CNOT}(\ket{+}\ket{0}) = \ket{\Phi^+}\) and \(\operatorname{CZ}(\ket{+}\ket{+}) = (\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}-\ket{11})/2\)—even though it leaves computational basis states like \(\ket{00}\) unentangled.

8.2.3 Oracle and reflection circuits§

Suppose we want to search \(N = 2^n\) bit strings for a marked target string \(w\in\{0,1\}^n\). As we will see in Chapter 9, quantum search alternates two multi-qubit unitary operations: a phase oracle \(O_f\) that multiplies \(\ket{w}\) by \(-1\) while leaving all other basis states unchanged, and a diffusion operator \(D\) that reflects the state vector about the uniform superposition of all \(N\) strings. How do we build both operations from elementary gates?

First, write \(\ket{0^n} = \ket{0\cdots 0}\) for the \(n\)-qubit all-zero state, and apply \(H^{\otimes n}\) (one Hadamard gate on each wire) to prepare the uniform superposition

\[\ket{s} = H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}\ket{x}.\]

How do we build a phase gate that flips the sign of a single chosen string \(w\)? An \(n\)-qubit controlled-\(Z\) gate flips the sign of the all-ones state \(\ket{11\cdots 1}\) and leaves every other \(n\)-bit basis state untouched. To flip the sign of \(\ket{w}\) instead, we simply wrap the multi-controlled-\(Z\) gate in a layer of \(X\) gates applied to every wire \(k\) where the bit \(w_k = 0\): the initial \(X\) gates map \(\ket{w}\mapsto\ket{11\cdots 1}\), the controlled-\(Z\) attaches a \(-1\) phase to \(\ket{11\cdots 1}\), and the matching final \(X\) gates map \(\ket{11\cdots 1}\) back to \(\ket{w}\)! (More generally, when \(w\) is recognized by a Boolean predicate circuit \(f(x)=1\), the compute, phase, uncompute construction from Section 7.3.3 implements \(O_f\) without our knowing \(w\) in advance.)

Second, how do we build the reflection \(D = 2\ketbra{s}{s} - I\) about the uniform superposition \(\ket{s}\)? Notice how \(D\) acts geometrically: the projector \(\ketbra{s}{s}\) extracts the component of any state along \(\ket{s}\), so \(D = 2\ketbra{s}{s} - I\) preserves \(\ket{s}\) (\(D\ket{s} = 2\ket{s}-\ket{s} = \ket{s}\)) and negates every vector orthogonal to \(\ket{s}\). Because \(\ket{s} = H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n}\) and \((H^{\otimes n})^\dagger = H^{\otimes n}\), we can conjugate a reflection about \(\ket{0^n}\) by Hadamard layers:

\[D = 2\ketbra{s}{s} - I = H^{\otimes n}\bigl(2\ketbra{0^n}{0^n} - I\bigr)H^{\otimes n}.\]

The middle operation \(2\ketbra{0^n}{0^n} - I\) simply attaches \(+1\) to \(\ket{0^n}\) and \(-1\) to every nonzero bit string! For two qubits (\(n=2\)), the diagonal matrix \(2\ketbra{00}{00} - I = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)\) is up to an overall global minus sign equal to \(\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)\), which flips the sign of \(\ket{00}\) alone—implemented by applying \(X\otimes X\), then controlled-\(Z\), then \(X\otimes X\). Surrounding that core with \(H\otimes H\) on both sides implements \(-D\) (where the overall \(-1\) is an unobservable global phase unless the reflection itself is used as a controlled subroutine). For general \(n\), the \(n\)-qubit controlled-\(Z\) gate can be decomposed into a linear number of Toffoli and CNOT gates using ancilla workspace.

8.3

Universal Gate Sets

In Chapter 2, we proved that the NAND gate (or the reversible Toffoli gate) is universal for classical Boolean computation: any Boolean function on \(n\) bits can be built from a finite network of those gates. What is the quantum counterpart of universality? In this section, we see why single-qubit gates alone are never universal, state the universality theorem for CNOT plus single-qubit gates, use a parameter-counting argument to show that most \(n\)-qubit unitaries require exponentially many gates, and explain how a finite discrete gate set such as Clifford+\(T\) approximates any unitary to arbitrary accuracy.

8.3.1 Limits of local operations§

Can a circuit composed solely of single-qubit gates ever implement every unitary transformation on an \(n\)-qubit register? No: any sequence of single-qubit gates on \(n\) wires combines into a tensor product of single-qubit unitaries, \(U_1\otimes U_2\otimes\cdots\otimes U_n\). Acting on any product state \(\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_n}\), it produces another product state:

\[(U_1\otimes\cdots\otimes U_n)(\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_n}) = (U_1\ket{\psi_1})\otimes\cdots\otimes(U_n\ket{\psi_n}).\]

No matter how many single-qubit gates we stack on individual wires, a circuit without multi-qubit gates can never turn \(\ket{00}\) into the entangled Bell state \(\ket{\Phi^+}\). Every universal quantum gate set must therefore include at least one multi-qubit entangling gate.

8.3.2 Entangling gates and universality§

Once we supplement arbitrary single-qubit unitary gates with a single two-qubit entangling gate—namely CNOT—do we need any three-qubit or four-qubit primitives? A fundamental theorem of quantum computation says no: the set consisting of CNOT together with all single-qubit unitary gates is universal, meaning that every unitary matrix \(U\) on an \(n\)-qubit register can be synthesized exactly as a finite circuit of CNOTs and single-qubit gates. (Notice that universality is a property of the combined set: CNOT alone only permutes computational basis strings, and single-qubit gates alone never entangle, but together they generate the entire unitary group on \(2^n\) dimensions.)

How is this exact synthesis theorem proved? First, standard matrix elimination decomposes any \(2^n\times 2^n\) unitary matrix into a product of "two-level" unitaries, each of which acts nontrivialy on only two computational basis states at a time; second, Gray-code routing and multi-controlled rotations decompose each two-level unitary into CNOTs and single-qubit gates.

Crucially, universality tells us what is possible in principle, not whether the resulting circuit is short! How many elementary gates are needed to build a typical \(n\)-qubit unitary matrix? We can find out by counting continuous real parameters. Specifying an arbitrary \(2^n\times 2^n\) unitary matrix (up to a global phase) requires \(4^n - 1\) independent real numbers. Each single-qubit or two-qubit gate in a circuit supplies at most a fixed constant number \(k\) of adjustable real parameters. A circuit of \(L\) gates can therefore tune at most \(kL\) real parameters, so reaching a generic \(n\)-qubit unitary requires \(kL \ge 4^n - 1\)—a gate count \(L\) that grows exponentially with the number of qubits \(n\)! Just as with classical Boolean functions, almost all \(n\)-qubit unitary matrices are exponentially expensive to build; practical quantum algorithms work by targeting structured unitaries (such as arithmetic circuits, phase oracles, reflections, and Fourier transforms) that can be assembled from polynomially many gates.

8.3.3 Exact synthesis and approximation§

The exact universality theorem above assumed we could apply single-qubit rotations \(R_P(\theta)\) for any continuous real angle \(\theta\). Realistic fault-tolerant quantum architectures, however, provide only a finite discrete set of elementary gates. Can a finite gate set construct every unitary matrix with zero error? Never: from a finite alphabet of gates, there are only countably many finite circuits in total, whereas the set of unitary matrices forms an uncountable continuum. With a finite gate set, universality must mean approximating any target unitary \(U\) to within any desired accuracy \(\epsilon > 0\).

How do we measure the distance between an ideal unitary \(U\) and an approximating circuit \(V\)? We use the worst-case state-vector error over all normalized input states \(\ket{\psi}\):

\[\|U - V\| = \max_{\|\psi\|=1} \bigl\|(U - V)\ket{\psi}\bigr\|.\]

This quantity is called the operator-norm error (or spectral-norm distance). Guaranteeing \(\|U - V\| \le \epsilon\) ensures that for every possible input state \(\ket{\psi}\), the output vector \(V\ket{\psi}\) lies within Euclidean distance \(\epsilon\) of the ideal output \(U\ket{\psi}\), which in turn keeps all outcome probabilities within \(2\epsilon\) of their ideal values. (When comparing standalone circuits, we match global phases before computing \(\|U - V\|\); if \(U\) is used inside a controlled gate, its phase becomes a relative phase and must be matched as well.)

The standard finite universal gate set is Clifford+T, consisting of just four gates: the Hadamard gate \(H\), the phase gate \(S\), the two-qubit CNOT gate, and the \(\pi/4\)-phase gate \(T\). While circuits built from the Clifford gates \(\{H, S, \operatorname{CNOT}\}\) alone can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer and cover only a finite group of rotations on one qubit, adding the single gate \(T\) makes the set dense in the unitary group: the Solovay–Kitaev theorem shows that any single-qubit gate can be approximated to error \(\delta\) using a remarkably short sequence of \(H\) and \(T\) gates (growing only polylogarithmically in \(1/\delta\)).

How do gate approximation errors accumulate across a multi-gate circuit? Suppose a two-gate circuit replaces the ideal gates \(U_1, U_2\) with approximations \(V_1, V_2\). By adding and subtracting \(V_2 U_1\), we can split the total error into two pieces:

\[V_2 V_1 - U_2 U_1 = V_2(V_1 - U_1) + (V_2 - U_2)U_1.\]

Because unitary matrices \(V_2\) and \(U_1\) preserve vector lengths, the triangle inequality implies \(\|V_2 V_1 - U_2 U_1\| \le \|V_1 - U_1\| + \|V_2 - U_2\|\)! By induction, in a circuit of \(L\) gates where gate \(i\) has approximation error at most \(\epsilon_i\), the errors add at most linearly: \(\|V_L\cdots V_1 - U_L\cdots U_1\| \le \sum_{i=1}^L \epsilon_i\). Thus, to guarantee an overall circuit accuracy of \(\epsilon\) across \(L\) gates, it suffices to synthesize each individual gate to tolerance \(\epsilon/L\).

8.4

Implementation Resources

Showing that a unitary transformation can be built from elementary gates is only the first step toward a practical implementation. To evaluate what a quantum algorithm actually costs—both on paper and on physical hardware—we must track circuit size, depth, and workspace, account for device routing and data-loading overhead, and understand how quantum error correction protects fragile superpositions against physical noise.

8.4.1 Gate count, depth, and workspace§

When we analyze a quantum circuit, three primary resource metrics govern its cost: the gate count (the total number of elementary gates in the circuit), the depth (the number of sequential time layers when gates on disjoint wires run in parallel), and the width (the total number of qubits used by the circuit, including both input qubits and working ancilla qubits initialized in \(\ket{0}\)).

Just as in classical computer architecture, space (width) and time (depth) can often be traded against each other. Providing extra ancilla qubits allows intermediate sub-results to be computed in parallel and stored, reducing circuit depth; conversely, uncomputing and recomputing intermediate values on the fly saves ancilla qubits at the price of a larger gate count and greater depth.

One crucial theoretical requirement prevents a circuit family from hiding uncomputable or intractable work inside its wiring design. Because a circuit has a fixed number of input wires \(n\), solving a problem for inputs of arbitrary length requires a family of circuits \(\{C_n\}\)—one for each input size \(n\). A circuit family is called uniform if there is a single efficient (polynomial-time) classical algorithm that, given \(n\), outputs the gate-by-gate description of \(C_n\). Uniformity ensures that the work of designing the circuit is itself accounted for rather than smuggled in for free.

8.4.2 Connectivity and data preparation§

On paper, we can draw a CNOT gate between the top wire and the bottom wire just as easily as between two adjacent wires. On physical quantum hardware—such as a superconducting chip arranged on a two-dimensional grid—two-qubit gates can usually act only between physically adjacent qubits. This hardware constraint is the device's connectivity graph. To apply a CNOT between two distant qubits on a sparse connectivity graph, the compiler must route their quantum states next to each other using a chain of two-qubit SWAP gates (where each \(\operatorname{SWAP}\ket{a}\ket{b} = \ket{b}\ket{a}\) can be built from three alternating CNOTs), increasing both gate count and circuit depth.

A second hidden cost is input preparation. If an algorithm assumes that a large classical table of \(N = 2^n\) numbers has already been loaded into the \(2^n\) amplitudes of an \(n\)-qubit state \(\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}\), preparing that state from \(\ket{0^n}\) can require \(2^n\) gates—completely swamping any polynomial or \(\sqrt{N}\) speedup of the subsequent algorithm! Similarly, when an oracle \(U_f\) is replaced by an explicit logic circuit, the gate complexity of evaluating \(f\) multiplies the query count.

Any realistic assessment of quantum advantage must therefore specify the elementary gate set, the hardware connectivity overhead, the cost of state preparation and oracle synthesis, and the number of measurement repetitions required.

8.4.3 Error accumulation and reliability§

Even when a circuit is synthesized exactly on paper, physical quantum gates and idle wires suffer from environmental noise (decoherence, stray fields, and control imperfections). In classical computing, we protect against bit flips by making redundant copies (\(0\mapsto 000\), \(1\mapsto 111\)) and taking a majority vote. In quantum computing, two obstacles immediately seem to block that strategy: the no-cloning theorem forbids copying an unknown superposition \(a\ket{0}+b\ket{1}\) into \((a\ket{0}+b\ket{1})^{\otimes 3}\), and measuring the qubits to check for errors would collapse the superposition!

How can quantum error correction detect and fix an error without cloning the state and without measuring the encoded information? Let us trace the three-qubit bit-flip code. Instead of cloning \(a\ket{0}+b\ket{1}\), we apply two CNOT gates from our data qubit onto two ancilla qubits initialized in \(\ket{0}\), encoding the single logical qubit into an entangled three-qubit state:

\[a\ket{0} + b\ket{1} \quad\longmapsto\quad a\ket{000} + b\ket{111}.\]

Now suppose a stray bit-flip error \(X\) strikes the second physical qubit, corrupting the state into \(a\ket{010} + b\ket{101}\). How can we locate which qubit flipped without learning anything about \(a\) and \(b\)?

The secret is to measure two-qubit parities rather than individual qubit values! Notice that in both branches \(\ket{010}\) and \(\ket{101}\) of the corrupted state, qubits \(1\) and \(2\) disagree (parity \(x_1\oplus x_2 = 1\)), and qubits \(2\) and \(3\) also disagree (parity \(x_2\oplus x_3 = 1\)). By applying CNOTs from the data qubits into two fresh syndrome ancillas, we write the two parities \((x_1\oplus x_2,\, x_2\oplus x_3)\) into the ancillas and measure only the ancillas. Because both branches \(\ket{010}\) and \(\ket{101}\) have the exact same parities \((1,1)\), the ancilla measurement yields \((1,1)\) with certainty and leaves the superposition \(a\ket{010} + b\ket{101}\) completely uncollapsed! Those measured parity bits form the error syndrome: syndrome \((0,0)\) means no qubit flipped, \((1,0)\) means qubit \(1\) flipped, \((1,1)\) means qubit \(2\) flipped, and \((0,1)\) means qubit \(3\) flipped. Applying a corrective \(X\) gate to qubit \(2\) restores \(a\ket{000} + b\ket{111}\) exactly.

Full quantum error correction extends this parity-check principle to simultaneously correct arbitrary bit flips (\(X\)), phase flips (\(Z\)), and combined errors (\(Y\))—and fault-tolerant circuit design ensures that errors occurring inside the correction gates themselves do not spread uncontrollably. While fault-tolerant architecture lies beyond the scope of this book, the lesson is clear: quantum mechanics not only introduces continuous phase errors, but also supplies the entangled subspaces and projective syndrome measurements needed to correct them.
