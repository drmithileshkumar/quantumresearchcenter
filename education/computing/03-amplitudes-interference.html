Presentation slides ↗24 slides · Fullscreen presentation or print to PDF

So far, we have treated computation as a logical procedure that moves between configurations. A machine, however, must carry out those transitions through physical processes. This led us to reversibility: discarding logical information has a thermodynamic cost, whereas a reversible computation keeps enough information to undo each step. We saw how to make such computations possible in principle.

We now turn to quantum computation. Its evolution between measurements is reversible, but a measurement need not give a predictable result. What should replace our description of a computation as a single path through configurations? We will compare deterministic, nondeterministic, and probabilistic transitions, then ask what must change to describe interference.

This chapter develops that picture through paths and their weights. The next expresses the same rules in the language of vectors, matrices, and measurement.

How can two steps that each look random combine into a certain result?

A quantum step can look as random as a coin flip, yet two such steps in a row can give a certain result. Explaining how leads to the central ideas of quantum mechanics.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Understand why probabilities alone do not describe interference, and learn to calculate with amplitudes.

Before you begin

Recall that a computation is a run of configurations, and that a reversible gate can be undone.

What you should be able to do

  • Draw a transition rule as a state diagram, and list its runs as paths in a time-expanded diagram.
  • Apply the product rule along a path and the sum rule across paths.
  • Show why the same two-state probabilistic rule cannot reproduce two Hadamard steps.
  • Use the Born rule, and derive the constraints that preserve total probability.
  • Construct a complex square root of NOT.
  • Explain how a leftover record destroys interference and how uncomputation restores it.

The example to follow

The same bit, updated twice, runs through the whole chapter. Its quantum version, two Hadamard steps, returns in Deutsch’s algorithm.

Scope and assumptions

The Born rule and unitary evolution are physical postulates. The examples motivate them; they do not derive quantum mechanics from classical probability. The next chapter explains the state after measurement.

3.1

States and Transitions

Deterministic, nondeterministic, and probabilistic computations look different. To compare them, we first describe what we know about a register at a given time, its state, and find that all three kinds of knowledge take the same form. We then ask the same question under each kind of rule: from a given start, where can a two-step run end? Doing the calculation three times shows what the three kinds of rule have in common.

3.1.1 States§

configuration · state · weight · definite

Take a register holding one bit. Its possible contents, \(0\) and \(1\), are its configurations. At any moment the register holds exactly one of them.

Do we always know which? We may have set the bit ourselves, and then we know it. We may know only that either configuration is possible. Or the bit may have been set by a fair coin, so that each configuration has probability \(1/2\). What we know about the register at a given time is its state.

All three kinds of knowledge take the same form: a number \(v_j\) for each configuration \(j\), its weight in the state. To keep each weight beside its configuration, we write configuration \(j\) as \(\ket j\), read “ket \(j\),” and the state as a weighted sum of these kets,

\[v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1.\]

For now this is bookkeeping: each term pairs a configuration with its weight. Terms for different configurations stay separate, while two terms for the same configuration combine by adding their weights, as in \(\tfrac14\ket0+\tfrac14\ket0=\tfrac12\ket0\).

What we know\(v_0\)\(v_1\)State
The bit is \(0\)\(1\)\(0\)\(\ket0\)
Either value is possible\(1\)\(1\)\(\ket0+\ket1\)
A fair coin set the bit\(1/2\)\(1/2\)\(\tfrac12\ket0+\tfrac12\ket1\)

The weights mean different things in different rows. A weight \(1\) or \(0\) records whether a configuration is possible; a probability records how often it occurs. A state with weight \(1\) on one configuration and \(0\) on the others is definite, and we write it simply as that configuration’s ket, such as \(\ket0\).

Problems

  1. A two-bit register has configurations \(00,01,10,11\). Write the weights of its state when (a) the register is known to hold \(10\); (b) the first bit is known to be \(1\) and the second could be either; (c) both bits were set by independent fair coins.

    Show solutionHide solution

    (a) \(v_{10}=1\) and the other three weights are \(0\). (b) \(v_{10}=v_{11}=1\), and \(v_{00}=v_{01}=0\): these weights record which configurations are possible. (c) Each configuration has probability \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14\), so all four weights are \(1/4\).

  2. Someone describes a bit by the weights \(v_0=v_1=1\). Can these weights be probabilities? What do they say about the bit, and what do they leave unsaid? Give two different probability states that are consistent with them.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Probabilities of the two configurations must add to \(1\), and these add to \(2\), so they are not probabilities. They record that both values are possible and nothing more: they say nothing about how often each occurs. The states with \(v_0=v_1=1/2\) and with \(v_0=1/10\), \(v_1=9/10\) are both consistent with them, as is any pair of positive probabilities adding to \(1\).

  3. A register has \(k\) bits. A possibility state gives each configuration weight \(0\) or \(1\), with at least one weight \(1\). How many possibility states are there? How many probability states are there? Which probability states are consistent with the possibility state in which every configuration is possible?

    Show solutionHide solution

    There are \(2^k\) configurations, and a possibility state is fixed by the set of configurations with weight \(1\). Any nonempty set will do, so there are \(2^{2^k}-1\) possibility states. Probability states are infinitely many: any nonnegative weights adding to \(1\) give one, and they vary continuously. A probability state is consistent with “every configuration is possible” exactly when every one of its weights is positive. The possibility state keeps only which weights are nonzero.

3.1.2 Transitions§

transition rule · state diagram · weight · run · time-expanded diagram · path

A computation changes the register step by step. A transition rule says which configurations may follow each one. As an example, take NOT, which flips the bit.

We draw a rule as a state diagram: a circle for each configuration, and an arrow \(i\to j\) whenever the rule lets \(j\) follow \(i\). Each arrow carries a weight. For a step \(S\), we write the weight of the arrow from \(i\) to \(j\) as

\[\bra jS\ket i,\]

with the final configuration on the left, the step in the middle, and the initial configuration on the right. Read it from right to left, in the order things happen: start at \(i\), apply \(S\), arrive at \(j\). A missing arrow has weight zero. Writing \(X\) for NOT, \(\bra1X\ket0=\bra0X\ket1=1\), because the step certainly follows those arrows, while \(\bra0X\ket0=\bra1X\ket1=0\).

Applying a rule step by step produces a run \(c_0,c_1,c_2\), where \(c_t\) is the configuration after \(t\) steps. From \(0\), NOT gives \(0,1,0\). To see a run in a diagram, copy the configurations once for each time \(t=0,1,2\) and join the copies by the arrows of the rule. In this time-expanded diagram, a run is a path from time \(0\) to time \(2\).

Rules differ in how many arrows leave each configuration and in what their weights mean. These two differences separate the three kinds of rule that follow.

Problems

  1. A rule on the configurations \(0,1,2\) sends \(0\to1\), \(1\to2\), and \(2\to0\). Draw its state diagram and write the run from \(0\) for four steps.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The diagram has three circles and the arrows \(0\to1\), \(1\to2\), \(2\to0\), each with weight \(1\). The run from \(0\) is \(0,1,2,0,1\): the configuration returns to \(0\) every three steps.

  2. For the same rule, draw the time-expanded diagram for the times \(t=0,1,2\). How many arrows does it contain, and which path is the run from \(2\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each of the two steps, from \(t=0\) to \(1\) and from \(t=1\) to \(2\), repeats the three arrows of the rule, so the diagram has \(6\) arrows. The run from \(2\) is \(2,0,1\), the path from \(2\) at time \(0\) to \(0\) at time \(1\) to \(1\) at time \(2\).

  3. A rule on \(m\) configurations has \(a\) arrows in its state diagram. How many arrows does its time-expanded diagram have for the times \(0,1,\dots,t\)? Show that the runs of \(t\) steps are exactly the paths from time \(0\) to time \(t\), and explain why the time-expanded diagram never contains a cycle, even when the state diagram does.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each of the \(t\) steps repeats the \(a\) arrows, so there are \(ta\) arrows. A run \(c_0,\dots,c_t\) uses an allowed arrow \(c_s\to c_{s+1}\) at every step, which is exactly an arrow from \(c_s\) at time \(s\) to \(c_{s+1}\) at time \(s+1\); conversely, a path from time \(0\) to time \(t\) visits one configuration at each time, joined by allowed arrows, which is a run. Every arrow of the time-expanded diagram goes from time \(s\) to time \(s+1\), so no path can return to an earlier time: a cycle of the state diagram, such as RESET’s arrow \(0\to0\), becomes a chain of arrows moving forward in time.

3.1.3 Deterministic transitions§

deterministic · reversible

Compare NOT with RESET, which writes \(0\) whatever the bit holds.

ConfigurationNext under NOTNext under RESET
\(0\)\(1\)\(0\)
\(1\)\(0\)\(0\)

From \(0\), NOT gives the run \(0,1,0\) and RESET gives \(0,0,0\). Under both rules, exactly one arrow leaves each configuration: for each \(i\), one weight \(\bra jS\ket i\) is \(1\) and the rest are \(0\). The start therefore fixes the whole run, and both rules are deterministic. From a definite state, the state stays definite at every step.

Can a run also be traced backward? Under NOT, exactly one arrow enters each configuration, so the previous bit is always known. Under RESET, two arrows enter \(0\), and a run that reaches \(0\) may have come from either bit. A deterministic rule with exactly one arrow entering each configuration is reversible.

Problems

  1. On two bits, the rule SWAP exchanges the bits: \(01\to10\) and \(10\to01\), while \(00\) and \(11\) stay as they are. Write the two-step run from \(01\). Is SWAP deterministic? Is it reversible?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The run is \(01,10,01\). Exactly one arrow leaves each configuration, so SWAP is deterministic. Exactly one arrow also enters each configuration: \(00\) from \(00\), \(01\) from \(10\), \(10\) from \(01\), and \(11\) from \(11\). So SWAP is reversible; in fact, applying it twice restores every configuration, so it is its own inverse.

  2. On the configurations \(0,1,2,3\), the rule INC adds \(1\) modulo \(4\), and the rule HALVE replaces \(n\) by \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), the integer part of \(n/2\). Draw both state diagrams, write the two-step runs from \(3\), and decide which rule is reversible.

    Show solutionHide solution

    INC has the arrows \(0\to1\to2\to3\to0\), so the run from \(3\) is \(3,0,1\). HALVE has the arrows \(0\to0\), \(1\to0\), \(2\to1\), and \(3\to1\), so the run from \(3\) is \(3,1,0\). Both are deterministic. INC is reversible: each configuration has exactly one incoming arrow, and subtracting \(1\) undoes it. HALVE is not: two arrows enter \(0\) and two enter \(1\), while none enters \(2\) or \(3\). From the end \(0\) of a run we cannot tell whether it started at \(0\), \(1\), \(2\), or \(3\).

  3. Prove that a deterministic rule on finitely many configurations is reversible exactly when every configuration has at least one incoming arrow. Then show that the statement fails for the rule \(n\mapsto\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) on all nonnegative integers.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Suppose there are \(N\) configurations. A deterministic rule has exactly one outgoing arrow at each, so \(N\) arrows in total, and these \(N\) arrows enter the \(N\) configurations. If every configuration receives at least one, none can receive two, since that would require more than \(N\) arrows. So each receives exactly one, and the rule is reversible. Conversely, a reversible rule gives every configuration exactly one incoming arrow, hence at least one. For \(n\mapsto\lfloor n/2\rfloor\) on all nonnegative integers, every \(n\) has an incoming arrow, from \(2n\); yet \(2n\) and \(2n+1\) both go to \(n\), so the rule is not reversible. The counting argument needs finitely many configurations.

3.1.4 Nondeterministic transitions§

nondeterministic · reachable

Nondeterminism relaxes the requirement of a unique successor. A configuration may have several permitted successors, so specifying the present no longer specifies a unique future. The rule describes a set of possible runs rather than a single run.

For a bit, consider KEEP-OR-FLIP: from either configuration, both \(0\) and \(1\) are permitted. Its state diagram has all four arrows. Starting from \(0\), two steps admit four paths:

\[0\to0\to0,\qquad0\to1\to0,\qquad0\to0\to1,\qquad0\to1\to1.\]

A path is possible when every transition along it is permitted. A configuration is reachable when at least one possible path ends there. Both zero and one are therefore reachable after two steps. In a nondeterministic model of computation, acceptance is defined in the same way: an input is accepted if at least one permitted run accepts it.

This is a statement about possibility, not a mechanism for choosing among paths. Even if more paths reached one endpoint than another, the rule would not make that endpoint more likely. Counting possibilities and assigning probabilities are different tasks. To predict how often a future occurs, we need more information on the arrows.

Problems

  1. A rule on \(\{0,1\}\) allows \(0\to1\), \(1\to0\), and \(1\to1\), and forbids \(0\to0\). List the possible two-step runs from \(0\), and the configurations reachable at time \(2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    From \(0\) the only allowed arrow is \(0\to1\); from \(1\), both \(1\to0\) and \(1\to1\) are allowed. The possible runs are \(0\to1\to0\) and \(0\to1\to1\). Both \(0\) and \(1\) are reachable at time \(2\), each by one path.

  2. For the same rule, list the possible three-step runs from \(0\). Which configurations are reachable at time \(3\), and by how many paths?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Extend each two-step run by an allowed arrow. From \(0\to1\to0\), only \(0\to1\) is allowed, giving \(0\to1\to0\to1\). From \(0\to1\to1\), both arrows are allowed, giving \(0\to1\to1\to0\) and \(0\to1\to1\to1\). So \(1\) is reachable by two paths and \(0\) by one. The counts differ, but neither configuration is “more possible”: both are simply reachable.

  3. On the configurations \(0,1,\dots,k\), a rule allows \(i\to i\) and \(i\to i+1\) for \(i\lt k\), and only \(k\to k\) at \(k\). Show that \(k\) is reachable from \(0\) at time \(t\) exactly when \(t\geq k\), and that the number of paths from \(0\) to \(k\) at time \(t\) is \(\binom tk\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each step either stays or advances by one, so reaching \(k\) needs \(k\) advances, and \(k\) is reachable at time \(t\) exactly when \(t\geq k\). Once at \(k\), every later step must stay. A path from \(0\) to \(k\) at time \(t\) is therefore fixed by choosing which \(k\) of the \(t\) steps advance: the last advance may fall anywhere, and the forced stays afterward are consistent with the choice. Every choice of \(k\) steps out of \(t\) gives exactly one allowed path, so there are \(\binom tk\) of them. The number of paths grows quickly with \(t\), but it measures only how many ways there are to reach \(k\), not how likely \(k\) is.

3.1.5 Probabilistic transitions§

probabilistic · conditional probability · Markov

A probabilistic rule supplies the information that nondeterminism leaves unspecified: the probability of each next configuration, given the present one. The weight of the arrow from \(i\) to \(j\) is now the probability of \(j\) given \(i\), written \(P(j\mid i)\): \(\bra jS\ket i=P(j\mid i)\). Since some next configuration must occur, the weights leaving each configuration add to one, \(\sum_j\bra jS\ket i=1\).

The current configuration is assumed to contain all the information needed for this prediction. If earlier history matters, that history must be included in the configuration. With this assumption, the process is called Markov.

Consider COIN: whatever the bit currently holds, replace it by the result of a fresh fair coin flip. Its permitted arrows are the same as those of KEEP-OR-FLIP, but each now carries probability \(1/2\): \(\bra j{\mathrm{COIN}}\ket i=1/2\) for every \(i\) and \(j\).

For the path \(0\to1\to0\), the first step has probability \(1/2\), and the second has conditional probability \(1/2\) given the intermediate configuration. The path probability is therefore \(1/4\). The other path to zero, \(0\to0\to0\), also has probability \(1/4\). These are mutually exclusive histories, so their probabilities add:

\[P(c_2=0)=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12,\]

where \(c_2\) is the configuration after two steps. The probability of ending at one is also \(1/2\). Unlike the deterministic rule, COIN does not fix the outcome of a single run. Unlike the nondeterministic rule, it predicts the distribution of outcomes over independently repeated runs.

Problems

  1. A step keeps the bit with probability \(2/3\) and flips it with probability \(1/3\). Starting from \(0\), compute \(P(c_2=0)\) by listing the paths that end at \(0\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Two paths end at \(0\). The path \(0\to0\to0\) has probability \((2/3)(2/3)=4/9\), and \(0\to1\to0\) has probability \((1/3)(1/3)=1/9\). They cannot both happen, so \(P(c_2=0)=4/9+1/9=5/9\).

  2. For the same rule, compute \(P(c_3=0)\) from \(0\). Check your answer against the formula \(P(c_t=0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\left(\tfrac13\right)^t\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    After two steps the probabilities are \(5/9\) for \(0\) and \(4/9\) for \(1\). A third step reaches \(0\) by keeping \(0\) or by flipping \(1\): \[P(c_3=0)=\tfrac59\cdot\tfrac23+\tfrac49\cdot\tfrac13=\tfrac{10}{27}+\tfrac4{27}=\tfrac{14}{27}.\] The formula gives \(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac1{27}=14/27\). They agree.

  3. Prove the formula \(P(c_t=0)=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\left(\tfrac13\right)^t\) for the rule above, and describe what happens to the run’s memory of its starting configuration as \(t\) grows.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Write \(p_t=P(c_t=0)\). The run is at \(0\) at time \(t+1\) if it was at \(0\) and kept, or at \(1\) and flipped: \(p_{t+1}=\tfrac23p_t+\tfrac13(1-p_t)=\tfrac13p_t+\tfrac13\). Subtracting \(\tfrac12\) from both sides gives \(p_{t+1}-\tfrac12=\tfrac13\bigl(p_t-\tfrac12\bigr)\). Since \(p_0-\tfrac12=\tfrac12\), induction gives \(p_t-\tfrac12=\tfrac12\left(\tfrac13\right)^t\). As \(t\) grows, \(p_t\) approaches \(\tfrac12\), whatever the start: each step shrinks the difference between the two starting points by a factor of three, so the run gradually forgets where it began.

3.2

Sum and Product Rules

What is the result of a computation once states and transitions carry weights? It is the final state: a weight on each configuration at the last time. That weight answers the question each kind of rule asks about the end of a run. For a deterministic rule it is \(1\) at the configuration reached and \(0\) elsewhere. For a nondeterministic rule it is the number of runs that end at the configuration. A state of such a rule recorded only \(0\) or \(1\); the sum rule now counts runs, and only whether the count is nonzero matters: a nonzero count means the configuration is reachable. For a probabilistic rule it is the probability of ending there. The sum and product rules compute this weight from the weights of the transitions: the product rule gives the weight of a single path, and the sum rule combines the paths that end at the same configuration.

The three models differ in what they say about the future, but a run has the same structure in each: a sequence of transitions between configurations. This lets us separate two questions. What does a particular path contribute, and how do the paths to the same endpoint combine? We first assign weights to individual transitions, then use products along paths and sums across paths. The meaning of the weights will depend on the model; the calculation will have a common form.

3.2.1 The product rule along a path§

product rule

A path specifies every intermediate configuration, so following it requires every transition along it. Suppose a step \(S\) is followed by a step \(T\). The path \(i\to j\to k\) uses the arrow from \(i\) to \(j\) under \(S\), then the arrow from \(j\) to \(k\) under \(T\). Its weight is the product

\[\bra kT\ket j\,\bra jS\ket i.\]

Read from right to left, the factors appear in the order the steps happen: the first step stands next to the initial configuration. When the same rule acts twice, \(T=S\).

For deterministic and nondeterministic rules, let an allowed arrow have weight one and a missing arrow weight zero. The product is one precisely when the whole path is permitted: a single missing transition makes it zero.

For a probabilistic rule, use the transition probabilities as weights. Multiplication then gives the probability of that particular history. The factors are conditional probabilities for successive steps; they need not describe independent events. Their dependence on the past is already accounted for by the current configuration.

For COIN, each two-step path has weight \((1/2)(1/2)=1/4\). A path of \(t\) steps has weight \(2^{-t}\). This determines the contribution of one history. An endpoint may be reached through several histories, whose contributions must still be combined.

3.2.2 The sum rule across paths§

sum rule

An endpoint specifies where the run finishes without specifying how it got there. To calculate its weight, we therefore sum the contributions of all paths that reach it. For two steps from \(i\) to \(k\), the paths differ only in the intermediate configuration \(j\):

\[\bra kTS\ket i=\sum_j\bra kT\ket j\,\bra jS\ket i.\]

Each term accounts for one route; the sum accounts for all routes to the chosen endpoint. The left side treats the two steps together as one combined step \(TS\), first \(S\) and then \(T\), with this total as the weight of its arrow from \(i\) to \(k\). As before, the step applied first stands on the right.

For a deterministic rule, only one path from the start is permitted. The sum is one at its endpoint and zero elsewhere. For a nondeterministic rule, the sum counts permitted paths. A positive count establishes reachability, but is not a probability.

For a probabilistic rule, the histories are mutually exclusive, so the sum is the endpoint probability. Writing \(P_2(k\mid i)=\bra kSS\ket i\) for the two-step probability, we have

\[P_2(k\mid i)=\sum_jP(k\mid j)P(j\mid i).\]

For COIN, this is \(1/4+1/4=1/2\) at either endpoint. In all three models, every contribution is nonnegative. Adding a permitted path can therefore increase an endpoint’s total weight or leave it unchanged, but cannot reduce it. This restriction will matter when we encounter quantum transitions.

3.2.3 States and steps§

So far each calculation began from a definite state. The same rules apply to any state \(\sum_iv_i\ket i\), with weight \(v_i\) on each configuration \(i\).

During a step, the contribution from \(i\) to \(j\) is \(\bra jS\ket i\,v_i\). Summing over all starting configurations gives the new weight at \(j\):

\[v^\prime_j=\sum_i\bra jS\ket i\,v_i.\]

Kets record the same calculation. A step sends a definite configuration to the weighted sum of its successors,

\[S\ket i=\sum_j\bra jS\ket i\,\ket j,\]

so that, for example, \(\mathrm{COIN}\ket0=\tfrac12\ket0+\tfrac12\ket1\). A general state goes to \(\sum_iv_i\,S\ket i\): each configuration’s successors are weighted by \(v_i\), and terms that land on the same configuration add. That addition is the sum rule; collecting the weight of \(\ket j\) gives exactly \(v'_j\).

The state thus collects the information needed to predict the next step, without retaining a separate list of all earlier paths. For COIN, \(v^\prime_0=v^\prime_1=(v_0+v_1)/2=1/2\). Every initial probability distribution becomes the same fair distribution.

Whether a step can be undone depends on what this update preserves. A reversible deterministic rule permutes the configurations; reversing its arrows restores every initial state. CNOT is an example on two bits, where each two-bit configuration such as \(00\) gets its own ket: \(\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{00}=\ket{00}\), \(\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{01}=\ket{01}\), \(\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{10}=\ket{11}\), and \(\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{11}=\ket{10}\).

RESET and COIN instead send distinct initial states to the same final state. No rule acting only on that final state can recover which preparation was used. To undo such a process, we would need additional information outside this description.

3.3

Signed Weights

What changes if weights can be negative? We keep multiplying along paths and adding across paths. The new feature is cancellation: nonzero contributions can sum to zero. We first follow the signs through these rules, then construct a step in which cancellation restores the starting state.

3.3.1 Negative weights§

signed contribution · product rule

So far, weights have counted paths or described probabilities. In either case they were nonnegative. Let us keep the same sum and product rules, but allow a state or an arrow to carry a negative real weight. This is a mathematical extension of our path description; its physical interpretation will come later.

The product rule still assigns a path the product of its arrow weights. For a path \(i\to j\to k\) under a step \(S\) applied twice, the weight is

\[\bra kS\ket j\,\bra jS\ket i.\]

The sign now matters. A path with an odd number of negative arrow weights has negative weight; a path with an even number has positive weight. For example, \((1/2)(-1/2)=-1/4\), while \((-1/2)(-1/2)=1/4\). A negative arrow can therefore reverse the sign of a contribution that was already negative.

If the starting configuration itself has weight \(v_i\), its contribution along this path is \(\bra kS\ket j\,\bra jS\ket i\,v_i\). We include the sign of \(v_i\) as well as those of the arrows.

A negative path weight is not a negative probability or a negative number of paths. It is a signed contribution to a later sum. The path remains present in the diagram; what changes is how its contribution combines with others.

3.3.2 Cancellation across paths§

sum rule · cancellation

The sum rule still adds the contributions of all paths with the same start and endpoint. With two intermediate configurations,

\[\bra kSS\ket i=\bra kS\ket{j_1}\,\bra{j_1}S\ket i+\bra kS\ket{j_2}\,\bra{j_2}S\ket i.\]

Write the first pair of arrow weights as \(a,c\) and the second as \(b,d\). The two path contributions are \(ca\) and \(db\), and the total is \(ca+db\). If \(a=b=c=1/2\) and \(d=-1/2\), then

\[\bra kSS\ket i=\frac14-\frac14=0.\]

Both paths have nonzero weight, yet their combined contribution vanishes. This is cancellation. With nonnegative weights, a sum can vanish only when every term vanishes. Signed weights allow a zero total even when individual terms are nonzero.

Cancellation need not be complete: \(1/4-1/8=1/8\). Contributions with the same sign reinforce one another: \(-1/4-1/4=-1/2\). Adding a path can now decrease the total weight, and removing a negative contribution can increase it. The sum of the weights is no longer required to be one or even positive.

3.3.3 The Hadamard step§

Hadamard operation

We can use cancellation to construct a step that spreads a definite input over two configurations, yet undoes itself when repeated. Call the step \(H\). Give three arrows weight \(c\) and the remaining arrow weight \(-c\):

\[\bra0H\ket0=\bra1H\ket0=\bra0H\ket1=c,\qquad \bra1H\ket1=-c.\]

Start at \(0\) with weight one. After one step the state is \(H\ket0=c\ket0+c\ket1\). After two steps, the paths \(0\to0\to0\) and \(0\to1\to0\) each contribute \(c^2\), giving \(2c^2\) at \(0\). The paths \(0\to0\to1\) and \(0\to1\to1\) contribute \(c^2\) and \(-c^2\), giving zero at \(1\).

To recover the original weight one, choose \(2c^2=1\), or \(c=1/\sqrt2\). This gives the Hadamard operation:

\[\bra0H\ket0=\bra1H\ket0=\bra0H\ket1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\qquad \bra1H\ket1=-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}.\]

In ket form, the step sends each configuration to

\[H\ket0=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2},\qquad H\ket1=\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

Each term records one arrow: its coefficient is the arrow weight \(\bra jH\ket i\). The minus sign before \(\ket1\) in \(H\ket1\) is the negative arrow, and it will matter when a later step adds contributions that reach the same configuration.

The factor \(1/\sqrt2\) has been fixed by composition: two applications must return the original weights. Starting at \(1\) provides the other check. The paths to \(0\) cancel; the paths to \(1\) reinforce, including the path with two negative arrows, whose product is positive. Thus \(\bra kHH\ket i\) is one when \(k=i\) and zero otherwise: \(HH=I\), where \(I\) is the identity step, whose only arrows are self-loops of weight one, \(\bra jI\ket i=1\) for \(j=i\) and \(0\) otherwise.

Notice what this choice does to the weights after one step. From either definite input, their squares add to one:

\[c^2+c^2=c^2+(-c)^2=1.\]

The weights themselves may be negative, and their sum need not be one. Their squares, however, are nonnegative and here give \(1/2\) for each configuration. This suggests using squared weights as probabilities, while retaining the signed weights for composition and cancellation. It is a motivation for the quantum probability rule, rather than a derivation of it; we state that rule when we connect these transitions to physical outcomes.

For a general input state, the update is

\[H(v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1)=\frac{v_0+v_1}{\sqrt2}\ket0+\frac{v_0-v_1}{\sqrt2}\ket1.\]

Applying the same update again gives back \(v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\). The intermediate weights retain enough information to recover the input: \(H\ket0\) and \(H\ket1\) differ only in the sign in front of \(\ket1\), and that sign determines which endpoint survives the second step.

If we replace the negative arrow by a positive one, the two contributions to each endpoint reinforce. Both inputs then produce the same intermediate weights, and the second step cannot recover the starting configuration. Cancellation is what makes the signed step reversible.

Negative real weights have given us opposite signs and cancellation. Could a continuously varying direction take the place of this two-way choice of sign?

3.4

Complex Amplitudes and Interference

The signs of real amplitudes retain a distinction that probabilities discard. Complex amplitudes extend that distinction from a choice of sign to a continuously varying phase. We examine what this extension permits by constructing a square root of NOT, then ask how phase affects an observable outcome. Finally, we examine whether another part of the system keeps a record that distinguishes the contributing paths, changing which complete configurations the paths reach.

3.4.1 Complex weights§

amplitude · conjugate · squared magnitude · phase

The Hadamard operation satisfies \(H^2=I\). A square root of NOT is an operation \(\sqrt X\) satisfying \(\sqrt X\sqrt X=X\), where \(X\) denotes NOT. Neither deterministic reversible operation on one bit has this property: both the identity and NOT square to the identity.

Let \(\sqrt X\) have stay-weight \(a=\bra0\sqrt X\ket0=\bra1\sqrt X\ket1\) and switch-weight \(b=\bra1\sqrt X\ket0=\bra0\sqrt X\ket1\), the same from either configuration. In two steps, staying twice or switching twice returns to the initial configuration, with total weight \(a^2+b^2\). Staying then switching or switching then staying reaches the opposite configuration, with total weight \(ab+ba=2ab\). For the combined operation to be NOT,

\[a^2+b^2=0,\qquad2ab=1.\]

For real \(a,b\), the first equation forces both to vanish, contradicting the second. Can a number have a negative square? Not on the number line, but in the plane. Picture a complex number \(u+iv\) as the point \((u,v)\), and add two numbers by adding their points as arrows. Multiplying by \(i\) turns an arrow a quarter turn, since \(i(u+iv)=-v+iu\). Two quarter turns make a half turn, which sends every arrow to its opposite: \(i^2=-1\). Squares of complex numbers can therefore cancel each other. Choose

\[a=\frac{1+i}{2},\qquad b=\frac{1-i}{2}.\]

Then \(a^2=i/2\), \(b^2=-i/2\), and \(2ab=1\). The two-step transformation is exactly NOT. Weights that may be complex are called amplitudes; they obey the same sum and product rules as real weights.

The weights of \(\sqrt X\) now give the desired composition, but we still need to associate them with probabilities. In the real-weight example, squaring gave a nonnegative number. Here the squares \(a^2=i/2\) and \(b^2=-i/2\) cannot serve that purpose. Multiplying a complex weight by its conjugate offers a natural extension: if \(z=u+iv\), its conjugate is \(z^*=u-iv\), and

\[zz^*=(u+iv)(u-iv)=u^2+v^2=|z|^2.\]

This is the squared magnitude of \(z\). It is real and nonnegative, and becomes the ordinary square when the weight is real. We can therefore propose that a configuration with amplitude \(v_j\) has probability

\[p(j)=v_jv_j^*=|v_j|^2.\]

With this assignment, the requirement that probabilities sum to one becomes a condition on the amplitudes:

\[\sum_jp(j)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_j|v_j|^2=1.\]

We call this normalization. For the two outgoing weights of \(\sqrt X\), \(|a|^2=|b|^2=1/2\), so their probabilities do sum to one. The same assignment also gives the probabilities \(1/2,1/2\) for a Hadamard step. Thus it agrees with the real-weight example and accommodates the complex weights just constructed.

A computation involves more than one step, however. The probability assignment must remain consistent as weights multiply along paths and add at their endpoints. Multiplication has a useful property here:

\[|z_1z_2|^2=(z_1z_2)(z_1z_2)^*=|z_1|^2|z_2|^2.\]

The squared magnitude of a path product is the product of the squared magnitudes of its factors. When several paths meet, their amplitudes must still be added before assigning a probability. For two contributions,

\[|A+B|^2=|A|^2+|B|^2+AB^*+A^*B.\]

The last two terms retain the dependence on how the contributions combine. In two steps of \(\sqrt X\), the return contributions are \(A=i/2\) and \(B=-i/2\); their sum is zero, and so is the return probability. Assigning separate probabilities and adding them would give \(1/4+1/4=1/2\), losing the cancellation. The distinction is therefore between adding amplitudes at a common endpoint and adding probabilities over distinct final configurations.

This cancellation changes the probability of a particular configuration. The total over all final configurations must nevertheless remain one. That places a restriction on the transition weights. If the input amplitudes are \(v_i\), the sum and product rules give, for a step \(S\),

\[v'_j=\sum_i\bra jS\ket i\,v_i.\]

For a definite input \(i\), only \(v_i=1\) is nonzero, so the outgoing amplitudes are simply \(\bra jS\ket i\). Normalization then requires

\[\sum_j|\bra jS\ket i|^2=1\]

for each input configuration. This checks the transition on definite inputs. With several nonzero input amplitudes, contributions from different starting configurations combine at each endpoint, producing the same kind of cross terms as \(A+B\) above. Summing over endpoints gives

\[\sum_j|v'_j|^2=\sum_i|v_i|^2\sum_j|\bra jS\ket i|^2+\sum_{i\ne k}v_i^*v_k\sum_j\bigl(\bra jS\ket i\bigr)^*\bra jS\ket k.\]

The outgoing normalization condition makes the first term equal to the original total \(\sum_i|v_i|^2\). The second term must therefore vanish for every choice of input amplitudes. Since their relative magnitudes and complex directions can vary, this requires

\[\sum_j\bigl(\bra jS\ket i\bigr)^*\bra jS\ket k=0\qquad(i\ne k).\]

This cross-sum condition allows contributions to reinforce or cancel at individual endpoints while ensuring that their cross terms cancel in the total. Together with outgoing normalization, it makes the proposed probability assignment compatible with the amplitude update. Each step takes a normalized input to a normalized output, so a sequence of such steps preserves total probability.

The step \(\sqrt X\) satisfies both requirements. Its outgoing squared magnitudes sum to \(|a|^2+|b|^2=1\), and the cross sum for its two inputs is

\[a^*b+b^*a=-\frac i2+\frac i2=0.\]

It therefore preserves normalization for arbitrary input amplitudes, as well as for the definite inputs used in its construction. After two steps, the return amplitude \(a^2+b^2=0\) gives probability zero, and the switch amplitude \(2ab=1\) gives probability one. Both the one-step probabilities and the composition into NOT fit the proposed rule.

How can we describe the direction of an amplitude in the plane? A nonzero amplitude is an arrow of some length \(r\) at some angle \(\phi\) from the positive real axis. We write the unit arrow at angle \(\phi\) as \(e^{i\phi}=\cos\phi+i\sin\phi\), so that \(z=re^{i\phi}\). Here \(r=|z|\) is its magnitude and \(\phi\) its phase. Multiplying two unit arrows and using the angle-addition formulas gives \(e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}=\cos(\alpha+\beta)+i\sin(\alpha+\beta)=e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}\). The probability \(|z|^2=r^2\) retains the magnitude but discards the phase. Positive and negative real weights correspond to phases zero and \(\pi\); complex weights allow the intervening angles. Multiplication adds phases, and addition depends on their relative values. This is how two amplitude lists with the same probabilities can give different probabilities after a further transition.

    To \(0\): \[\bra0\sqrt X\ket1\,\bra0\sqrt X\ket0+\bra1\sqrt X\ket1\,\bra0\sqrt X\ket1=\tfrac14\bigl((1-i)(1+i)+(1+i)(1-i)\bigr)=1.\] To \(1\): \[\bra0\sqrt X\ket1\,\bra1\sqrt X\ket0+\bigl(\bra1\sqrt X\ket1\bigr)^2=\tfrac14\bigl((1-i)^2+(1+i)^2\bigr)=0.\] Two steps of \(\sqrt X\) send \(1\) to \(0\), as NOT does.

  3. Find every operation with \(S(0\to0)=S(1\to1)=a\) and \(S(0\to1)=S(1\to0)=b\) whose two steps give NOT. Show in particular that \(a\) and \(b\) cannot both be real.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Two steps from \(0\) give weight \(a^2+b^2\) at \(0\) and \(2ab\) at \(1\), and from \(1\) the same by symmetry. We need \(a^2+b^2=0\) and \(2ab=1\). If \(a\) and \(b\) were real, the first equation would force \(a=b=0\), contradicting the second. Over the complex numbers, \(a^2=-b^2\) gives \(a=\pm ib\). With \(a=ib\), \(2ib^2=1\), so \(b^2=-i/2\) and \(b=\pm(1-i)/2\), \(a=\pm(1+i)/2\). With \(a=-ib\), \(b^2=i/2\), so \(b=\pm(1+i)/2\) and \(a=\pm(1-i)/2\). There are four solutions: \(S\), \(-S\), and the two operations obtained from them by conjugating every weight. Each is unitary.

3.4.2 Interference and phase§

interference · coherent · global phase · relative phase

When paths end at the same configuration, we add their amplitudes and then take the squared magnitude. The probability rule therefore gives

\[p(k)=\Bigl|\sum_{\text{paths to }k}\text{path amplitude}\Bigr|^2.\]

The contributions can reinforce or cancel. For two Hadamard steps starting at \(0\), the two paths to \(0\) each contribute \(1/2\). They add to one, giving probability one. The two paths to \(1\) contribute \(1/2\) and \(-1/2\). They cancel, giving probability zero.

Taking the squared magnitude of each path separately would give \(1/4+1/4=1/2\) at either endpoint. Adding the amplitudes first raises one probability and lowers the other. This effect is called interference. It is constructive when the probability is greater than the sum of the separate squared magnitudes, and destructive when it is smaller. Paths whose amplitudes combine in this way are called coherent.

For complex amplitudes, phase determines how the contributions combine. Multiplying every amplitude by the same factor \(e^{i\theta}\) changes all their phases by the same amount. Every later sum acquires that same factor, and its squared magnitude stays unchanged because \(|e^{i\theta}|=1\). Such a common phase is called a global phase. It has no effect on the probabilities, even after further transitions.

The phase difference between two amplitudes is their relative phase. Changing it can change their sum. For example, take the state

\[\frac{\ket0+e^{i\phi}\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

Both squared magnitudes are \(1/2\), whatever the value of \(\phi\). But the two contributions arriving at \(0\) after a Hadamard step are \(1/2\) and \(e^{i\phi}/2\). Their sum depends on \(\phi\), so

\[p(0)=\left|\frac{1+e^{i\phi}}2\right|^2=\frac{1+\cos\phi}{2}.\]

At \(\phi=0\), the contributions have the same sign and add to one. At \(\phi=\pi\), they have opposite signs and cancel. At \(\phi=\pi/2\), the probability is \(1/2\). The initial probabilities are the same in all three cases; the relative phase changes the probabilities after \(H\).

Predict first. Predict \(p(0)\) after \(H\) for \(\phi=0\), \(\pi/2\), and \(\pi\). Then change only the global phase \(\theta\): what happens to the arrows, and what happens to the probabilities?

After trying it: compare your explanation

\((1+\cos\phi)/2\) gives \(1\), \(1/2\), and \(0\). A global phase turns both contributions, and so their sum, by the same angle. The lengths of the sums, and therefore the probabilities, do not change.

A simple way to change this phase is to multiply the amplitude at \(1\) by \(-1\), leaving the amplitude at \(0\) unchanged. This is the operation \(Z\), with stay-weights \(\bra0Z\ket0=1\) and \(\bra1Z\ket1=-1\) and no arrows between configurations. It changes the relative phase by \(\pi\). Starting at \(0\), two Hadamard steps return to \(0\); inserting \(Z\) between them gives \(1\) instead. An algorithm can use such phase changes to control which contributions reinforce and which cancel, and so change the final probabilities.

Check your understanding

What goes wrong if we take the squared magnitude of each path amplitude before adding?

Compare your explanation

Taking squared magnitudes first removes the signs and phases that allow cancellation. For the two paths to \(1\) under two Hadamard steps, it gives \(1/4+1/4=1/2\) instead of zero.

Problems

  1. Two paths reach an outcome with amplitudes \(1/2\) and \(i/2\). What is its probability? Is the interference constructive, destructive, or neither?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Adding first, \(|1/2+i/2|^2=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12\). Squaring each path separately also gives \(\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12\). The two contributions point in perpendicular directions in the complex plane, so they neither reinforce nor cancel.

  2. Two paths contribute \(0.6\) and \(-0.2\) to an outcome. Compare the probability obtained by adding first with the result of squaring first.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Adding first gives \((0.4)^2=0.16\). Squaring first gives \(0.36+0.04=0.40\). Partial destructive interference lowers the probability.

  3. Start with amplitudes \((1/\sqrt2,e^{i\phi}/\sqrt2)\) and apply \(H\). The probability of \(0\) is \((1+\cos\phi)/2\). Which values of \(\phi\) give the same probabilities, and what extra operation could distinguish them?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\phi\) and \(-\phi\) have the same cosine, so they give the same probabilities. Before \(H\), multiply the amplitude at configuration \(1\) by \(e^{-i\pi/2}\). This changes the probability to \((1+\sin\phi)/2\), which differs for \(\phi\) and \(-\phi\) unless \(\sin\phi=0\).

3.4.3 Path information§

record · uncomputation

Two Hadamard steps starting at \(0\) return to \(0\). The two contributions to final configuration \(1\) are \(+1/2\) and \(-1/2\), so they cancel. What changes if another part of the machine records which intermediate configuration was visited?

Introduce a second bit as a record, initially zero. A complete configuration now has two labels, \(ij\): \(i\) is the value of the first system and \(j\) the record. Thus \(10\) and \(11\) are different configurations even though their first labels agree. We apply the same path rules to these complete configurations: multiply weights along a path, add amplitudes at a common endpoint, and then take the squared magnitude.

Starting at \(\ket{00}\), apply \(H\) to the first label while leaving the record unchanged. This gives the state \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\). Next record the first label by a controlled-NOT operation: leave the second label alone when the first is zero, and flip it when the first is one. Its transitions are

\[\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{00}=\ket{00},\quad\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{01}=\ket{01},\quad\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{10}=\ket{11},\quad\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{11}=\ket{10},\]

each with weight one. The state is now \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). Apply the final \(H\) to the first label only. From \(00\), it reaches \(00\) and \(10\), with transition weights \(+1/\sqrt2\) each. From \(11\), it reaches \(01\) with weight \(+1/\sqrt2\) and \(11\) with weight \(-1/\sqrt2\).

The final state is

\[\tfrac12\ket{00}+\tfrac12\ket{01}+\tfrac12\ket{10}-\tfrac12\ket{11}.\]

The contributions \(+1/2\) and \(-1/2\) associated with first-label value one end at \(10\) and \(11\). They cannot cancel because these are different complete configurations. Each has probability \(1/4\). If we ask only whether the first label is one, we include both distinct outcomes and add their probabilities:

\[p(\text{first label}=1)=p(10)+p(11)=\frac14+\frac14=\frac12.\]

Ignoring the record does not make its two values the same configuration. The loss of interference here follows from the different endpoints, whether or not anyone reads the record.

We can recover the earlier interference by undoing the recording step before the final \(H\). Controlled-NOT is its own inverse: a second application takes \(00\) back to \(00\) and \(11\) back to \(10\), both with weight one. The record is again zero on both alternatives, and their amplitudes remain \(1/\sqrt2\). Now the final \(H\) has two routes to the same endpoint \(10\), with contributions \(+1/2\) and \(-1/2\). They cancel. The contributions to \(00\) add to one, restoring a certain return to \(00\).

This is uncomputation applied to path information. It means reversing the operation that created the record while preserving the amplitudes, not merely deleting a label from our description. The bookkeeping lesson is that every part of the machine capable of distinguishing the paths belongs in the configuration. Next we treat these weighted sums of kets as vectors and state the physical rules for evolution, measurement, and composite systems.

Problems

  1. After retaining the record, what is the probability of each final configuration? What is the probability that the record is one?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each final amplitude has magnitude \(1/2\), so each complete configuration has probability \(1/4\). The record is one in \(01\) and \(11\), giving total probability \(1/2\).

  2. Follow the routes from the two configurations after recording, \(00\) and \(11\), through the final \(H\). Calculate the four final amplitudes and explain why the contributions to first-label value one do not cancel.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The input amplitude \(1/\sqrt2\) at \(00\) gives \(+1/2\) at both \(00\) and \(10\). The input amplitude \(1/\sqrt2\) at \(11\) gives \(+1/2\) at \(01\) and \(-1/2\) at \(11\). The contributions to first-label value one have different complete endpoints, \(10\) and \(11\), so their probabilities add rather than their amplitudes.

  3. Suppose the recording is undone only after the final \(H\). Track the four amplitudes through this extra controlled-NOT. Does the first label now return to zero with certainty? Explain why the timing matters.

    Show solutionHide solution

    After the final H, the amplitudes in order \(00,01,10,11\) are \((1/2,1/2,1/2,-1/2)\). Controlled-NOT exchanges \(10\) and \(11\), giving \((1/2,1/2,-1/2,1/2)\). The probability that the first label is one remains \(1/2\). Only before recombination does this controlled-NOT undo the original recording: after H, the first label has changed, so the same operation no longer clears the record on both contributing alternatives.

Summary

  • States and transitions share one description. A state assigns a weight to each configuration, written \(\sum_iv_i\ket i\), and a step \(S\) assigns a weight \(\bra jS\ket i\) to each arrow from \(i\) to \(j\). Deterministic, nondeterministic and probabilistic rules differ in how many arrows leave a configuration and in what the weights mean.
  • Endpoints are computed by products and sums. Multiply weights along a path (product rule) and add the paths that end at the same configuration (sum rule). For a whole state, \(v'_j=\sum_i\bra jS\ket i\,v_i\), or in kets \(S\ket i=\sum_j\bra jS\ket i\,\ket j\).
  • Signed weights allow cancellation. With nonnegative weights, adding a path can never lower a total; with signed weights, nonzero contributions can cancel. The Hadamard step uses this to spread a definite input and then undo itself: \(H^2=I\).
  • Complex amplitudes go further. Complex weights permit steps such as a square root of NOT, which no real rule allows. A configuration with amplitude \(v_j\) has probability \(|v_j|^2\). This is a postulate that the examples motivate, not a derivation.
  • Preserving probability constrains every step. For every input state, total probability stays one only if each configuration’s outgoing amplitudes are normalized and the cross sums between different inputs vanish.
  • Add amplitudes first, then square. Paths to a common endpoint interfere, constructively or destructively. A global phase changes no probability, while a relative phase changes the probabilities produced by later steps.
  • Path records destroy interference. A record of which path was taken sends the paths to different complete configurations, so they cannot cancel, whether or not anyone reads the record. Uncomputing the record restores the interference.

A proposed step \(S\) has all four weights \(\bra jS\ket i\) equal to \(1/\sqrt2\). (a) Check the outgoing-normalization condition for each definite input. Hint 2

Each new amplitude is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(v_0+v_1)\).

Worked solution

(a) From \(0\) or from \(1\) the outgoing squared magnitudes are \(\frac12+\frac12=1\), so definite inputs stay normalized. (b) The cross sum is \(\frac12+\frac12=1\neq0\), so the condition fails. 8. Misconception §

A student says: “After one Hadamard step from \(0\), the bit is \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(\frac12\) each. That is the same as a fair coin, so a second Hadamard step should act like a second coin flip, giving \(\frac12,\frac12\) again.” Compute what actually happens, and identify the step in the student’s reasoning that fails.

Hint 1

Add the amplitudes of the two paths to each endpoint before squaring.

Hint 2

Would a fair coin have the same amplitudes, or only the same probabilities?

Worked solution

Two Hadamard steps from \(0\) give amplitude \(\frac12+\frac12=1\) at \(0\) and \(\frac12-\frac12=0\) at \(1\): the result is \(0\) with certainty. The student treated the intermediate state as a probability distribution and combined the two paths by adding probabilities, \(\frac14+\frac14\). But the intermediate state is the amplitude list \((\frac{1}{\sqrt2},\frac{1}{\sqrt2})\), and the paths to each endpoint must be combined by adding amplitudes, which can cancel. Equal probabilities after one step do not determine what happens after the next. The amplitudes carry the signs that make cancellation possible, and a coin’s probabilities do not.

10. Extension §

Find a one-bit step \(A\) that changes only phases (\(\bra1A\ket0=\bra0A\ket1=0\)) and satisfies \(A^2=Z\). How many such steps are there? Then compute the probability of \(0\) after \(H\), \(A\), \(H\) starting at \(0\), and compare it with \(H\), \(Z\), \(H\). What does “half of \(Z\)” do to the interference?

Hint 1

Write \(\bra0A\ket0=p\), \(\bra1A\ket1=q\), and require \(p^2=1\), \(q^2=-1\).

Hint 2

Use \(p(0)=(1+\cos\phi)/2\) for a relative phase \(\phi\).

Worked solution

Two steps of a diagonal step multiply each weight by itself, so \(p^2=1\) and \(q^2=-1\): \(p=\pm1\) and \(q=\pm i\). That gives four steps, and each satisfies normalization because \(|p|=|q|=1\).
