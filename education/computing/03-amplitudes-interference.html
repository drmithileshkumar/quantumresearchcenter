Chapter 03

Probability Amplitudes and Interference

What does it mean to consider alternatives when those alternatives can cancel one another?

Ordinary probability teaches us to add the weights of mutually exclusive possibilities. A two-state experiment makes that familiar rule insufficient for quantum alternatives: identical probabilities at one step can lead to different predictions after the next. We follow the calculation through competing paths, signs, and phases to discover why amplitudes must be combined before probabilities are assigned. The question becomes when alternatives interfere, and when distinguishing them removes that interference.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Replace classical path probabilities with amplitudes and see how interference changes predictions.

Before you begin

Review vectors, matrix multiplication, and the probability of an outcome.

What you should be able to do

  • Add amplitudes before taking squared magnitudes.
  • Calculate two successive Hadamard transformations.
  • Distinguish relative phase from global phase.

The example to follow

The two-Hadamard example returns in Deutsch’s algorithm: the final gate makes relative signs observable.

Scope and assumptions

Coherent alternatives interfere only when their distinguishing records do not remain available in other systems.

3.1

Classical Probability

A probability model specifies both the alternatives and how their weights combine.

3.1.1 Outcomes, distributions, and frequencies§

A classical bit has a value zero or one. If its preparation is uncertain, describe that uncertainty by probabilities \(p_0,p_1\geq0\) with \(p_0+p_1=1\). Repeating the same preparation independently gives outcome frequencies that tend toward these probabilities; a short sample need not match them exactly. The distribution describes uncertainty across outcomes, not a fraction of a bit observed in one trial. This distinction between a predictive description and an individual result remains important in quantum mechanics.

Problems

  1. What must the probabilities of all possible outcomes sum to?

    Show solutionHide solution

    They sum to one for a complete set of mutually exclusive outcomes.

  2. A device produces 0 with probability 0.7. What is the probability of 1 if these are its only outcomes?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The remaining probability is \(1-0.7=0.3\).

  3. A fair coin can produce ten heads in a row. What would such a record tell you about the claim that the coin is fair? Why can a probability model be tested by frequencies without requiring every finite sample to match its probabilities?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Ten specified heads have probability \((1/2)^{10}=1/1024\) for independent fair tosses. This is unlikely but possible, so it is evidence to assess rather than a logical disproof. Statistical tests compare observed patterns with their predicted likelihoods; they do not require each finite sample to reproduce the model’s probabilities exactly.

3.1.2 Conditional probability and path weights§

Suppose a transition has conditional probabilities \(P(j\mid i)\): the probability of next state \(j\) given current state \(i\). The probability of a two-step path \(i\to j\to k\) is the product of its transition probabilities. To obtain the probability of reaching \(k\), sum over the disjoint intermediate alternatives: \[P(k\mid i)=\sum_j P(k\mid j)P(j\mid i),\] when the transition rule depends only on the current state. These are the product and sum rules for a Markov process. A fair randomizing step has every matrix entry \(1/2\); applying it twice still leaves a fair distribution.

Problems

  1. How do you calculate the probability of a route with two successive conditional steps?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Multiply the probability of the first step by the conditional probability of the second given the first.

  2. A route takes its first step with probability \(1/2\) and its second with conditional probability \(1/3\). Calculate its probability.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \((1/2)\times(1/3)=1/6\).

  3. Two different routes lead to the same final outcome. When is adding their route probabilities justified? What assumptions about the routes and their conditional weights are hidden in that calculation?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Route probabilities add when the routes are disjoint classical events. Along each route, conditional probabilities must refer to the actual preceding history. If routes are indistinguishable coherent quantum alternatives, adding their separate probabilities omits interference; their amplitudes must instead be combined first.

3.1.3 Nondeterministic and probabilistic transitions§

A nondeterministic model records permitted successors, not likelihoods. A computation accepts if some permitted branch accepts. Branch weights can be written as zero or one, but their combination uses logical AND along a path and OR across paths. Two successful paths still mean “possible,” not probability two. A probabilistic model instead weights branches and asks how likely acceptance is. Keeping these meanings distinct prevents a common confusion: quantum amplitudes are neither a list of permitted classical guesses nor ordinary probabilities.

Problems

  1. Does a nondeterministic choice specify probabilities?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. Nondeterminism specifies allowed alternatives and an existence condition, not a probability distribution.

  2. A random procedure chooses uniformly among eight paths, one successful. Find its success probability and compare it with the statement “a successful path exists.”

    Show solutionHide solution

    The random procedure succeeds with probability \(1/8\). The nondeterministic statement is simply true because at least one successful path exists; it does not assign the probability \(1/8\).

  3. A nondeterministic description says that a successful path exists. A probabilistic procedure must actually reach one. Why does the first statement alone give no guarantee of a useful success probability?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Existence gives no measure of how often a randomized rule selects the successful path. For one successful path among \(2^n\) equally likely paths, success per random trial is \(2^{-n}\). The nondeterministic existence statement can hold even when random sampling almost always misses the path.

3.2

Quantum Transitions

We now compare coherent quantum composition with the two-state random model.

3.2.1 A two-state quantum transformation§

Consider the Hadamard transformation \[H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\] For a definite input zero, it produces amplitudes \((1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)^T\); input one gives \((1/\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2)^T\). Measuring the output in the zero–one basis gives each outcome with probability one half, the squared magnitude of either entry. For now take these as the predictions of a quantum operation. The negative sign is invisible in this single measurement. The next calculation shows why it cannot be discarded from the state description.

Problems

  1. What state does \(H\) produce from \(\ket0\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), which is \(\ket+\).

  2. Use \(H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) to calculate the probabilities of measuring 0 and 1.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each amplitude is \(1/\sqrt2\), so each squared magnitude is \(1/2\).

  3. One use of a Hadamard gate gives equal outcome probabilities from either basis input. If you retained only those probabilities, what prediction would you make for two uses? Which information would your description have lost?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A probability-only transition model would mix two equal distributions and predict \(1/2\) for each final outcome. In fact \(H^2\ket0=\ket0\). The discarded signs distinguish the two routes to outcome 1, whose amplitudes cancel in the second step.

3.2.2 Composition of transitions§

Matrix multiplication gives \(H^2=I\). Starting at zero, two successive Hadamards return zero with certainty. The fair randomizing classical step does not: its square is still a fair randomizer. The comparison assumes no measurement between the quantum steps. Inserting a measurement after the first Hadamard destroys the coherence needed for the certain return. We are not claiming that all classical devices lack hidden internal information; rather, a model that retains only the two outcome probabilities cannot reproduce this coherent composition.

Problems

  1. Which transformation acts first in the matrix product BA applied to a column vector?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(A\) acts first, followed by \(B\).

  2. Apply \(H\) to \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) using the two basis-state rules. Combine the coefficients of each basis state.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(H(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2=[(\ket0+\ket1)+(\ket0-\ket1)]/2=\ket0\). The \(\ket1\) coefficients cancel.

  3. Why does composing two quantum transformations require their amplitudes rather than just their one-step outcome probabilities? Use the two-Hadamard example to locate exactly where a probability-only calculation fails.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Composing transformations sums products of amplitudes over intermediate states. For final outcome 1 in \(H^2\ket0\), the two products are \(+1/2\) and \(-1/2\), giving probability zero. Squaring each route first instead gives \(1/4+1/4=1/2\) and loses the cancellation.

3.2.3 Signed amplitudes and outcome probabilities§

Amplitudes combine before they are converted to probabilities. Two paths contributing \(+1/2\) and \(-1/2\) to the same final outcome sum to zero, hence give zero probability. Squaring each first would instead give \(1/4+1/4=1/2\), a different prediction. The order of operations matters: \[p(k)=\left|\sum_{\text{paths to }k}\text{path amplitude}\right|^2.\] This rule applies to alternatives that remain coherent. If a measurement or environment keeps a distinguishable record of the alternatives, we must include that record in the joint description rather than summing as if it were absent.

Problems

  1. How do you obtain probability from an amplitude?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Take its squared magnitude: \(p=|a|^2\).

  2. Calculate the probabilities associated with amplitudes \(1/2\) and \(-1/2\). Are they different?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Both give probability \(1/4\). Squaring the magnitude removes the minus sign.

  3. Both \(+a\) and \(-a\) give the same probability when considered alone. In what sense can their difference still be physically meaningful? What additional operation would allow the difference to affect an observation?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A sign matters relative to another component that can interfere with it. For example, \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) have identical computational-basis probabilities, but \(H\) maps them to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\). Multiplying the entire state by \(-1\) is only a global phase and does not produce this distinction.

3.3

Path Interference

The path calculation makes the distinction between amplitudes and probabilities explicit.

3.3.1 Sum and product rules§

For two Hadamards with input zero, the path through intermediate zero contributes \(1/2\) to final zero, and the path through intermediate one contributes another \(1/2\). The final amplitude is one. For final one, the contributions are \(1/2\) and \(-1/2\), summing to zero. Thus both outcomes are accounted for, and the probabilities sum to one. This is matrix multiplication read as a path calculation: \((H^2)_{k0}=\sum_j H_{kj}H_{j0}\). The intermediate label is a bookkeeping basis, not necessarily an observed trajectory.

Problems

  1. Do amplitudes add or multiply along successive steps of one route?

    Show solutionHide solution

    They multiply along successive steps of a route; contributions from coherent alternative routes add.

  2. Two coherent routes contribute \(1/2\) and \(1/2\) to the same outcome. Add them and calculate the resulting probability.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The sum is \(1/2+1/2=1\), so the outcome probability is \(|1|^2=1\).

  3. Why do amplitudes multiply along a route but add across coherent routes? Trace two routes to one output and identify the point where taking squared magnitudes too early would change the prediction.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Matrix composition multiplies transition amplitudes along each route and sums over the intermediate alternatives. With two contributions of \(1/2\), the amplitude sum is one and the probability is one. Taking magnitudes first would give \(1/4+1/4=1/2\), discarding the cross term that represents interference.

3.3.2 Constructive and destructive interference§

Constructive interference means contributions reinforce; destructive interference means they partly or completely cancel. Neither creates probability from nothing. A unitary transformation redistributes amplitudes while preserving their total squared magnitude. An algorithm must arrange reinforcement at useful outcomes and cancellation elsewhere. Merely creating many nonzero amplitudes does not accomplish this. The two-Hadamard example is small enough that every term can be inspected, but the same sum-of-products structure governs a register with many basis labels.

Problems

  1. What is destructive interference?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It is cancellation, partial or complete, when coherent amplitude contributions add with opposing phases.

  2. Two coherent contributions to an outcome are \(1/2\) and \(-1/2\). Calculate their total amplitude and the outcome probability.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The total amplitude is \(1/2-1/2=0\), so the probability is zero.

  3. If adding a second route makes an outcome less likely, which assumption behind the classical picture of mutually exclusive routes has failed? Does destructive interference mean that a negative probability was ever present?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The classical rule treats the alternatives as disjoint events with separately assignable probabilities. Coherent routes to the same outcome require adding amplitudes instead. The resulting squared magnitude remains nonnegative; a negative amplitude is not a negative probability.

3.3.3 Path information and interference§

Write a state vector as a ket, \(\ket r\); the overlap of two vectors is written \(\braket{r_0}{r_1}\). If the two intermediate alternatives leave orthogonal records \(\ket{r_0}\) and \(\ket{r_1}\), the final amplitude contributions are attached to different joint states. Their squared norms add rather than cancel. More generally, the interference term is proportional to the overlap \(\braket{r_0}{r_1}\). Identical records preserve full interference; orthogonal records remove it from the unobserved-register statistics. Chapter 5 develops these joint states. For now the practical lesson is that information left elsewhere matters even when nobody reads it. Observation by a conscious observer is not required.

Problems

  1. Can a path record matter even if nobody reads it?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Yes. A physical correlation can distinguish paths whether or not a person observes it.

  2. One record is identical for both paths; another perfectly identifies the path. Which one preserves the possibility of interference between the paths?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The identical record does not distinguish the paths and preserves the possibility of interference. A perfectly distinguishing record removes the cross term when only the original system is measured.

  3. Why can a path record suppress interference even when no person reads it? Compare a record that distinguishes the paths perfectly with one that is identical for both paths.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For joint branches \(a\ket0\ket{e_0}\) and \(b\ket1\ket{e_1}\), local interference contains the record overlap \(\braket{e_0}{e_1}\). Identical normalized records give overlap one; orthogonal records give zero. The physical overlap, rather than anyone’s awareness, determines whether the local cross term survives.

3.4

Complex Amplitudes

Complex coordinates extend signs to phases while preserving the same composition rules.

3.4.1 Relative and global phase§

A sign is a special case of phase. Write an amplitude as \(re^{i\phi}\), where \(r\geq0\) is its magnitude. Its outcome probability is \(r^2\). Multiplying every amplitude by the same phase changes no prediction: it is a global phase. Changing one amplitude’s phase relative to another can alter interference. For example, \((\ket0+e^{i\phi}\ket1)/\sqrt2\) gives equal zero–one probabilities for every \(\phi\), but a subsequent Hadamard gives zero with probability \((1+\cos\phi)/2\).

Problems

  1. What is the difference between global and relative phase?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A global phase multiplies every amplitude by the same unit-magnitude factor. A relative phase is a phase difference between components.

  2. Compare \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), \(-(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), and \((\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Which pair differs only by global phase?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The first and second states differ by the common factor \(-1\), a global phase. The third changes the sign of only the \(\ket1\) component and therefore has a different relative phase.

  3. A phase factor on an entire state is unobservable, yet a phase factor on one component can matter. How does the second operation change a relation within the state that the first leaves unchanged?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A common phase leaves ratios between nonzero amplitudes unchanged and cancels from every probability. A phase on one component changes those ratios and the interference cross terms after later operations. \(H\), for example, converts the relative-sign difference between \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) into different certain outcomes.

3.4.2 Complex numbers and phase representation§

The complex number \(i\) satisfies \(i^2=-1\). For \(z=a+ib\), its conjugate is \(z^*=a-ib\), and \(|z|^2=z^*z=a^2+b^2\). Complex addition combines directed contributions in a plane; multiplication multiplies magnitudes and adds phases. This makes complex numbers convenient for coherent path weights. Conjugation matters because probabilities must be real and nonnegative: squaring \(i/\sqrt2\) gives \(-1/2\), whereas its squared magnitude is \(1/2\).

Problems

  1. What is the squared magnitude of \(i\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(|i|^2=(-i)i=1\).

  2. For \((\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), calculate the two computational-basis probabilities. Compare them with those of \(\ket+\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The probabilities are \(|1/\sqrt2|^2=1/2\) and \(|i/\sqrt2|^2=1/2\), the same computational-basis probabilities as \(\ket+\).

  3. What intermediate states become available when relative phase varies continuously instead of being restricted to a plus or minus sign? How could a later interference experiment distinguish those states?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The states \((\ket0+e^{i\phi}\ket1)/\sqrt2\) fill a continuous family. After \(H\), \(p(0)=(1+\cos\phi)/2\). This distinguishes many phases, but not \(\phi\) from \(-\phi\). A known phase shift before \(H\) supplies a complementary interference setting sensitive to \(\sin\phi\), allowing those cases to be distinguished statistically using fresh copies.

3.4.3 State vectors and transition matrices§

Choose an ordering of outcomes and collect amplitudes in a column \(\psi\). Put the amplitude for \(i\to j\) in matrix entry \(U_{ji}\): column \(i\) is the result of the transformation on basis input \(i\). Then \(\psi'=U\psi\), and successive steps give \(VU\psi\), with the rightmost operation acting first. The matrix is a compact description of the same path rules. Quantum states require normalization and physical transformations require additional constraints; the next chapter states those requirements and relates coordinates to measurements.

Problems

  1. What do the entries of a state vector represent?

    Show solutionHide solution

    They are the amplitudes of the state in the chosen ordered basis.

  2. Multiply the matrix \(X\), which swaps the two coordinates, by \((a,b)^T\). Write the resulting state.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(X(a,b)^T=(b,a)^T\), giving \(b\ket0+a\ket1\).

  3. A state vector and a matrix depend on a choice of basis. Which predictions should remain unchanged if both are expressed consistently in a different basis? Why would changing just one give misleading results?

    Show solutionHide solution

    All predictions for the same physical preparation, operation, and measurement remain unchanged under a consistent basis change. The state coordinates, operator matrices, and measurement description must transform together. Changing only the state coordinates while keeping a fixed numerical measurement matrix generally describes a different physical question.

Check your understanding

What goes wrong if path probabilities are added after squaring each quantum contribution?

Compare your explanation

It discards relative signs and phases before they can interfere. The resulting classical mixture can predict a nonzero probability where coherent amplitudes cancel.

Questions and worked problems

Attempt each question before opening a hint. Explain your reasoning, then compare it with the solution.

1. Quick check §

Two contributions to one outcome are \(+1/2\) and \(-1/2\). What is the outcome probability?

Hint 1

Add first.

Hint 2

Take the squared magnitude of the sum.

Worked solution

The total amplitude is zero, so the probability is zero. Adding the squared magnitudes separately would incorrectly give \(1/2\) for coherent alternatives.

2. Worked problem §

Starting in \(\ket0\), apply \(H\), then \(Z\), then \(H\). Compare with applying \(H\) twice.

Hint 1

\(H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

Hint 2

\(Z\) changes the sign of the \(\ket1\) component.

Worked solution

\(H\) then \(Z\) gives \((\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\). The final \(H\) gives \(\ket1\). By contrast \(H^2\ket0=\ket0\). The intermediate probabilities are \(1/2\) and \(1/2\) in both cases, but the relative phase changes the final result.

Revisit the relevant explanation

3. Extension §

Suppose an unobserved system records which intermediate basis state occurred between two Hadamards. Must the final result remain \(\ket0\)?

Hint 1

Write the joint state using record states \(\ket{e_0}\) and \(\ket{e_1}\).

Hint 2

If the records are orthogonal, cross terms vanish when only the qubit is measured.

Worked solution

No. After the first \(H\) and recording, the state is \((\ket0\ket{e_0}+\ket1\ket{e_1})/\sqrt2\). After the second \(H\), the \(\ket0\) component is \((\ket{e_0}+\ket{e_1})/2\). Its squared norm is \(1/2\) for orthogonal records, rather than 1 for identical records. Chapter 5 develops this joint-state calculation.
