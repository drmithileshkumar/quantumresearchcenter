Chapter 05

Composite Systems and Entanglement

Can a whole have a definite quantum state when its parts have no individual pure states of their own?

Combining two systems first looks like an exercise in keeping track of coordinates. Tensor products soon reveal states that cannot be separated into a state for each part, and correlations that require us to reason about the whole. We follow those correlations through the limits on copying quantum states and communicating by local actions. Teleportation then brings the pieces together: transferring an unknown state requires entanglement, measurement, and an ordinary classical message.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Describe joint states and distinguish entanglement from ordinary correlation.

Before you begin

Know how to normalize a state, measure in a basis, and apply a unitary transformation.

What you should be able to do

  • Expand tensor-product states.
  • Compare a Bell state with a correlated mixture.
  • Track teleportation outcomes and classical corrections.

The example to follow

Prepare a Bell state, then reuse that resource in teleportation. Compare computational-basis counts with X-basis counts.

Scope and assumptions

Teleportation transfers a state using shared entanglement and classical communication; it does not transmit a controllable signal faster than light.

5.1

Composite State Spaces

Joint states of several systems are built from the product rule.

5.1.1 Tensor products and product bases§

A register of two qubits has four configurations, \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\), so its state has four amplitudes. Suppose we prepare the first qubit in \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) and, independently, the second in \(c\ket0+d\ket1\). What are the four joint amplitudes?

Reaching the configuration \(01\) means that the first qubit is \(0\) and the second is \(1\). The two preparations are independent, so by the product rule the amplitude is the product of the two local amplitudes, \(ad\). Ordering the configurations with the first qubit as the leftmost bit,

\[\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix}.\]

This combination is the tensor product. For basis states it gives \(\ket a\otimes\ket b=\ket{ab}\); for example, \(\ket0\otimes\ket1=\ket{01}\). The four vectors \(\ket{ab}\) form the product basis. Two qubits need four amplitudes and three qubits need eight: dimensions multiply rather than add.

How does an operation on the first qubit alone act on the pair? It changes the first label and leaves the second unchanged: each arrow \(i\to j\) of \(U\), with weight \(w\), becomes the arrows \(ib\to jb\), with the same weight, for each value of \(b\). We write this operation \(U\otimes I\). On a product state, \((U\otimes I)(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi)=(U\ket\psi)\otimes\ket\phi\).

Problems

  1. List the computational-basis labels for two qubits.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\).

  2. Expand \(\ket0\otimes(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) in the joint basis.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Distribute the tensor product: \((\ket0\otimes\ket0+\ket0\otimes\ket1)/\sqrt2=(\ket{00}+\ket{01})/\sqrt2\).

  3. Why does combining two two-dimensional systems require four joint basis labels rather than merely four alternatives to which system exists? What does a tensor-product label say about simultaneous properties of both systems?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Both systems exist together, so a basis label must specify one local label for each. Two choices for the first and two for the second give \(2\times2=4\) combinations. For example, \(01\) means the first is in basis state 0 and the second in basis state 1, not that only one of the systems exists.

5.1.2 Product states§

A joint state of the form \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi\) is a product state. Its amplitudes are products of local amplitudes, so its probabilities are products of local probabilities: the two qubits behave independently.

Does a superposition of several joint configurations signal something more? Not necessarily. The state \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) has two nonzero amplitudes, yet

\[\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\ket0:\]

the first qubit is in an equal superposition, and the second is certainly \(0\).

The real question is whether one local vector for each qubit reproduces every joint amplitude. When it does, the state is a product state.

Problems

  1. What does it mean for a pure joint state to be a product state?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It can be written as \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi\), with one pure state for each subsystem.

  2. Factor \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) into one state for each qubit.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Both terms have the second qubit in \(\ket0\). Factoring gives \(((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2)\otimes\ket0=\ket+\otimes\ket0\).

  3. A product state may have several nonzero joint amplitudes. Why is counting those amplitudes an unreliable test for entanglement? What relationship among them would establish that the state factors?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each local state can itself have several nonzero coefficients, so their product can also have many nonzero joint amplitudes. What matters is whether every joint coefficient has the form \(a_ib_j\) for the same two local coefficient lists. For a nonzero two-qubit pure state, the \(2\times2\) coefficient matrix factors exactly when it has rank one.

5.1.3 The composition postulate§

The product rule tells us how independently prepared systems combine. Must every joint state be a product? Quantum mechanics does not require it. It takes the whole tensor-product space as the state space of the pair.

Postulate 4 (composition). The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the state spaces of its parts. Its basis vectors are the products of basis vectors of the parts.

A register of \(n\) qubits therefore has \(2^n\) basis vectors, and a general state needs \(2^n\) amplitudes. Does a measurement reveal \(2^n\) numbers? No: measuring all \(n\) qubits gives a single \(n\)-bit label.

Storing a general \(n\)-qubit state on a classical computer takes up to \(2^n\) amplitudes, which quickly becomes impractical. Does that by itself make quantum computers powerful? No. An advantage needs a task, and a procedure that makes its answer observable efficiently.

Problems

  1. How many computational-basis states do three qubits have?

    Show solutionHide solution

    There are \(2^3=8\) computational-basis states.

  2. For three qubits, compare the number of state-vector coordinates with the number of bits in one basis-measurement outcome.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The vector has eight complex coordinates. A single computational-basis measurement returns one label of length three bits, such as \(101\).

  3. An \(n\)-qubit state has exponentially many coordinates, but a basis measurement returns only \(n\) bits. Why does the size of the mathematical description not by itself establish a computational advantage?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Having many mathematical coordinates does not make them all readable in one run, nor guarantee that a useful state can be prepared efficiently. An advantage requires an algorithm that transforms a specific property into observable outcomes with favorable total resource costs. Description size alone supplies neither the algorithm nor a classical lower bound.

5.2

Entangled States

Two gates turn a product state into one that no pair of local states describes.

5.2.1 Bell-state preparation§

Start from \(\ket{00}\), apply \(H\) to the first qubit, then CNOT with the first qubit as control. What happens?

\(H\) gives \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\), still a product state. CNOT acts on each configuration: it leaves \(00\) alone and sends \(10\) to \(11\):

\[\ket{00}\to\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}\to\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.\]

The result is a Bell state, written \(\ket{\Phi^+}\).

The circuit drawing records this sequence, with time running from left to right. Does the vertical line of CNOT mean that the first qubit is measured and the result used to decide whether to flip the second? No. CNOT acts on both configurations of the superposition at once, without any measurement. That is why the result is a superposition of \(00\) and \(11\), rather than one of them.

Predict first. Reset to \(\ket{00}\). After \(H\) on the first qubit, which two outcomes are possible? Which outcomes remain after CNOT?

Experiment: build a Bell state

The final pure state is entangled: its joint amplitudes do not factor into two single-qubit states. Its probabilities alone would not distinguish it from a classical mixture.

After trying it: compare your explanation

After \(H\), \(00\) and \(10\) each have probability \(1/2\). CNOT changes \(10\) to \(11\), so the final outcomes are \(00\) and \(11\), each with probability \(1/2\). The bars alone do not establish entanglement.

Problems

  1. Which two gates prepare the Bell state from \(\ket{00}\) in this subsection?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(H\) on the first qubit, followed by CNOT with the first qubit as control.

  2. Write the state after \(H\) on the first qubit and then after CNOT with that qubit as control.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\ket{00}\) becomes \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) after \(H\). CNOT leaves \(00\) unchanged and maps \(10\) to \(11\), giving \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\).

  3. In Bell-state preparation, CNOT correlates the two qubits without measuring its control. How would the resulting preparation differ if the first qubit were measured after \(H\) and its outcome then used as an ordinary control?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Measuring after \(H\) produces a random classical bit. Using that bit to control the target gives \(00\) or \(11\) with probability \(1/2\) each, an incoherent mixture when the record is ignored. Coherent CNOT instead gives the Bell superposition. The preparations agree in computational-basis counts but differ, for example, when both qubits are measured in the \(X\) basis.

5.2.2 Nonfactorization of amplitudes§

Can \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) be written as a product \((a\ket0+b\ket1)\otimes(c\ket0+d\ket1)\)? Compare amplitudes: we would need \(ac=bd=1/\sqrt2\) and \(ad=bc=0\). The first two equations make all four of \(a,b,c,d\) nonzero, and then \(ad\) cannot be \(0\). No product reproduces \(\ket{\Phi^+}\).

A joint state that is not a product state is entangled. Neither qubit of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) has a state vector of its own; only the pair does.

Is entanglement just correlation? Measuring both qubits in the computational basis gives \(00\) or \(11\), each with probability \(1/2\). A classical mixture, in which a coin decides whether to prepare \(\ket{00}\) or \(\ket{11}\), gives the same counts. The two differ in other measurements.

Problems

  1. What must fail for a pure two-qubit state to be entangled?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It must be impossible to write the joint pure-state coefficients as products of coefficients of two local states.

  2. Try factoring \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). Explain why nonzero \(00\) and \(11\) coefficients force unwanted cross terms in a product.

    Show solutionHide solution

    A product gives coefficients \(ac\), \(ad\), \(bc\), \(bd\). The Bell state requires \(ac=bd=1/\sqrt2\), so \(a,b,c,d\) must all be nonzero. Then \(ad\) and \(bc\) cannot vanish, contradicting the required zero coefficients of \(01\) and \(10\).

  3. Why is failure to factor amplitudes stronger evidence of pure-state entanglement than observing correlated outcomes in one basis? What comparison would expose the difference between a Bell state and a classical mixture?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Nonfactorization excludes every pure product description, whereas correlated counts in one basis can also arise from a classical mixture. Measure both qubits in the \(X\) basis: the Bell state gives \({+}{+}\) and \({-}{-}\) each with probability \(1/2\); the equal mixture of \(00\) and \(11\) gives all four \(X\) outcomes with probability \(1/4\).

5.2.3 Joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities§

How do we read probabilities from a joint state? With amplitudes \(\alpha_{ab}\), the joint probability is \(p(a,b)=|\alpha_{ab}|^2\). The probability that the first qubit gives \(a\), whatever the second gives, is the marginal \(p(a)=\sum_bp(a,b)\), and the conditional probability of \(b\) given \(a\) is \(p(b\mid a)=p(a,b)/p(a)\) when \(p(a)\gt 0\).

For \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), \(p(00)=p(11)=1/2\); each qubit alone gives \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\); and once the first result is known, the second is certain.

Now measure both qubits in the \(\pm\) basis. Rewriting \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) in that basis gives

\[\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{\ket{++}+\ket{--}}{\sqrt2},\]

so the two results again agree with certainty. The classical mixture behaves differently. Each of \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket{11}\) is a product of states that give \(+\) and \(-\) with probability \(1/2\), so in the \(\pm\) basis the mixture gives all four pairs of results with probability \(1/4\).

Correlation in one basis is therefore not enough to establish entanglement; the measurement must be specified.

Problems

  1. How do you obtain a marginal probability from joint probabilities?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Sum over the outcomes of the subsystem whose value you are not retaining.

  2. For \(p(00)=p(11)=1/2\) and all other outcomes zero, calculate \(p(\text{first}=0)\) and \(p(\text{second}=0 \text{ given first}=0)\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(p(\text{first}=0)=p(00)+p(01)=1/2\). The conditional probability is \(p(00)/p(\text{first}=0)=(1/2)/(1/2)=1\).

  3. Knowing the first Bell measurement outcome lets you predict the second, although either local outcome is random. What changes when you condition on the first outcome: the local statistics available without the message, or the predictions available with it?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The predictions conditioned on the communicated outcome change. Without that message, the second observer still has the fair marginal distribution. Knowing the first outcome allows selecting a correlated subset of runs; it does not change the statistics available to an observer who has no access to that outcome.

5.3

Quantum Information Constraints

The joint-state description limits what can be copied and what can be communicated.

5.3.1 Basis-state copying§

Can a quantum state be copied? CNOT seems to copy. With a blank target, \(\ket0\ket0\to\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket1\ket0\to\ket1\ket1\).

What does it do to a superposition? By linearity,

\[(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\ket0\to\alpha\ket{00}+\beta\ket{11}.\]

Two copies would instead be

\[(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\otimes(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)=\alpha^2\ket{00}+\alpha\beta\ket{01}+\alpha\beta\ket{10}+\beta^2\ket{11}.\]

These differ whenever \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are both nonzero. CNOT copies the labels \(0\) and \(1\), not superpositions of them.

Problems

  1. Which target value allows CNOT to copy a basis label?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A target initially in \(\ket0\).

  2. Apply CNOT to \(\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket1\ket0\), then use linearity to find its action on \(\ket+\ket0\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    CNOT maps \(\ket0\ket0\) to \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket1\ket0\) to \(\ket{11}\). By linearity, \(\ket+\ket0\) maps to \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\).

  3. Why does a device that copies both basis states fail to copy their arbitrary superposition? Expand the desired two-copy output and identify the terms that linear evolution does not produce.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For input \(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\), the copier’s linear output is \(\alpha\ket{00}+\beta\ket{11}\). Two actual copies would be \(\alpha^2\ket{00}+\alpha\beta\ket{01}+\alpha\beta\ket{10}+\beta^2\ket{11}\). The cross terms and coefficient dependence differ. Copying two basis labels therefore does not imply cloning their superpositions.

5.3.2 The no-cloning theorem§

Could some cleverer unitary copy every state? Suppose \(U\ket\psi\ket0=\ket\psi\ket\psi\) for every state \(\ket\psi\). Take two states \(\ket\psi\) and \(\ket\phi\). A unitary preserves inner products, and the inner product of two product states is the product of the inner products of their parts, so

\[\braket\phi\psi\braket00=\braket\phi\psi^2,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad\braket\phi\psi=\braket\phi\psi^2.\]

The overlap must therefore be \(0\) or \(1\). For two different states that are not orthogonal, such as \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) with overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), this fails. No unitary copies every state. This is the no-cloning theorem.

What does the theorem not forbid? States known to belong to an orthogonal set, such as basis labels, can be copied, and a state that we know how to prepare can simply be prepared again. What is impossible is a single fixed operation that perfectly copies an arbitrary unknown state.

Problems

  1. Does the no-cloning theorem forbid copying known orthogonal basis labels?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. The theorem excludes a single device cloning arbitrary unknown states, including nonorthogonal possibilities.

  2. Compare CNOT’s output on \(\ket+\ket0\) with \(\ket+\ket+\) by expanding both in the joint basis.

    Show solutionHide solution

    CNOT gives \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). By contrast, \(\ket+\ket+=(\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})/2\). These states have different amplitudes and measurement statistics.

  3. What assumption in the no-cloning argument must change if the possible input states are known to be orthogonal? Why is a machine for copying that restricted set not a counterexample to the theorem?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Restrict the allowed inputs to a known orthogonal set. The overlap condition required by cloning is then \(0=0^2\) for distinct inputs, so the nonorthogonal-state contradiction disappears. A basis copier operates on this restricted promise; it still does not clone arbitrary superpositions, and therefore is not a universal cloner.

5.3.3 Local states and no-signalling§

Alice and Bob share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), Alice holding the first qubit and Bob the second. What state does Bob’s qubit have on its own? Not a state vector, since the pair is entangled. Whatever basis Bob measures in, each of his two outcomes has probability \(1/2\). These predictions are summarized by the density matrix \(I/2\), described in Appendix D.

Can Alice change Bob’s statistics by acting on her own qubit? Suppose she applies a unitary to it, or measures it without telling Bob the result. Bob’s outcome probabilities do not change. If they could, Alice could send a message instantly by choosing her action.

When Alice tells Bob her result, his description does change: given her result \(0\), his qubit is \(\ket0\). But that information travels through an ordinary classical channel. Entanglement provides correlations, not a way to signal.

Problems

  1. What is the local outcome distribution for either half of a Bell state?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each local computational-basis outcome has probability \(1/2\).

  2. If the other qubit is measured but its outcome is not sent to you, do your computational-basis probabilities change? Explain using the two possible outcomes.

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. The remote outcome is 0 or 1 with probability \(1/2\). Conditional on it, your matching bit is certain, but averaging over the unknown outcome still gives \(1/2\) for each local bit.

  3. A distant measurement can change the state you assign after learning its outcome. Why can this not be used to send a chosen message without communicating the outcome? Distinguish conditional knowledge from locally observable statistics.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The remote observer cannot choose the individual random outcome and cannot change your reduced state by an unconditioned local trace-preserving operation. Your conditional state assignment changes only after the outcome is known. Sending that information requires ordinary communication, so it cannot supply a controllable faster-than-light message.

5.4

Quantum Teleportation

Entanglement, measurement, and two classical bits transfer an unknown state.

5.4.1 Entanglement resources§

Alice holds a qubit in an unknown state \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) and wants Bob to have it. She cannot copy it, and measuring it would reveal only one bit. What can she do if they share a Bell pair?

The three qubits start in \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket{\Phi^+}\); Alice holds the first two and Bob the third. Alice applies CNOT from her input qubit to her half of the Bell pair, then \(H\) to her input qubit. Nothing quantum is sent to Bob. To see what has happened, we follow the complete three-qubit state.

Problems

  1. What shared quantum resource does teleportation require?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A shared entangled pair, conventionally a Bell pair, with one qubit held by each party.

  2. List the shared quantum resource and the classical communication used to teleport one qubit.

    Show solutionHide solution

    One shared Bell pair and two classical bits from the sender to the receiver, together with the sender’s measurement and the receiver’s conditional correction.

  3. Why must shared entanglement be included among the resources used by teleportation? If preparing and distributing the entangled pair were costly, how would that change an assessment of the protocol?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The protocol relies on correlations already established between the parties; the classical message alone cannot transfer the unknown state. Preparing, distributing, and maintaining the entangled pair are resources. If those steps are expensive or unreliable, they can dominate the total cost even though the online message is only two bits.

5.4.2 Measurement and conditional corrections§

Start from \((\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). CNOT flips Alice’s Bell half where the input is \(1\), and \(H\) then acts on the input qubit:

\[\frac{\alpha\ket0(\ket{00}+\ket{11})+\beta\ket1(\ket{10}+\ket{01})}{\sqrt2}\;\to\;\frac{\alpha(\ket0+\ket1)(\ket{00}+\ket{11})+\beta(\ket0-\ket1)(\ket{10}+\ket{01})}{2}.\]

Group the terms by the configuration of Alice’s two qubits:

\[\tfrac12\Bigl[\ket{00}(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)+\ket{01}(\alpha\ket1+\beta\ket0)+\ket{10}(\alpha\ket0-\beta\ket1)+\ket{11}(\alpha\ket1-\beta\ket0)\Bigr]=\frac12\sum_{m,n\in\{0,1\}}\ket{mn}\otimes X^nZ^m\ket\psi.\]

Alice now measures her two qubits. Each outcome \((m,n)\) has probability \(1/4\) and leaves Bob’s qubit in \(X^nZ^m\ket\psi\).

Bob does not know which. If Alice sends him the two bits \(m\) and \(n\), he applies \(X^n\) and then \(Z^m\). Since \(Z^mX^nX^nZ^m=I\), his qubit ends in \(\ket\psi\).

Does teleportation depend on a lucky outcome? No: the calculation covers all four outcomes, and each one is corrected.

Problems

  1. Why does the receiver apply a correction in teleportation?

    Show solutionHide solution

    To undo the known outcome-dependent transformation of the input state on the receiver’s qubit.

  2. If the receiver’s conditional state is \(X\ket\psi\), which gate recovers \(\ket\psi\)? Check by applying it.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Apply \(X\) again. Since \(X^2=I\), \(X(X\ket\psi)=\ket\psi\).

  3. Before the classical correction, the receiver’s state depends on the sender’s measurement outcome. Why is knowing which correction to apply essential even though each possible correction is reversible?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Invertibility helps only when the receiver knows which transformation occurred. Different measurement outcomes require different inverse corrections; choosing the wrong one generally gives a different state. The two-bit message identifies the branch. Without it, the receiver averages over branches instead of holding a known corrected copy of the input.

5.4.3 State transfer and classical communication§

Could Bob use his qubit before the two bits arrive? Without them, his qubit is in each of the four states \(X^nZ^m\ket\psi\) with probability \(1/4\). Averaged over the four, his predictions are those of a fair coin, \(I/2\), whatever \(\ket\psi\) is. He learns nothing about \(\ket\psi\), and nothing has travelled faster than the classical bits.

Has the state been copied? No. Alice’s measurement leaves her qubits in \(\ket m\ket n\), with no trace of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), and the Bell pair has been used up. The unknown state now exists only at Bob, consistent with the no-cloning theorem.

Teleportation places three descriptions side by side: the joint state of all three qubits, Bob’s state given Alice’s result, and Bob’s state when the result is unknown. Keeping them apart is the key to reasoning about entanglement.

Problems

  1. How many classical bits are sent in single-qubit teleportation?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Two classical bits.

  2. Explain why receiving the classical bits alone, without the shared Bell pair, is not the teleportation protocol.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The message specifies a correction to a qubit already correlated with the sender’s system through entanglement. Without the shared pair, there is no such correlated receiver state for the message to correct.

  3. Teleportation communicates only two classical bits while transferring an arbitrary qubit state. Why does that not mean the two bits contain a classical description of all its amplitudes? What other resources make the transfer possible?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The two bits identify one of four correction branches; they do not specify the input amplitudes. The physical input state, shared entanglement, the joint measurement, and conditional correction together enable transfer. The sender’s original state is not retained as an extra copy, and the shared pair is consumed.

Is \((\ket{00}+\ket{01})/\sqrt2\) entangled?

Factor out a common first-qubit state.

Both terms have the first qubit in \(\ket0\).

No. It is \(\ket0\otimes(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), a product state. A sum of basis states is not automatically entangled.

2. Worked problem §

Apply \(H\) to the first qubit of \(\ket{00}\), then CNOT with that qubit as control. Give joint and first-qubit probabilities in the computational basis.

Hint 1

Expand after \(H\) before applying CNOT.

Hint 2

CNOT sends \(\ket{10}\) to \(\ket{11}\).

Worked solution

The intermediate state is \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) and the final state is \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). The joint probabilities are \(p(00)=p(11)=1/2\) and \(p(01)=p(10)=0\). Summing over the second bit gives \(p(\text{first}=0)=p(\text{first}=1)=1/2\).

3. Extension §

Compare measuring both qubits in the \(X\) basis for the Bell state \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) and for an equal classical mixture of \(00\) and \(11\).

Hint 1

Use \(\ket0=(\ket++\ket-)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket1=(\ket+-\ket-)/\sqrt2\).

Hint 2

The Bell amplitudes for +− and −+ cancel.

Worked solution

For \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), the outcomes \({+}{+}\) and \({-}{-}\) each have probability \(1/2\). The mixture yields \({+}{+}\), \({+}{-}\), \({-}{+}\), and \({-}{-}\) with probability \(1/4\) each. Their computational-basis counts agree, but these \(X\)-basis counts distinguish coherence from the mixture.
