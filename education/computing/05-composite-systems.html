Chapter 05

Composite Systems and Entanglement

A quantum computer is a register of many qubits, not a single one. So far a state has described one qubit. How should we describe several? For independently prepared qubits, the product rule gives the answer: the joint amplitudes are products of the individual ones. But the postulates also allow joint states that are not products, and these states have no classical counterpart.

We begin with the tensor product, which builds joint states from individual ones, and adopt it as the postulate for composite systems. A two-gate circuit then produces a Bell state, which cannot be written as a product, and we learn to tell such entanglement apart from classical correlation. Entanglement and linearity limit what can be done with quantum information: an unknown state cannot be copied, and acting on one part of an entangled pair cannot send a message to the other. Teleportation shows what remains possible within these limits: with shared entanglement and two classical bits, an unknown state can be moved from one place to another.

With registers of qubits in hand, the next chapter turns to computation itself and asks how a quantum algorithm can learn about a function.

Can a whole have a definite quantum state when its parts have no individual pure states of their own?

Two qubits can have a pure joint state while neither has an individual pure-state vector. We will calculate what this changes about copying, communication, and measurement.

5.1

Composite State Spaces

We first build joint states from the states of the parts, and find that a superposition of joint configurations need not be anything new: it is often a product of individual states. Only then do we ask which joint states the theory admits.

5.1.1 Tensor products and product bases§

A register of two qubits has four configurations, \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\), so its state has four amplitudes. Suppose we prepare the first qubit in \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) and, independently, the second in \(c\ket0+d\ket1\). What are the four joint amplitudes?

Reaching the configuration \(01\) means that the first qubit is \(0\) and the second is \(1\). The two preparations are independent, so by the product rule the amplitude is the product of the two local amplitudes, \(ad\). Ordering the configurations with the first qubit as the leftmost bit,

\[\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix}.\]

This combination is the tensor product. For basis states it gives \(\ket a\otimes\ket b=\ket{ab}\); for example, \(\ket0\otimes\ket1=\ket{01}\). The four vectors \(\ket{ab}\) form the product basis. Two qubits need four amplitudes and three qubits need eight: dimensions multiply rather than add.

How does an operation on the first qubit alone act on the pair? It changes the first label and leaves the second unchanged: each arrow \(i\to j\) of \(U\), with weight \(w\), becomes the arrows \(ib\to jb\), with the same weight, for each value of \(b\). We write this operation \(U\otimes I\). On a product state, \((U\otimes I)(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi)=(U\ket\psi)\otimes\ket\phi\).

Problems

  1. Compute \(\bigl(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigr)^T\otimes(1,0)^T\) and \(\bigl(\tfrac35,\tfrac45\bigr)^T\otimes(0,1)^T\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each joint amplitude is a product of local amplitudes, in the order \(00,01,10,11\): the first is \(\bigl(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0,\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0\bigr)^T\), and the second is \(\bigl(0,\tfrac35,0,\tfrac45\bigr)^T\).

  2. Compute \((H\otimes I)\ket{01}\) and \((I\otimes H)\ket{01}\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \((H\otimes I)\ket{01}=(H\ket0)\otimes\ket1=(\ket{01}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). \((I\otimes H)\ket{01}=\ket0\otimes(H\ket1)=(\ket{00}-\ket{01})/\sqrt2\). The same gate on a different qubit gives a different joint state.

  3. Show that \(\braket{a\otimes b}{c\otimes d}=\braket ac\braket bd\) for vectors of two coordinates, and deduce that the tensor product of two unit vectors is a unit vector.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The coordinates of \(a\otimes b\) are \(a_ib_j\), so \(\braket{a\otimes b}{c\otimes d}=\sum_{i,j}(a_ib_j)^*c_id_j=\Bigl(\sum_ia_i^*c_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_jb_j^*d_j\Bigr)=\braket ac\braket bd\). With \(c=a\) and \(d=b\), \(\|a\otimes b\|^2=\|a\|^2\|b\|^2=1\) for unit vectors.

5.1.2 Product states§

Which joint states arise from independent preparations? A joint state of the form \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket\phi\) is a product state. Its amplitudes are products of local amplitudes, so its probabilities are products of local probabilities: the two qubits behave independently.

Does a superposition of several joint configurations signal something more? Not necessarily. The state \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) has two nonzero amplitudes, yet

\[\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\ket0:\]

the first qubit is in an equal superposition, and the second is certainly \(0\).

The real question is whether one local vector for each qubit reproduces every joint amplitude. When it does, the state is a product state.

Problems

  1. Is \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})/2\) a product state? If so, factor it.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Yes: \(\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}=\ket+\ket+\). Every product of the local amplitudes \(1/\sqrt2\) is \(1/2\).

  2. Factor \((\ket{00}-\ket{01}+\ket{10}-\ket{11})/2\), and find the probability that the second qubit gives \(0\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The sign depends only on the second label, so the state is \(\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\tfrac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}=\ket+\ket-\). The second qubit gives \(0\) with probability \(1/2\).

  3. Show that a two-qubit state with amplitudes \(\alpha_{00},\alpha_{01},\alpha_{10},\alpha_{11}\) is a product state exactly when \(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    For a product \((a\ket0+b\ket1)\otimes(c\ket0+d\ket1)\), \(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=acbd=adbc=\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}\). Conversely, suppose the condition holds. If \(\alpha_{00}\neq0\), then \((\alpha_{00}\ket0+\alpha_{10}\ket1)\otimes(\ket0+\tfrac{\alpha_{01}}{\alpha_{00}}\ket1)\) has amplitudes \(\alpha_{00},\alpha_{01},\alpha_{10}\), and \(\alpha_{10}\alpha_{01}/\alpha_{00}=\alpha_{11}\). If \(\alpha_{00}=0\), the condition gives \(\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}=0\): if \(\alpha_{01}=0\) the state is \(\ket1\otimes(\alpha_{10}\ket0+\alpha_{11}\ket1)\), and if \(\alpha_{10}=0\) it is \((\alpha_{01}\ket0+\alpha_{11}\ket1)\otimes\ket1\).

5.1.3 The composition postulate§

The product rule tells us how independently prepared systems combine. Must every joint state be a product? Quantum mechanics does not require it. It takes the whole tensor-product space as the state space of the pair.

Composition. The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the state spaces of its parts. Its basis vectors are the products of basis vectors of the parts.

A register of \(n\) qubits therefore has \(2^n\) basis vectors, and a general state needs \(2^n\) amplitudes. Does a measurement reveal \(2^n\) numbers? No: measuring all \(n\) qubits gives a single \(n\)-bit label.

Storing a general \(n\)-qubit state on a classical computer takes up to \(2^n\) amplitudes, which quickly becomes impractical. Does that by itself make quantum computers powerful? No. An advantage needs a task, and a procedure that makes its answer observable efficiently.

Problems

  1. How many amplitudes describe a general state of \(10\) qubits? How many bits does a measurement of all \(10\) qubits return?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(2^{10}=1024\) amplitudes, but a measurement returns only \(10\) bits.

  2. A classical simulation stores each amplitude as two \(8\)-byte numbers. How much memory does a general \(40\)-qubit state need?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(2^{40}\cdot16\) bytes \(\approx1.8\times10^{13}\) bytes, about \(18\) terabytes. Each additional qubit doubles this.

  3. A product state of \(n\) qubits is described by \(n\) pairs of amplitudes. Show that applying one one-qubit operation to each qubit of a product state can be simulated with work proportional to \(n\). What does this show about the size of the state space as a source of difficulty?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \((U_1\otimes\cdots\otimes U_n)(\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_n})=U_1\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_n\ket{\psi_n}\), so it suffices to multiply each \(2\times2\) matrix by its pair of amplitudes: \(n\) small multiplications. The state space has \(2^n\) dimensions, yet this computation is easy to simulate. Difficulty requires entangling operations, not a large state space alone.

5.2

Entangled States

The composition postulate allows joint states that are not products. Can a circuit actually produce one? Two gates suffice: a Hadamard and a CNOT turn \(\ket{00}\) into a Bell state, and comparing amplitudes proves that no product of individual states equals it. Its measurement outcomes are perfectly correlated, but so are those of two bits that are both \(0\) or both \(1\) at random. Measuring in another basis tells the two apart.

5.2.1 Bell-state preparation§

Start from \(\ket{00}\), apply \(H\) to the first qubit, then CNOT with the first qubit as control. What happens?

\(H\) gives \((\ket{00}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\), still a product state. CNOT acts on each configuration: it leaves \(00\) alone and sends \(10\) to \(11\):

\[\ket{00}\to\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{10}}{\sqrt2}\to\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.\]

The result is a Bell state, written \(\ket{\Phi^+}\).

The circuit drawing records this sequence, with time running from left to right. Does the vertical line of CNOT mean that the first qubit is measured and the result used to decide whether to flip the second? No. CNOT acts on both configurations of the superposition at once, without any measurement. That is why the result is a superposition of \(00\) and \(11\), rather than one of them.

Predict first. Reset to \(\ket{00}\). After \(H\) on the first qubit, which two outcomes are possible? Which outcomes remain after CNOT?

Problems

  1. Apply \(H\) to the first qubit of \(\ket{10}\), then CNOT with the first qubit as control. Which state results?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(H\ket1=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\), giving \((\ket{00}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2\). CNOT sends \(10\) to \(11\), so the result is \((\ket{00}-\ket{11})/\sqrt2\), the Bell state \(\ket{\Phi^-}\).

  2. Apply the same circuit to \(\ket{01}\) and to \(\ket{11}\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    From \(\ket{01}\): \((\ket{01}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\to(\ket{01}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\), the Bell state \(\ket{\Psi^+}\). From \(\ket{11}\): \((\ket{01}-\ket{11})/\sqrt2\to(\ket{01}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2\), the Bell state \(\ket{\Psi^-}\).

  3. Show that the four states produced from \(\ket{00},\ket{01},\ket{10},\ket{11}\) form an orthonormal basis, and give a circuit that turns each of them back into its label.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The circuit is unitary, and a unitary preserves inner products, so it maps the orthonormal computational basis to an orthonormal set of four vectors in a four-dimensional space: a basis, the Bell basis. The inverse circuit undoes the gates in reverse order: CNOT, then \(H\) on the first qubit, since each is its own inverse. Followed by a computational-basis measurement, it identifies which Bell state was present.

5.2.2 Nonfactorization of amplitudes§

Try to factor the Bell state as \((a\ket0+b\ket1)\otimes(c\ket0+d\ket1)\). Matching coefficients requires

\[ac=bd=1/\sqrt2,\qquad ad=bc=0.\]

The first pair makes all four factors nonzero; the second pair then cannot hold. The state is entangled: this pure joint state is not a product of pure local states.

Each qubit still has a local state description, but it is a density matrix rather than a pure-state vector. For this Bell state, each local density matrix is \(I/2\). The pair’s vector contains relationships that no pair of local pure vectors can reproduce.

Do correlated zero–one results establish entanglement? No. Preparing \(00\) or \(11\) according to a fair classical coin gives the same computational-basis counts. We need a measurement that distinguishes that mixture from the coherent Bell superposition.

Problems

  1. Is \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}-\ket{11})/2\) entangled?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A product would need \(ac=ad=bc=1/2\) and \(bd=-1/2\). Then \((ac)(bd)=-1/4\) while \((ad)(bc)=1/4\), but both equal \(abcd\). So no product exists: the state is entangled.

  2. For which real numbers \(\varepsilon\) is \((\ket{00}+\varepsilon\ket{11})/\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}\) a product state?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The product condition \(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}\) reads \(\varepsilon=0\) (up to the common normalization). Only \(\varepsilon=0\), the state \(\ket{00}\), is a product. However small \(\varepsilon\neq0\) is, the state is entangled, though only slightly.

  3. For a two-qubit state in which both outcomes of the first qubit are possible, measure the first qubit in the computational basis. Show that the state is entangled exactly when the conditional state of the second qubit depends on the outcome.

    Show solutionHide solution

    After outcome \(0\), the second qubit is proportional to \(\alpha_{00}\ket0+\alpha_{01}\ket1\); after outcome \(1\), to \(\alpha_{10}\ket0+\alpha_{11}\ket1\). These describe the same state exactly when the two vectors are proportional, that is, when \(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}\), which is the product condition. So the conditional state is the same for both outcomes exactly when the state is a product.

5.2.3 Joint, marginal, and conditional probabilities§

Although neither qubit of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) has an individual pure-state vector, each still has well-defined local measurement probabilities. How do we read probabilities from a joint state? With amplitudes \(\alpha_{ab}\), the joint probability is \(p(a,b)=|\alpha_{ab}|^2\). The probability that the first qubit gives \(a\), whatever the second gives, is the marginal \(p(a)=\sum_bp(a,b)\), and the conditional probability of \(b\) given \(a\) is \(p(b\mid a)=p(a,b)/p(a)\) when \(p(a)\gt 0\).

For \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), \(p(00)=p(11)=1/2\); each qubit alone gives \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\); and once the first result is known, the second is certain.

Now measure both qubits in the \(\pm\) basis. Rewriting \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) in that basis gives

\[\ket{\Phi^+}=\frac{\ket{++}+\ket{--}}{\sqrt2},\]

so the two results again agree with certainty. The classical mixture behaves differently. Each of \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket{11}\) is a product of states that give \(+\) and \(-\) with probability \(1/2\), so in the \(\pm\) basis the mixture gives all four pairs of results with probability \(1/4\).

Correlation in one basis is therefore not enough to establish entanglement; the measurement must be specified.

Problems

  1. For \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), compute \(p(\text{first}=1)\) and \(p(\text{second}=1\mid\text{first}=1)\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(p(\text{first}=1)=p(10)+p(11)=0+\tfrac12=\tfrac12\), and \(p(\text{second}=1\mid\text{first}=1)=p(11)/p(\text{first}=1)=1\).

  2. For \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{11})/\sqrt3\), compute the joint probabilities, both marginal distributions, and \(p(\text{second}=1\mid\text{first}=0)\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(p(00)=p(01)=p(11)=1/3\) and \(p(10)=0\). The first qubit gives \(0\) with probability \(2/3\) and \(1\) with \(1/3\); the second gives \(0\) with \(1/3\) and \(1\) with \(2/3\). Finally, \(p(\text{second}=1\mid\text{first}=0)=p(01)/p(\text{first}=0)=\tfrac{1/3}{2/3}=\tfrac12\).

  3. Both qubits of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) are measured in the basis \(\ket{u_0}=\cos\theta\ket0+\sin\theta\ket1\), \(\ket{u_1}=-\sin\theta\ket0+\cos\theta\ket1\). Show that the two results agree with certainty, for every \(\theta\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    For real vectors, \(\braket{u_a\otimes u_b}{\Phi^+}=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(u_a(0)u_b(0)+u_a(1)u_b(1)\bigr)=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\braket{u_a}{u_b}\). This is \(1/\sqrt2\) for \(a=b\) and \(0\) for \(a\neq b\), so the outcomes \(00\) and \(11\) each have probability \(1/2\), and differing outcomes never occur. A classical mixture of \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket{11}\) agrees with certainty only at \(\theta=0\) and in similar special bases.

5.3

Quantum Information Constraints

Entangled states show that the joint state carries more than the states of the parts. What does this description allow us to do with quantum information? Two natural tasks turn out to be impossible. CNOT copies basis states, but linearity forbids any operation that copies every state. And acting on one half of an entangled pair changes the joint state but not the statistics of the other half, so entanglement alone cannot carry a message.

5.3.1 Basis-state copying§

Can a quantum state be copied? CNOT seems to copy. With a blank target, \(\ket0\ket0\to\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket1\ket0\to\ket1\ket1\).

What does it do to a superposition? By linearity,

\[(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\ket0\to\alpha\ket{00}+\beta\ket{11}.\]

Two copies would instead be

\[(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\otimes(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)=\alpha^2\ket{00}+\alpha\beta\ket{01}+\alpha\beta\ket{10}+\beta^2\ket{11}.\]

These differ whenever \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are both nonzero. CNOT copies the labels \(0\) and \(1\), not superpositions of them.

Problems

  1. Apply CNOT to \(\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\ket0\) and to \(\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\otimes\ket1\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\) and \((\ket{01}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\). In neither case is the result two copies of \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), which would be \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})/2\).

  2. For which states \(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) does CNOT turn \((\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\ket0\) into two copies, \((\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\otimes(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    CNOT gives \(\alpha\ket{00}+\beta\ket{11}\); two copies are \(\alpha^2\ket{00}+\alpha\beta\ket{01}+\alpha\beta\ket{10}+\beta^2\ket{11}\). The \(\ket{01}\) amplitudes require \(\alpha\beta=0\), so the state is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), up to a global phase. Only the basis states are copied.

  3. A unitary \(U\) copies both basis states: \(U\ket0\ket0=\ket0\ket0\) and \(U\ket1\ket0=\ket1\ket1\). Show that, whatever \(U\) does on other inputs, it sends \(\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\ket0\) to an entangled state.

    Show solutionHide solution

    By linearity, \(U\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\ket0=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(U\ket{00}+U\ket{10})=\tfrac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}\). Its amplitudes give \(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=\tfrac12\neq0=\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}\), so it is entangled. Copying basis states forces entangling superpositions; it cannot copy them.

5.3.2 The no-cloning theorem§

Could some cleverer unitary copy every state? Suppose \(U\ket\psi\ket0=\ket\psi\ket\psi\) for every state \(\ket\psi\). Take two states \(\ket\psi\) and \(\ket\phi\). A unitary preserves inner products, and the inner product of two product states is the product of the inner products of their parts, so

\[\braket\phi\psi\braket00=\braket\phi\psi^2,\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad\braket\phi\psi=\braket\phi\psi^2.\]

The overlap must therefore be \(0\) or \(1\). For two different states that are not orthogonal, such as \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) with overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), this fails. No unitary copies every state. This is the no-cloning theorem.

What does the theorem not forbid? States known to belong to an orthogonal set, such as basis labels, can be copied, and a state that we know how to prepare can simply be prepared again. What is impossible is a single fixed operation that perfectly copies an arbitrary unknown state.

Problems

  1. Compute \(\braket0+\) and \(\braket{00}{++}\). Why do these values rule out a unitary that copies both \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\), while \(\braket{00}{++}=\braket0+^2=1/2\). The inputs \(\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket+\ket0\) have overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), so a unitary would keep that overlap, but the desired outputs \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket{++}\) have overlap \(1/2\).

  2. Show that a unitary can copy each of the two states \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) into a register prepared in \(\ket0\). Give one.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Take \(U=(H\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,(H\otimes I)\). For \(\ket+\ket0\): \(H\otimes I\) gives \(\ket{00}\), CNOT leaves it, and \(H\otimes H\) gives \(\ket+\ket+\). For \(\ket-\ket0\): \(\ket{10}\to\ket{11}\to\ket-\ket-\). Orthogonal states can be copied; the no-cloning theorem forbids only a cloner for nonorthogonal ones.

  3. A cloning machine may change its own state: \(U\ket\psi\ket0\ket e=\ket\psi\ket\psi\ket{e_\psi}\), where the final machine state \(\ket{e_\psi}\) may depend on \(\ket\psi\). Show that it can still clone two states only if they are orthogonal or equal up to phase.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Inner products are preserved: \(\braket\phi\psi=\braket\phi\psi^2\braket{e_\phi}{e_\psi}\). Taking magnitudes with \(c=|\braket\phi\psi|\), we get \(c=c^2|\braket{e_\phi}{e_\psi}|\leq c^2\). So \(c=0\) or \(c\geq1\); since \(c\leq1\) for unit vectors, \(c=0\) or \(c=1\). The states are orthogonal, or equal up to phase. A machine that keeps a record of what it copied gains nothing.

5.3.3 Local states and no-signalling§

Alice and Bob share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\). Bob’s local state is \(I/2\): every orthonormal qubit-basis measurement gives two equally likely outcomes. Alice cannot change this distribution by applying a local unitary or by making a measurement whose result she does not communicate.

For the computational-basis example, Alice gets zero or one with equal probability. Conditional on her outcome, Bob has the matching basis state. Without her message, he averages over the alternatives:

\[\tfrac12\ket0\bra0+\tfrac12\ket1\bra1=I/2.\]

Changing Alice’s measurement basis changes the conditional ensemble, but not Bob’s averaged density matrix. Appendix D gives the general local-operation argument.

This is why the correlations cannot carry a chosen message by themselves. The outcome is not under Alice’s control, and Bob’s local statistics do not reveal her choice. Learning a remote outcome changes his conditional predictions only after the ordinary message arrives.

Problems

  1. Alice and Bob share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\). Alice measures her qubit and does not report the result. What is the probability that Bob obtains \(0\) in a computational-basis measurement?

    Show solutionHide solution

    With probability \(1/2\), Alice obtains \(0\) and Bob’s qubit is \(\ket0\); otherwise it is \(\ket1\). Bob obtains \(0\) with probability \(\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot0=\tfrac12\), exactly as without Alice’s measurement.

  2. Alice applies \(X\) to her qubit of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), without measuring. Find the new joint state and Bob’s computational-basis probabilities.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \((X\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+}=(\ket{10}+\ket{01})/\sqrt2\). Bob obtains \(0\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/2\) each, as before. Only the correlations between the two qubits have changed.

  3. Alice measures her half of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) in either the computational basis or the \(\pm\) basis, without reporting her result. Bob tests for any normalized state \(\ket v=a\ket0+b\ket1\). Show that his probability of success is \(1/2\) for either choice. Why is this stronger than checking only Bob’s computational-basis outcomes?

    Show solutionHide solution

    For Alice’s computational-basis choice, Bob’s probability is \(\tfrac12|a|^2+\tfrac12|b|^2=1/2\). For her \(\pm\) choice, it is \(\tfrac14|a+b|^2+\tfrac14|a-b|^2=\tfrac12(|a|^2+|b|^2)=1/2\). The cross terms cancel. Thus changing Bob’s measurement basis cannot reveal Alice’s choice. Matching probabilities in just one basis would not establish this: \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\), for example, agree in the computational basis but are distinguishable in another.

5.4

Quantum Teleportation

No-cloning and no-signalling say what entanglement cannot do. Teleportation shows what it can: with a shared Bell pair and two classical bits, an unknown state moves from sender to receiver without the qubit that holds it travelling. We follow the joint state through the sender’s operations and measurement, find the corrections the receiver must apply, and check that neither limit is violated.

5.4.1 Entanglement resources§

Alice holds a qubit in an unknown state \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) and wants Bob to have it. She cannot copy it, and measuring it would reveal only one bit. What can she do if they share a Bell pair?

The three qubits start in \(\ket\psi\otimes\ket{\Phi^+}\); Alice holds the first two and Bob the third. Alice applies CNOT from her input qubit to her half of the Bell pair, then \(H\) to her input qubit. Nothing quantum is sent to Bob. To see what has happened, we follow the complete three-qubit state.

Problems

  1. In teleportation, how many qubits does each party hold at the start? Write the initial three-qubit state for \(\ket\psi=\ket1\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Alice holds two qubits, the input and her half of the Bell pair; Bob holds one. The state is \(\ket1\otimes\tfrac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}=\tfrac{\ket{100}+\ket{111}}{\sqrt2}\).

  2. For \(\ket\psi=\ket1\), apply Alice’s CNOT and \(H\), and group the result by the configuration of Alice’s two qubits.

    Show solutionHide solution

    CNOT gives \((\ket{110}+\ket{101})/\sqrt2\). \(H\) on the first qubit gives \(\tfrac12\bigl(\ket{010}+\ket{001}-\ket{110}-\ket{101}\bigr)\). Grouped: \(\tfrac12\bigl(\ket{00}\ket1+\ket{01}\ket0-\ket{10}\ket1-\ket{11}\ket0\bigr)\), which matches \(X^nZ^m\ket1\) for each outcome \((m,n)\).

  3. Suppose Alice sends Bob only the bit \(m\), and Bob applies \(Z^m\). Show that for \(\ket\psi=\ket0\) his qubit is wrong with probability \(1/2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Bob’s qubit before correction is \(X^nZ^m\ket0=X^n\ket0=\ket n\), and \(Z^m\) does not change a basis state, up to sign. With probability \(1/2\), \(n=1\) and Bob holds \(\ket1\) instead of \(\ket0\). Both classical bits are needed.

5.4.2 Measurement and conditional corrections§

Write Alice’s unknown input as \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\). After her CNOT and Hadamard, grouping the three-qubit state by her two basis labels gives

\[\frac12\bigl(\ket{00}\ket\psi+\ket{01}X\ket\psi+\ket{10}Z\ket\psi+\ket{11}XZ\ket\psi\bigr).\]

This identity is the protocol. Every branch has squared norm \(1/4\), regardless of \(\alpha,\beta\). Alice measures her two qubits, sends their values \((m,n)\), and Bob receives the conditional state \(X^nZ^m\ket\psi\).

Alice’s bitsBob’s conditional stateBob applies, in time order
00\(\ket\psi\)Nothing
01\(X\ket\psi\)\(X\)
10\(Z\ket\psi\)\(Z\)
11\(XZ\ket\psi\)\(X\), then \(Z\)

The correction works because \(Z^mX^nX^nZ^m=I\). Nothing requires Bob to know the amplitudes. He only needs to know which reversible disturbance to undo. All four outcomes work; teleportation does not wait for a lucky one.

To check the identity, expand the initial state \((\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)(\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\), apply Alice’s two gates to each term, and collect equal first-two-bit labels. The next problems let you test the same mechanism on specific inputs.

Problems

  1. Alice obtains \((m,n)=(1,0)\). Which state does Bob hold, and which correction does he apply?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Bob holds \(X^0Z^1\ket\psi=Z\ket\psi\), and applies \(Z\), since \(ZZ=I\).

  2. For \(\ket\psi=\ket+\) and Alice’s result \((1,1)\), write Bob’s state before correction, and check that the correction restores \(\ket+\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Bob holds \(XZ\ket+=X\ket-=-\ket-\). He applies \(X\), giving \(-X\ket-=\ket-\), and then \(Z\), giving \(Z\ket-=\ket+\).

  3. Bob applies his corrections in the wrong order: \(Z^m\) first, then \(X^n\). Show that he still obtains \(\ket\psi\), up to a global phase.

    Show solutionHide solution

    He applies \(X^nZ^m\) to \(X^nZ^m\ket\psi\). If \(m=0\) or \(n=0\), this is \(X^{2n}\ket\psi\) or \(Z^{2m}\ket\psi\), which is \(\ket\psi\). If \(m=n=1\), it is \(XZXZ\ket\psi\); since \(ZX=-XZ\), \(XZXZ=-XXZZ=-I\), giving \(-\ket\psi\), the same state up to a global phase.

5.4.3 State transfer and classical communication§

Before the classical bits arrive, Bob averages over four possible corrections. The resulting state is \(I/2\), independently of \(\ket\psi\). His qubit is already present, but he cannot use it as the recovered input or learn the input’s amplitudes from it. The useful transfer waits for the message.

Nor is there a second copy left with Alice. Her measured qubits are basis states, and the shared Bell pair has been consumed. Conditioned on the communicated outcome and Bob’s correction, the unknown input is now at Bob.

Keep three descriptions separate: the full joint state, Bob’s state given Alice’s result, and Bob’s state when that result is unknown. Confusing them makes teleportation look like instantaneous communication or cloning. Tracking them turns it into an explicit calculation with the rules we already have.

Problems

  1. Before the classical bits arrive, what is the probability that Bob obtains \(0\) when measuring his qubit, for \(\ket\psi=\ket0\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    His qubit is \(X^nZ^m\ket0=\pm\ket n\), with \(n=0\) or \(1\) equally likely, so he obtains \(0\) with probability \(1/2\).

  2. Show that, before the bits arrive, Bob obtains \(0\) with probability \(1/2\) for every input \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(Z^m\) changes only signs, so \(Z^m\ket\psi\) gives \(0\) with probability \(|\alpha|^2\); \(X\) swaps the amplitudes, giving \(|\beta|^2\). Averaging over \(n\), Bob obtains \(0\) with probability \(\tfrac12|\alpha|^2+\tfrac12|\beta|^2=\tfrac12\), independent of \(\psi\).

  3. After Bob’s correction, write the three-qubit state for a particular measurement result \((m,n)\). Do Alice’s qubits retain a second copy? Explain the scope of your conclusion.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The conditional state is \(\ket m\ket n\ket\psi\). Alice’s two qubits are basis states, and each pair \((m,n)\) has probability \(1/4\), independent of \(\psi\). Her remaining registers and the classical record therefore contain no second copy of the unknown input. This describes the systems in this protocol; it does not assert that an external preparer could not already have held another copy.

1. Quick check §

Is \((\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10})/\sqrt3\) entangled?

Hint 1

A product state satisfies \(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}\).

Hint 2

Here \(\alpha_{11}=0\).

Worked solution

\(\alpha_{00}\alpha_{11}=0\) but \(\alpha_{01}\alpha_{10}=1/3\), so the state is not a product: it is entangled.

2. Worked problem §

Prepare \(\ket{\Psi^-}=(\ket{01}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2\) from \(\ket{00}\) using \(X\), \(H\), and CNOT gates. Show that measuring both qubits in the same basis \(\{\cos\theta\ket0+\sin\theta\ket1,\,-\sin\theta\ket0+\cos\theta\ket1\}\) always gives opposite results.

Hint 1

First prepare \(\ket{11}\), then apply the Bell circuit.

Hint 2

\(\braket{u_a\otimes u_b}{\Psi^-}\) is a \(2\times2\) determinant divided by \(\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

\(X\otimes X\) gives \(\ket{11}\); \(H\) on the first qubit gives \((\ket{01}-\ket{11})/\sqrt2\); CNOT gives \((\ket{01}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2\). For real basis vectors, \(\braket{u_a\otimes u_b}{\Psi^-}=\bigl(u_a(0)u_b(1)-u_a(1)u_b(0)\bigr)/\sqrt2\), which is \(0\) when \(a=b\). So equal results never occur, in every such basis.

3. Extension §

Teleport \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). For each of Alice’s four outcomes \((m,n)\), give Bob’s state before correction and the correction he applies.

Hint 1

Bob holds \(X^nZ^m\ket\psi\).

Hint 2

He applies \(X^n\), then \(Z^m\).

Worked solution

\((0,0)\): \((\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), no correction. \((0,1)\): \((i\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), apply \(X\). \((1,0)\): \((\ket0-i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), apply \(Z\). \((1,1)\): \((-i\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), apply \(X\) and then \(Z\). In every case the result is \(\ket\psi\).
