Chapter 04

Quantum States and Measurement

Complex amplitudes describe computation through two composition rules: multiply along paths, add at common endpoints, and take squared magnitudes to obtain probabilities. Normalization restricts the allowed states and transitions. Kets then express these amplitude lists as quantum states, and the measurement postulate specifies both a reported outcome and the state left afterward.

State vectors give coordinates for quantum states. Inner products give amplitudes from initial states to final directions and define their lengths and overlaps. Matrices describe transitions between states, while adjoints connect this geometry to normalization and unitarity. With these tools, the state and measurement rules extend to finite systems with any number of configurations.

Different orthonormal bases give different measurement procedures. Their inner products determine outcome probabilities, while eigenspaces attach numerical values to outcomes. The same inner products also limit how well a measurement can distinguish two preparations.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Use state vectors and inner products to describe transition amplitudes and geometry, then express operations as matrices and calculate measurement outcomes.

Before you begin

The prerequisites are complex amplitudes, normalization, path sums, the cross-sum condition, and the state update after an ideal configuration measurement. Appendix C reviews vectors and matrices.

What you should be able to do

  • Write states as vectors and interpret inner products as final-state amplitudes.
  • Represent operations as matrices and use adjoints to test unitarity.
  • Compute measurement probabilities and post-measurement states in any orthonormal basis.
  • Explain why nonorthogonal states cannot be perfectly distinguished.

The example to follow

Compare state vectors by their inner products, then follow the Hadamard operation from transition amplitudes to a matrix and a choice of measurement basis.

Scope and assumptions

The main text treats finite-dimensional pure states and projective measurements. Mixed states and general measurements are in Appendix D.

4.1

Vectors and Matrices

A state vector collects amplitudes into one object. Inner products compare an initial state with a final direction, giving transition amplitudes and the geometry of state space. Matrices then describe operations on these vectors, and their adjoints express the conditions for reversible, normalization-preserving evolution.

4.1.1 State vectors and ket notation§

state vector · ket

A qubit state is described by two amplitudes \((v_0,v_1)\), or equivalently by the ket \(v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\). The sum and product rules act on these amplitudes together. Collecting them into a column gives the state vector:

\[\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}v_0\\v_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad \ket1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The ket \(\ket\psi\) names the whole vector. Its coordinates are the amplitudes, and their squared magnitudes give the probabilities of the two configuration outcomes. Normalization is therefore \(|v_0|^2+|v_1|^2=1\).

The same notation works with more configurations. For two qubits, choose the order \(00,01,10,11\). Then

\[\frac{\ket{00}-\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The first coordinate belongs to \(00\), the last to \(11\). Changing the coordinate order without changing the labels would describe a different state. The chosen order is part of the coordinate convention.

The definite configuration vectors form the computational basis. Other bases are possible. For example, \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) is a superposition in the computational basis, but it can itself be one of the vectors in another basis. A change of basis changes the coordinates used to describe the state. It does not change the state.

Problems

  1. Write \(\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\ket0+i\sqrt{\tfrac23}\ket1\) as a column vector, and check that its coordinates satisfy the condition for a state vector.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The vector is \(\bigl(1/\sqrt3,\;i\sqrt{2/3}\bigr)^T\). The squared magnitudes are \(1/3\) and \(|i|^2\cdot2/3=2/3\), which add to \(1\).

  2. With the two-qubit configurations ordered \(00,01,10,11\), write \((\ket{00}-\ket{11})/\sqrt2\) as a column vector, and give the probability of each configuration.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The vector is \((1/\sqrt2,\,0,\,0,\,-1/\sqrt2)^T\). The configurations \(00\) and \(11\) each have probability \(1/2\); \(01\) and \(10\) have probability \(0\).

  3. Find numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(\ket0=a\,\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}+b\,\tfrac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}\). Relative to which basis is \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) a superposition, and relative to which is it not?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Comparing coordinates: \((a+b)/\sqrt2=1\) and \((a-b)/\sqrt2=0\), so \(a=b=1/\sqrt2\). The state \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) is a superposition of \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\), but in the basis \(\{(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2,(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\}\) it is a single basis vector, while \(\ket0\) becomes a superposition. Whether a state is a superposition depends on the basis.

4.1.2 Inner products and transition amplitudes§

inner product · transition amplitude · bra · norm · orthogonal · orthonormal · conjugate symmetry

A state vector lists the amplitudes for the computational-basis configurations. The amplitude for a final configuration \(j\), given an initial state \(\ket\psi\), is its coordinate \(\psi_j\), written \(\braket j\psi\). More generally, the amplitude associated with a normalized final state \(\ket\phi\) is written with the final state on the left and the initial state on the right:

\[\mathcal A(\text{final}\leftarrow\text{initial})=\braket{\text{final}}{\text{initial}}=\braket\phi\psi.\]

Here the final state specifies the outcome being tested. In an orthonormal measurement basis containing \(\ket\phi\), the probability of that outcome is \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\). Any evolution before the test changes the initial vector to the state reached by that evolution.

The quantity \(\braket\phi\psi\) is the inner product of the two state vectors. In coordinates it is

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j.\]

Exchanging the initial and final states conjugates the amplitude:

\[\braket\psi\phi=\sum_j\psi_j^*\phi_j=\left(\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j\right)^*=\bigl(\braket\phi\psi\bigr)^*.\]

This reversal property is called conjugate symmetry. The phase changes sign, while the magnitude stays the same. Thus the probabilities for the corresponding state tests, with no intervening evolution, agree:

\[p(\psi\mid\phi)=|\braket\psi\phi|^2=|\braket\phi\psi|^2=p(\phi\mid\psi).\]

For example, if \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), then \(\braket1\psi=i/\sqrt2\), while \(\braket\psi1=-i/\sqrt2\). The amplitudes differ, but both give probability \(1/2\). For real vectors, conjugation changes nothing, so the inner product is unchanged by reversal.

The coordinates of the final state are conjugated. For a final basis state \(\ket j\), only its \(j\)th coordinate is nonzero, so the formula gives \(\braket j\psi=\psi_j\), as required. For the same initial and final state, it gives \(\braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\psi_j|^2\). Normalization therefore becomes \(\braket\psi\psi=1\).

The conjugated row belonging to the final ket is called its bra:

\[\ket\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_0\\\phi_1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \bra\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_0^*&\phi_1^*\end{pmatrix}.\]

Multiplying this row by the initial column \(\ket\psi\) gives the transition amplitude \(\braket\phi\psi\).

This inner product gives the state space a geometry. The norm of a vector is its length, \(\|\psi\|=\sqrt{\braket\psi\psi}\), so normalized states are unit vectors. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero. Mutually orthogonal unit vectors form an orthonormal set. In particular, the computational-basis vectors satisfy \(\braket jk=1\) for \(j=k\) and zero otherwise.

For a normalized final vector \(\ket\phi\), the initial vector separates into a component along \(\ket\phi\) and a component orthogonal to it:

\[\ket\psi=\braket\phi\psi\,\ket\phi+\ket{\psi_\perp},\qquad \braket\phi{\psi_\perp}=0.\]

The transition amplitude is exactly the coefficient of the component along the final direction. For normalized \(\ket\psi\), orthogonality gives

\[1=|\braket\phi\psi|^2+\|\psi_\perp\|^2.\]

Thus its squared magnitude lies between zero and one. It is one when the two vectors differ only by a global phase, and zero when they are orthogonal. In a plane of real unit vectors, \(\braket\phi\psi=\cos\theta\), where \(\theta\) is their angle. Complex state vectors extend this geometry while retaining phase in the inner product.

For example, with \(\ket\phi=\ket0\) and \(\ket\psi=(\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\), the amplitude is \(\braket0\psi=\sqrt3/2\) and the probability is \(3/4\). The remaining component, \((1/2)\ket1\), is orthogonal to the final direction.

Problems

  1. Compute \(\braket\phi\psi\) for \(\ket\phi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket\psi=(\ket0-i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Are the two states orthogonal?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket\phi\psi=\tfrac12\bigl(1\cdot1+(i)^*(-i)\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(1+(-i)(-i)\bigr)=\tfrac12(1-1)=0\). They are orthogonal. Without the conjugate, the sum would be \(\tfrac12(1+1)=1\), wrongly suggesting the two states are equal.

  2. For \(\ket\psi=(\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(i/2)\ket1\), find the amplitudes \(\braket0\psi\) and \(\braket1\psi\), their probabilities, and the norm of \(\ket\psi\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The final-state amplitudes are \(\sqrt3/2\) for \(\ket0\) and \(i/2\) for \(\ket1\). Their squared magnitudes are \(3/4\) and \(1/4\). Since they sum to one, \(\|\psi\|=1\).

  3. Let \(\ket\phi=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket\psi=\ket0\). Find the component \(\braket\phi\psi\,\ket\phi\) and the orthogonal remainder. Check that their squared norms add to one.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket\phi\psi=1/\sqrt2\), so the component along \(\ket\phi\) is \((\ket0+\ket1)/2\). Subtracting it from \(\ket0\) leaves \(\ket{\psi_\perp}=(\ket0-\ket1)/2\). Its inner product with \(\ket\phi\) is zero. Both components have squared norm \(1/2\), giving total one.

4.1.3 Transition matrices§

matrix · matrix product · identity matrix · linearity

A transition acts on the whole state vector. Each input contribution follows its allowed arrows, and the contributions arriving at the same configuration add. Geometrically, the operation moves each basis direction and combines the moved components to give the new vector.

For a qubit state \(\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\), this means

\[U\ket\psi=v_0U\ket0+v_1U\ket1.\]

The coefficients weight the images of the original basis vectors. Their sum is the output state, whose coordinates in the fixed computational basis will generally differ from the input coordinates. This is linearity: an operation carries a sum of input contributions to the same sum of their outputs. More generally,

\[U(a\ket\phi+b\ket\psi)=aU\ket\phi+bU\ket\psi.\]

A transition matrix records the amplitudes for this motion between basis configurations. Its entry \(U_{ji}\) is the amplitude to arrive at \(j\) from \(i\):

\[U_{ji}=\bra jU\ket i=w(i\to j).\]

We use column \(i\) for the initial configuration and row \(j\) for the final one. This convention places the amplitudes of \(U\ket i\) together. The matrix is thus a coordinate description of the same transition graph.

For a general input, the amplitude arriving at \(j\) is found by carrying each \(v_i\) along the arrow \(i\to j\) and adding:

\[v'_j=\sum_iw(i\to j)v_i=\sum_iU_{ji}v_i,\qquad \ket{\psi'}=U\ket\psi.\]

Matrix–vector multiplication performs these path sums. The resulting coordinates describe where the state vector has moved.

For example, consider the real transition

\[U=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}4&-3\\3&4\end{pmatrix}.\]

It rotates \(\ket0\) to \((4/5)\ket0+(3/5)\ket1\) and \(\ket1\) to \(-(3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\). The two directions remain perpendicular and retain their lengths. For \(\ket\psi=(3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\), the weighted images have horizontal components that cancel and vertical components that add to one. The vector therefore moves to \(\ket1\).

The familiar gates also have simple geometric actions. \(X\), or NOT, exchanges the two basis directions. \(Z\) changes the sign of the component along \(\ket1\). \(H\) sends the basis vectors to the two perpendicular diagonal directions \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \((\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Their transition matrices record these actions:

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Here \(Z\) can also be written \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\). The drawings use real vectors; the same linear description applies when amplitudes and transition weights are complex.

After the operation, comparison with a final state \(\ket\phi\) uses the inner product:

\[\mathcal A(\phi\leftarrow\psi;U)=\bra\phi U\ket\psi.\]

The operation moves the initial vector, and the final bra selects its component along the final direction. For definite initial and final configurations, this is the arrow amplitude \(U_{ji}\).

Two successive operations move the vector twice. If \(U\) acts first and \(V\) second, the final state is \(VU\ket\psi\). The amplitude between two configurations still comes from multiplying along each route and summing over the intermediate configuration:

\[(VU)_{ki}=\sum_jV_{kj}U_{ji}.\]

This defines the matrix product. The rightmost matrix acts first. For example, two Hadamard steps restore every input vector, so \(HH=I\), where \(I\) is the identity matrix.

Problems

  1. An operation has weights \(w(0\to0)=0\), \(w(0\to1)=i\), \(w(1\to0)=-i\), and \(w(1\to1)=0\). Write its matrix, and apply it to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    With \(U_{ji}=w(i\to j)\), the matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\). Its columns give \(U\ket0=i\ket1\) and \(U\ket1=-i\ket0\).

  2. Compute the matrices \(XH\) and \(HX\), and apply each to \(\ket0\). Can the outputs differ by only a global phase?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(XH=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(HX=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\). Their first columns give \(XH\ket0=\ket+\) and \(HX\ket0=\ket-\). These states are orthogonal, so they do not differ by a global phase. A measurement in the \(\pm\) basis distinguishes them with certainty.

  3. A proposed rule sends \(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) to \(\alpha\ket0+2\beta\ket1\), rescaled so that the squared magnitudes add to \(1\). Show that the rule is not linear by comparing its action on \(\ket0\), \(\ket1\), and \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The rule sends \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) to themselves after normalization. A linear map with those actions would be the identity. On \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), the rule instead gives \((\ket0+2\ket1)/\sqrt5\). It is therefore nonlinear and cannot describe deterministic closed-system evolution.

4.1.4 Adjoints and unitary operations§

adjoint · unitary

An operation changes a vector and therefore changes its overlap with a final direction. The same overlap can be calculated by leaving the initial vector fixed and applying a corresponding operation to the final vector. This operation is the adjoint, written \(U^\dagger\):

\[\bra\phi U\ket\psi=\braket{U^\dagger\phi}\psi.\]

On the left, \(U\) acts on the initial vector \(\ket\psi\). On the right, \(U^\dagger\) acts on the comparison vector \(\ket\phi\). The inner product has the same value in both descriptions. The transformed comparison vector need not be normalized when \(U\) is a general matrix.

The reversal property of the inner product gives the corresponding relation between basis transitions:

\[\bra iU^\dagger\ket j=\bigl(\bra jU\ket i\bigr)^*,\qquad (U^\dagger)_{ij}=U_{ji}^*.\]

In a transition graph, the adjoint reverses each arrow and conjugates its amplitude. In matrix notation, this is the conjugate transpose.

For closed-system evolution, normalization must survive for every state. Geometrically, the images of an orthonormal basis must remain unit vectors and remain mutually orthogonal. Otherwise, some combination of input components would acquire a different total squared length.

The overlap of the two images \(U\ket i\) and \(U\ket k\) can be written using the adjoint:

\[\braket{Ui}{Uk}=\bra iU^\dagger U\ket k.\]

The left side tests whether the transformed directions retain the geometry of the original basis: it must be one for the same basis vector and zero for distinct ones. The right side is also the amplitude to go from \(\ket k\) to \(\ket i\) by applying \(U\) and then \(U^\dagger\). Those requirements make every input return to itself:

\[U^\dagger U=I.\]

An operation satisfying this equation is unitary. Its adjoint is its inverse. It preserves lengths and inner products, so the orthogonal components of a state remain orthogonal as they move. For example, \(H^\dagger=H\), and applying it after \(H\) restores the original state.

The adjoint is defined more generally, even when the transformation does not preserve this geometry. For a real matrix, conjugation changes nothing, so the adjoint is the transpose. Consider

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The action of \(A\) is a horizontal shear: it slides each point horizontally by an amount set by its vertical coordinate. Its adjoint is a vertical shear. The initial state \(\ket\psi=(3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\) is therefore sent in different directions by the two transformations. The angle arcs compare those changes directly.

Applying one shear after the other moves the vector again. The result depends on their order: \(AA^\dagger\) applies the vertical shear first, while \(A^\dagger A\) applies the horizontal shear first. Neither sequence restores this initial vector.

To undo \(A\), we must slide horizontally in the opposite direction. Its inverse is \(A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\), rather than the vertical shear \(A^\dagger\). The adjoint transfers the action across an inner product; it undoes the action only when the operation is unitary.

These shears illustrate general linear maps, whose images need not have unit length. They are not closed-system quantum gates. The inner-product identity defining the adjoint still holds:

\[\bra\phi A\ket\psi=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi.\]

Problems

  1. Find the adjoints of \(\operatorname{diag}(1,i)\) and of \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Which of the two matrices is unitary?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\operatorname{diag}(1,i)^\dagger=\operatorname{diag}(1,-i)\), and their product is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,(-i)i)=I\), so the first is unitary. For the second, the adjoint is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\), and \(A^\dagger A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}\neq I\), so it is not.

  2. For \(U=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}\), find \(U^\dagger\) and compute \(U^\dagger U\). Interpret the four entries as transition amplitudes for the two-step process.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(U^\dagger=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}\). Their product has diagonal entries \((1+(-i)i)/2=1\) and off-diagonal entries \((i-i)/2=0\), so \(U^\dagger U=I\). After the two steps, the amplitude to return to the initial configuration is one, and the amplitude to reach the other configuration is zero.

  3. Using \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\), prove that \((AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger\). Conclude that the product of two unitaries is unitary.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For all \(\ket\phi,\ket\psi\): \(\braket\phi{AB\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}{B\psi}=\braket{B^\dagger A^\dagger\phi}\psi\). This is the defining property of \((AB)^\dagger\), so \((AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger\). For unitaries \(U,V\): \((UV)^\dagger UV=V^\dagger U^\dagger UV=V^\dagger V=I\).

4.2

Postulates

The vector and matrix notation expresses the amplitude rules without changing them. To apply those rules to physical systems, we specify the state, evolution, and measurement postulates. A measurement gives both a probability for its result and a state to use in the next calculation.

4.2.1 State and evolution postulates§

postulate

The amplitude rules have led to normalized vectors and unitary matrices. To use them as a theory of physical systems, we must specify which preparations and processes they describe. These physical assumptions are the postulates of the model.

State postulate. A pure state of a finite quantum system is represented by a unit vector in a complex inner-product space. Vectors differing only by a global phase represent the same physical state.

For a qubit, this means \(\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\), with \(|v_0|^2+|v_1|^2=1\). A common phase can make the first nonzero coordinate real and nonnegative. When both coordinates are nonzero, the remaining freedom is their relative magnitude and phase. It is convenient to write

\[\ket\psi=\cos\frac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\ket1,\qquad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi.\]

The angle \(\theta\) sets the two magnitudes, whose squares add to one; \(\phi\) sets their relative phase. At \(\theta=0\) or \(\pi\), one amplitude vanishes, so no relative phase remains.

Evolution postulate. The evolution of a closed system is unitary:

\[\ket\psi\longmapsto U\ket\psi,\qquad U^\dagger U=I.\]

The equation ensures that normalization is preserved, and \(U^\dagger\) reverses the evolution. It is the matrix form of the transition conditions derived from the proposed probability rule. Identifying such evolution with the behavior of a closed physical system is a postulate.

A pure-state vector describes a preparation with definite amplitudes. If a preparation is selected at random, or a subsystem is correlated with another system, a single ket need not describe the available state. Such situations require density matrices, developed later and in Appendix D. For now, the state and evolution postulates apply to pure states of finite closed systems.

Problems

  1. Normalize \(\ket0+2i\ket1\), and give the probabilities of a computational-basis measurement.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Show solutionHide solution

    A sequence of operations is a matrix \(V\), and \(V(e^{i\gamma}\ket\psi)=e^{i\gamma}V\ket\psi\) by linearity. Every coordinate is multiplied by \(e^{i\gamma}\), which has magnitude \(1\), so every probability is unchanged. If \(\ket0-\ket1=e^{i\gamma}(\ket0+\ket1)\), comparing coordinates would give \(e^{i\gamma}=1\) and \(e^{i\gamma}=-1\) at once, which is impossible.

4.2.2 The measurement postulate§

measurement postulate

Unitary evolution carries all amplitudes forward. An ideal measurement instead reports one outcome and leaves a state conditional on that outcome. The computational-basis measurement rule applies to any finite set of configurations.

Measurement postulate. For a normalized state

\[\ket\psi=\sum_jv_j\ket j,\]

an ideal measurement in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|v_j|^2\). Conditional on that outcome, the state becomes \(\ket j\).

The probability rule determines how often each result occurs across independently prepared systems. The state update determines what happens in a later operation on the measured system. For example, measuring

\[\ket\psi=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\ket0+\frac12\ket1\]

gives \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\). If the result is \(1\), a second measurement in the same basis immediately gives \(1\) again, since the state is now \(\ket1\). This repeatability is a property of the ideal measurement described by the postulate.

The update also explains the effect of measuring between two Hadamard steps. Starting from \(\ket0\), the first \(H\) gives \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). A measurement leaves either \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\). Applying \(H\) to either state then gives equal probabilities, whereas two uninterrupted Hadamard steps return to \(\ket0\).

The conditional measurement update is not a unitary operation on the measured system. After outcome \(0\), both an initial \(\ket0\) and an initial \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) leave the same state \(\ket0\). That state alone cannot recover the input. This does not prevent a larger system, including the measuring apparatus and its record, from evolving unitarily; the conditional description keeps only the reported branch.

4.2.3 Repeated measurements on independently prepared states§

A measurement probability concerns repeated preparations of the same state. To estimate it, prepare a fresh system on each run and measure each preparation in the same way. If an outcome has probability \(p\), then \(N\) independent runs give an expected count \(Np\).

The observed count fluctuates. Writing each run as an indicator that is one when the outcome occurs and zero otherwise, each indicator has variance \(p(1-p)\). Independent variances add, so the count has variance \(Np(1-p)\). Dividing by \(N\) gives the standard deviation of the observed fraction:

\[\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}N}.\]

For \(p=1/2\), this is \(0.05\) with 100 runs and \(0.005\) with 10,000 runs. Reducing this fluctuation scale by a factor of ten requires a hundred times as many preparations. The standard deviation gives a scale for variation, not a bound on every observed fraction.

Repeatedly measuring one system uses a different preparation after the first result. For example, let

\[\ket+=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

A computational-basis measurement gives either outcome with probability \(1/2\). Once it gives \(0\), the state is \(\ket0\), and immediate repetitions give \(0\) with certainty. Those repetitions do not supply independent samples of the original \(\ket+\) state. Fresh preparations of \(\ket+\) still give each result with probability \(1/2\).

Thus a run must specify both the preparation and the measurement. Repeating the preparation tests its predicted probabilities; repeating the measurement on an unchanged measured system tests repeatability.

4.3

Measurement in Other Bases

The computational basis is one choice of orthonormal coordinates. Other choices give other ways to describe a state and other measurements to perform. A change of coordinates leaves the state unchanged; a unitary before a measurement changes which basis the apparatus distinguishes.

4.3.1 Changes of basis§

\(\pm\) basis

The computational basis ties coordinates to the configuration labels. The same state can also be described using other orthonormal vectors. The Hadamard outputs provide a useful pair:

\[\ket+=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2},\qquad \ket-=\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

Each has norm one, and \(\braket+-=(1-1)/2=0\). They therefore form an orthonormal basis, the \(\pm\) basis. For \(\ket\psi=v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\), the inner products with these vectors give

\[a=\braket+\psi=\frac{v_0+v_1}{\sqrt2},\qquad b=\braket-\psi=\frac{v_0-v_1}{\sqrt2},\]

so the same state is \(\ket\psi=a\ket++b\ket-\). In particular,

\[\ket0=\frac{\ket++\ket-}{\sqrt2},\qquad \ket1=\frac{\ket+-\ket-}{\sqrt2}.\]

A definite computational-basis state thus has two nonzero coordinates in the \(\pm\) basis. This is why calling a state a superposition requires a choice of basis.

The coordinate formulas resemble the action of \(H\), but their meanings differ. Rewriting \(\ket0\) as \((\ket++\ket-)/\sqrt2\) leaves the state unchanged. Applying \(H\) gives the different state \(\ket+\). In the first calculation the basis changes; in the second the state changes while the coordinate basis is held fixed.

For any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\), the same argument gives

\[\ket\psi=\sum_j\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}.\]

Taking an inner product with \(\ket{u_k}\) removes every term except the one indexed by \(k\), which explains why the inner products are the coordinates in that basis.

4.3.2 Measurement in an orthonormal basis§

measurement in a basis · projector · projective measurement

A change of coordinates alone leaves the measurement procedure unchanged. To make a measurement distinguish another basis, we can first apply a unitary that carries its basis vectors to the computational basis. The Hadamard operation does this for the \(\pm\) basis:

\[H\ket+=\ket0,\qquad H\ket-=\ket1.\]

If \(\ket\psi=a\ket++b\ket-\), applying \(H\) gives \(a\ket0+b\ket1\). A computational-basis measurement then gives \(0\) with probability \(|a|^2\) and \(1\) with probability \(|b|^2\). These are exactly the squared magnitudes of the coordinates in the \(\pm\) basis.

The same construction works for an orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\). Put its vectors into the columns of \(U\), so \(U\ket j=\ket{u_j}\). The matrix is unitary, and \(U^\dagger\) carries those vectors back to \(\ket j\). The probability of result \(j\) after applying \(U^\dagger\) and measuring is therefore

\[p(j)=|\bra jU^\dagger\ket\psi|^2=|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2.\]

The system is then in \(\ket j\). Applying \(U\) afterward leaves it in \(\ket{u_j}\). Including this final operation gives the ideal measurement in a basis: outcome \(j\) has probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\), and its conditional state is \(\ket{u_j}\).

The component along \(\ket{u_j}\) can be selected by the projector

\[P_j=\ket{u_j}\bra{u_j},\qquad P_j\ket\psi=\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}.\]

Its squared norm gives the probability. Dividing the selected component by its norm gives the conditional state, up to an irrelevant global phase.

A measurement can also select a whole subspace. For projectors onto mutually orthogonal subspaces, with \(\sum_jP_j=I\), the ideal projective measurement rule is

\[p(j)=\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2,\qquad \ket{\psi_j}=\frac{P_j\ket\psi}{\sqrt{p(j)}}\quad\text{when }p(j)>0.\]

The probabilities sum to one because the orthogonal components account for the whole vector. If the selected subspace has more than one dimension, the outcome does not specify a single basis vector: the part of the input within that subspace survives, normalized. This distinction will matter when different states share the same measured value.

4.3.3 Overlaps under unitary evolution§

A unitary preserves the norm of each state. The inner-product formula shows that it also preserves the overlap of any two states:

\[\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\bra\phi U^\dagger U\ket\psi=\braket\phi\psi.\]

For example, \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\). Applying \(H\) sends these states to \(\ket+\) and \(\ket0\), whose inner product is still \(1/\sqrt2\). Their coordinates change, but their overlap does not. In particular, a unitary cannot turn a nonorthogonal pair into an orthogonal pair.

This constrains any attempt to make states easier to distinguish before measurement. A unitary can put a chosen orthonormal basis into the computational basis, but it cannot do the same for two nonorthogonal states. Its inverse would otherwise have to map orthogonal states back to nonorthogonal ones.

Normalizing the output of an arbitrary matrix does not generally avoid the restriction. For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)\), dividing each output by its norm leaves both \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) unchanged. A linear map with those actions would be the identity. Yet on \(\ket+\) the proposed rule gives

\[\frac{A\ket+}{\|A\ket+\|}=\frac{\ket0+2\ket1}{\sqrt5},\]

which differs from \(\ket+\). The normalization depends on the input, making this rule nonlinear. It cannot be a deterministic unitary operation.

Conditional measurement states are normalized in this way too, but they occur with specified probabilities. A branch may change overlaps; that branch alone is not the complete deterministic evolution. The distinction between unitary evolution and a conditional measurement update is therefore essential when interpreting a calculation.

4.4

Observables and Distinguishability

A projective measurement selects an outcome subspace. Assigning a real value to each subspace gives an observable, whose eigenspaces describe the possible outcomes. Orthogonality then connects the mathematics of these subspaces to a physical limit: which states a measurement can distinguish with certainty.

4.4.1 Eigenvectors and eigenspaces§

eigenvector · eigenvalue · eigenspace

So far a measurement outcome has been a configuration or subspace label. To describe a reported number, such as an energy, we need to attach a value to each outcome subspace. An operator that multiplies vectors in each subspace by that value provides such a description.

A nonzero vector satisfying

\[A\ket v=\lambda\ket v\]

is an eigenvector of \(A\), with eigenvalue \(\lambda\). Its span is preserved by \(A\); the vector is only rescaled. For example,

\[Z\ket0=\ket0,\qquad Z\ket1=-\ket1.\]

The computational-basis vectors are eigenvectors of \(Z\) with eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\). For \(X\), the corresponding eigenvectors are in the \(\pm\) basis:

\[X\ket+=\ket+,\qquad X\ket-=-\ket-.\]

If two eigenvectors have the same eigenvalue, linearity gives that eigenvalue to every nonzero combination of them. All such vectors, together with the zero vector, form an eigenspace. An eigenspace can have several dimensions, so an eigenvalue need not identify one state.

For a unitary, preservation of the norm requires \(\|U\ket v\|=|\lambda|\|v\|=\|v\|\), hence \(|\lambda|=1\). A unitary eigenvector only acquires a phase. The operators used to label measurement values will have a different requirement: their eigenvalues must be real numbers.

4.4.2 Hermitian operators and spectral projections§

observable · Hermitian · spectral projectors

Suppose a projective measurement has outcome projectors \(P_j\), and outcome \(j\) is assigned a real number \(a_j\). The operator

\[A=\sum_j a_jP_j\]

multiplies every vector in the range of \(P_j\) by \(a_j\). It therefore records both the outcome subspaces and the numbers attached to them. Such an operator is an observable.

Since \(P_j^\dagger=P_j\) and the labels are real, \(A^\dagger=A\). A matrix with this property is Hermitian. Its eigenvalues are real: if \(A\ket v=a\ket v\), then \(\bra vA\ket v=a\braket vv\), while Hermiticity makes \(\bra vA\ket v\) real. Dividing by the positive number \(\braket vv\) shows that \(a\) is real.

Eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal. Indeed, if \(A\ket v=a\ket v\) and \(A\ket w=b\ket w\), then

\[a\braket vw=\braket{Av}w=\bra vA\ket w=b\braket vw.\]

For \(a\ne b\), this forces \(\braket vw=0\). The finite-dimensional spectral theorem further ensures that these eigenspaces span the whole space. Their projectors are the spectral projectors of \(A\), and they define its ideal spectral measurement.

For example,

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}=2\ket0\bra0+5\ket1\bra1\]

has the same outcome directions as a computational-basis measurement, but reports \(2\) and \(5\). On \(v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1\), their probabilities are \(|v_0|^2\) and \(|v_1|^2\). The average over repeated preparations is

\[\langle A\rangle=\sum_j a_jp(j)=\bra\psi A\ket\psi.\]

If several basis vectors share the same value, their span is one eigenspace. The ideal spectral measurement selects the component in that whole subspace. Measuring each basis vector separately and then forgetting which occurred can remove interference within the subspace, so it need not produce the same conditional state.

4.4.3 Nonorthogonal state discrimination§

Orthogonal states can be included in one orthonormal basis. A measurement in that basis identifies which of them was prepared: each state gives its own outcome with certainty. Distinct states need not be orthogonal, however. For example, \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) have overlap \(1/\sqrt2\).

To distinguish two states \(\ket\phi\) and \(\ket\psi\) perfectly by a projective measurement, the outcomes assigned to the first must never occur for the second. Let \(P\) project onto the combined subspace of all outcomes assigned to \(\ket\phi\). Certainty for the first state and probability zero for the second require

\[P\ket\phi=\ket\phi,\qquad P\ket\psi=0.\]

Using \(P^\dagger=P\), their inner product is then

\[\braket\phi\psi=\braket{P\phi}\psi=\bra\phi P\ket\psi=0.\]

Thus perfect discrimination requires orthogonality. Together with the basis construction, this shows that two pure states can be distinguished with certainty exactly when they are orthogonal.

For \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\), the nonzero overlap rules out certainty, but a measurement can still give useful information. Suppose each state is prepared with probability \(1/2\). Measure in the computational basis and guess \(\ket+\) after result \(1\), or \(\ket0\) after result \(0\). The guess is always correct for \(\ket0\), and correct half the time for \(\ket+\). Its success probability is therefore \((1/2)1+(1/2)(1/2)=3/4\).

A different measurement can improve this success rate, but no unitary before the measurement can make it one: the overlap is preserved. Adding an apparatus or a record does not remove the restriction either. With the same initial apparatus state for both preparations, joint unitary evolution preserves the original overlap. Perfectly distinct records would place the joint outputs in orthogonal subspaces, contradicting that preservation.

The same limit holds for general measurements, beyond the projective ones used here. A procedure can sometimes identify the state without error if it also permits an inconclusive result. For nonorthogonal states, such a procedure cannot identify the preparation successfully on every run.

A measurement in a basis tilted halfway between the two states does better, reaching \(\tfrac12\bigl(1+1/\sqrt2\bigr)\approx0.85\), but no measurement reaches \(1\).

  3. Prove that two pure states can be distinguished perfectly by a measurement exactly when they are orthogonal: give a measurement when they are, and show that none exists when they are not.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For orthogonal states, include both in an orthonormal basis and measure in it. Conversely, group all outcomes identifying \(\ket\phi\) into a projector \(P\). Perfect identification requires \(P\ket\phi=\ket\phi\) and \(P\ket\psi=0\). Since \(P^\dagger=P\), \(\braket\phi\psi=\braket{P\phi}\psi=\bra\phi P\ket\psi=0\). This proves the claim for projective measurements; preserving overlap with an apparatus extends the restriction to general measurement procedures.

Check your understanding

Does an adjoint always undo an operation?

Compare your explanation

No. It transfers an operation across an inner product. It equals the inverse for unitary operators, but not for a general stretch or projection.

1. Quick check §

Is \(Z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) Hermitian, unitary, both, or neither? Answer the same for \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\).

Hint 1

Hermitian means \(A^\dagger=A\); unitary means \(A^\dagger A=I\).

Hint 2

Compute the conjugate transpose of each.

Worked solution

\(Z\) is both: \(Z^\dagger=Z\) and \(Z^\dagger Z=Z^2=I\). The second matrix is neither: its adjoint \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\) differs from it, and \(A^\dagger A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\neq I\).

2. Worked problem §

Normalize \(3\ket0+4i\ket1\). Find the probabilities of a measurement in the computational basis and in the \(\pm\) basis.

Hint 1

The squared norm is \(|3|^2+|4i|^2\).

Hint 2

\(\braket\pm\psi=(\psi_0\pm\psi_1)/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

The normalized state is \(\tfrac35\ket0+\tfrac{4i}5\ket1\). Computational basis: \(9/25\) and \(16/25\). \(\pm\) basis: \(\braket\pm\psi=(3\pm4i)/(5\sqrt2)\), each with squared magnitude \(25/50=1/2\). The relative phase \(i\) makes the \(\pm\) outcomes equally likely, although the computational-basis probabilities are unequal.

3. Extension §

Prove that no unitary maps \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) to two orthogonal states. What does this imply for any procedure that tries to identify which of the two was prepared?

Hint 1

A unitary preserves inner products.

Hint 2

\(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

\(\braket{U0}{U+}=\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\ne0\), so the outputs cannot be orthogonal. A unitary followed by a measurement cannot identify the state with certainty: perfect discrimination would require orthogonal outcome subspaces. Adding an apparatus does not change the conclusion, since the joint unitary also preserves the input overlap.
