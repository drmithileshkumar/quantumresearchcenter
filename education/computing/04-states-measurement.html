Chapter 04

Quantum States and Measurement

What can the mathematical description of a system tell us about what can be known through measurement?

The rules found for a single qubit take a compact form in vectors and matrices, and become the postulates of quantum mechanics. We then ask what a measurement in another basis reveals, which numbers a measurement can report, and why some different states can never be told apart with certainty.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Write the rules of quantum mechanics in vectors and matrices, adopt them as postulates, and derive measurement in any basis.

Before you begin

Recall the rules found for a qubit: amplitudes, the measurement rule, and the two conditions on an operation. Appendix C reviews the linear algebra.

What you should be able to do

  • Write states as vectors and operations as matrices.
  • Use inner products and adjoints, and test whether a matrix is unitary.
  • Compute measurement probabilities and post-measurement states in any orthonormal basis.
  • Explain why nonorthogonal states cannot be perfectly distinguished.

The example to follow

Calculate the same state in different bases; a change of coordinates is not itself a physical change.

Scope and assumptions

The main text treats finite-dimensional pure states and projective measurements. Mixed states and general measurements are in Appendix D.

4.1

Vectors and Matrices

The weights of a state and of an operation fit naturally into a vector and a matrix.

4.1.1 State vectors and ket notation§

The state of a qubit is a pair of amplitudes, one for each configuration. A system with more configurations, such as two qubits with configurations \(00,01,10,11\), has one amplitude for each of them. Can we treat such a list as a single object?

We can, as a column vector. List the configurations in a fixed order and write the amplitudes in that order. For a qubit,

\[\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\ket0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The bracket in \(\ket\psi\) names a vector; it is not an operation. This is Dirac notation.

The vectors \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) of the definite configurations form the computational basis. Every state is a combination of them, and the measurement rule reads its probabilities from the coordinates: \(|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\beta|^2\). The coordinates of a state vector therefore satisfy \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\).

Is “being in a superposition” a property of the state alone? No. The state \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) is a superposition of \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\), but it is itself a vector of another basis, as we will see. A superposition is always a superposition relative to some basis.

With \(n\) qubits there are \(2^n\) configurations, so a state vector has \(2^n\) coordinates.

Problems

  1. What are the coordinate vectors of \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) in the computational basis?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\ket0\) has coordinates \((1,0)^T\) and \(\ket1\) has coordinates \((0,1)^T\).

  2. Write \(3\ket0+4\ket1\) as a column vector. Which coordinate belongs to each basis vector?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The column vector is \((3,4)^T\): 3 belongs to \(\ket0\) and 4 to \(\ket1\). This vector is not yet normalized.

  3. If two people write different coordinate vectors for the same state in different bases, are they making different physical claims? What must you know before comparing their lists of amplitudes?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. Different bases can give different coordinates for the same vector. To compare the lists, identify the ordered bases and the transformation between them. Physical disagreement concerns different predictions after translating both descriptions into the same basis, not different coordinate lists by themselves.

4.1.2 Operations as matrices§

Applying an operation by path sums means writing out every arrow. Is there a more compact way? Collect the weights in a table whose entry in row \(j\) and column \(i\) is the weight of the arrow \(i\to j\). This table is a matrix \(U\), with \(U_{ji}=w(i\to j)\).

One step changes the state by \(v^\prime_j=\sum_iw(i\to j)\,v_i\). In matrix language this is \(v^\prime_j=\sum_iU_{ji}v_i\), the matrix–vector product: \(\ket{\psi^\prime}=U\ket\psi\). The sum and product rules for one step are exactly matrix multiplication.

Column \(i\) of \(U\) holds the weights of the arrows leaving \(i\), so it is the state \(U\ket i\). For the operations we have met, writing \(X\) for NOT and \(\operatorname{diag}(a,b)\) for the matrix with diagonal entries \(a,b\) and zeros elsewhere,

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1),\qquad H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Two steps, first \(U\) and then \(V\), sum over the intermediate configuration \(j\): \((VU)_{ki}=\sum_jV_{kj}U_{ji}\). This is the matrix product, with the rightmost matrix acting first. For example, \(HH=I\), the identity matrix.

Why is a matrix determined by what it does to the basis states? Because \(U(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)=\alpha\,U\ket0+\beta\,U\ket1\): each configuration’s amplitude is carried along separately and the results add. This property, linearity, is the sum rule in another form.

Which matrices are valid operations? The two conditions on the weights of an operation decide, and their compact form needs one more tool.

Problems

  1. What does linearity say about \(U(a\ket0+b\ket1)\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(U(a\ket0+b\ket1)=aU\ket0+bU\ket1\).

  2. Given \(U\ket0=\ket1\) and \(U\ket1=\ket0\), find \(U\) applied to \((\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The output is \((\ket1-\ket0)/\sqrt2=-\ket-\). Its overall minus sign is a global phase.

  3. Why does knowing an operator’s action on every basis vector determine its action on all superpositions? What could go wrong if a proposed rule were allowed to depend on how the same vector was decomposed?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A basis expansion determines coefficients uniquely, and linearity applies the same operator to each term. Consequently basis actions determine every output. A rule that produced different outputs for two decompositions of the same vector would not be a well-defined operation on that vector.

4.1.3 Inner products, bras, and adjoints§

The conditions on an operation were \(\sum_j|w(i\to j)|^2=1\) and \(\sum_jw(i\to j)^*\,w(k\to j)=0\) for \(i\neq k\). Since \(w(i\to j)=U_{ji}\), these are statements about the columns of \(U\): each column has squared magnitudes adding to \(1\), and for two different columns, the conjugated entries of one times the entries of the other add to \(0\). Such sums deserve a name.

The inner product of two vectors is

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j.\]

Why the conjugate? So that \(\braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\psi_j|^2\) is real and nonnegative. Without it, the one-coordinate vector \((i)\) would have “squared length” \(i^2=-1\). The norm \(\|\psi\|=\sqrt{\braket\psi\psi}\) is the length of \(\ket\psi\); a state vector has norm \(1\). Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is \(0\), and a basis of orthogonal unit vectors is orthonormal.

The bra \(\bra\phi\) is the row of conjugated coordinates of \(\ket\phi\), so that the row \(\bra\phi\) times the column \(\ket\psi\) is the inner product \(\braket\phi\psi\). For example, \(\bra0=(1,0)\), and \(\braket0\psi=\alpha\) picks out the first coordinate.

The conditions on an operation now say that the columns of \(U\) form an orthonormal basis. To write this as one equation, define the adjoint \(U^\dagger\) of a matrix as its conjugate transpose: \((U^\dagger)_{ij}=U_{ji}^*\). Then \((U^\dagger U)_{ik}=\sum_jU_{ji}^*U_{jk}\) is the inner product of columns \(i\) and \(k\), and the conditions say exactly

\[U^\dagger U=I.\]

A matrix with this property is unitary, and \(U^\dagger\) is its inverse: reverse each arrow and conjugate its weight.

Is the adjoint always the inverse? No. For the matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\), which is not unitary, the adjoint is \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\) itself, while the inverse is \(\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1)\). In general the adjoint is characterized by \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\): it moves a matrix from one side of an inner product to the other.

Problems

  1. What extra operation distinguishes a complex inner product from an ordinary coordinate-wise product?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Conjugate the entries of the first vector before multiplying and summing.

  2. Calculate the squared norm of \((1,i)^T\) by conjugating the first vector before multiplying.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The conjugate transpose is \((1,-i)\). Its product with \((1,i)^T\) is \(1+(-i)i=2\), so the squared norm is 2.

  3. Find the adjoint of the diagonal matrix with entries \(1\) and \(i\). Multiply the two matrices and inspect the result.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For \(U=\mathrm{diag}(1,i)\), \(U^\dagger=\mathrm{diag}(1,-i)\). Their product is \(\mathrm{diag}(1,(-i)i)=I\), so here the adjoint is also the inverse.

4.2

Postulates

The rules derived for a qubit, adopted for every quantum system.

4.2.1 State and evolution postulates§

For a qubit, the rules of quantum mechanics followed from a few observations and stated assumptions. Do they hold for every quantum system? Quantum mechanics asserts that they do. We adopt them as postulates: rules taken as the starting point of the theory and tested by experiment.

Postulate 1 (states). A state of a quantum system is a unit vector in a complex vector space whose orthonormal basis corresponds to the configurations of the system. Vectors that differ only by a global phase represent the same state.

Postulate 2 (evolution). An operation on a system that does not interact with anything else is a unitary matrix \(U\); it changes the state \(\ket\psi\) into \(U\ket\psi\).

What does a general qubit state look like? Remove a global phase so that the first coordinate is real and nonnegative. The state then has the form

\[\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1,\]

where the magnitudes fix \(\theta\) and the relative phase is \(\phi\).

Is every preparation described by a single state vector? No. If we know only that a system was prepared in one of several states with given probabilities, or if it is part of a larger system it has interacted with, a different description is needed, the density matrix of Appendix D. The main text works with single state vectors.

Problems

  1. What is the required norm of a pure-state vector?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Its norm is one, equivalently the sum of squared amplitude magnitudes is one.

  2. Normalize \(3\ket0+4\ket1\) and check that the resulting measurement probabilities sum to one.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The norm is \(\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\). Dividing by five gives \((3/5)\ket0+(4/5)\ket1\). Its probabilities are \(9/25\) and \(16/25\), summing to one.

  3. A vector and twice that vector point in the same direction. Why cannot both be used directly as normalized state vectors? Which part of the vector describes the state, and what role does normalization play in assigning probabilities?

    Show solutionHide solution

    If \(v\) has norm one, \(2v\) has norm two and would give a total squared weight of four if used directly. A pure physical state is represented by a ray: nonzero scalar multiples describe the same direction after normalization, with global phase irrelevant. Choosing a unit vector makes the Born probabilities sum to one.

4.2.2 The measurement postulate§

The measurement rule gives the probability of each outcome. What is the state after the measurement?

Measure a qubit and obtain \(0\), then measure it again immediately. Experiments find \(0\) again, every time. Which state gives \(0\) with certainty? Only \(\ket0\), up to a global phase. So after the outcome \(0\), the qubit is in the state \(\ket0\).

Postulate 3 (measurement). Measuring the state \(\sum_j\alpha_j\ket j\) in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\alpha_j|^2\), and leaves the system in the state \(\ket j\).

For \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\), the outcome is \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\); after the outcome \(1\), the state is \(\ket1\). A single run gives a single outcome; the probabilities describe how often each outcome occurs.

This also explains why measuring between two applications of \(H\) destroys their return to the start: after the measurement the qubit is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and \(H\) applied to either gives each outcome with probability \(1/2\).

Is a measurement an operation in the sense of Postulate 2? No. When the outcome is \(0\), it sends both \(\ket0\) and \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) to \(\ket0\). Different states merge, so a measurement cannot be undone and is not unitary.

Problems

  1. What does a computational-basis measurement return?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It returns one basis label, sampled with probability equal to the squared magnitude of that label’s amplitude.

  2. For \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\), calculate each outcome probability and state the conditional state after outcome 1.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(p(0)=3/4\) and \(p(1)=1/4\). Conditional on observing 1, the projected vector is \((1/2)\ket1\); normalization gives \(\ket1\).

  3. A measurement can have a multidimensional outcome subspace. Does observing its outcome determine a unique final vector? What information about the initial state can survive inside that subspace?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. For outcome projector \(P_j\), the conditional vector is \(P_j\ket\psi/\sqrt{p(j)}\), when \(p(j)>0\). If the outcome subspace has dimension greater than one, the projection can retain relative amplitudes within that subspace. The outcome identifies a subspace, not necessarily a unique vector.

4.2.3 Repeated measurements on independently prepared states§

How can a predicted probability be tested when a single run gives a single outcome? Prepare the same state many times, independently, and measure each copy.

If an outcome has probability \(p\), its count in \(N\) runs has mean \(Np\) and variance \(Np(1-p)\). The observed fraction therefore fluctuates around \(p\) by about \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\), which shrinks as \(1/\sqrt N\). A finite experiment estimates \(p\); it does not reproduce it exactly.

Is measuring one system again the same as measuring a fresh copy? No. After a measurement the system is in the observed configuration, so an immediate second measurement repeats the result with certainty. Measuring \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) gives \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\); measuring the same qubit again repeats that result, while a fresh copy again gives \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\).

The counts of an experiment therefore mean something only once both the preparation and the measurement are specified.

Problems

  1. What does “independently prepared copies” mean?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each system is prepared afresh in the specified state, without depending on the previous measurement outcome.

  2. Measure \(\ket+\) in the computational basis and obtain 0. Compare the next measurement of that same qubit with a measurement of a fresh \(\ket+\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    After the first outcome 0, the measured qubit is \(\ket0\), so an immediate repetition in the same basis returns 0 certainly. A fresh \(\ket+\) returns 0 or 1 with probability \(1/2\) each.

  3. Why is measuring one system repeatedly not generally equivalent to measuring many independently prepared copies? Which experiment would reveal the original preparation probabilities, and which would mostly test the state left by the first measurement?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Measurement generally changes the state. Fresh copies sample the original preparation distribution repeatedly; repeated measurements of one unchanged post-measurement system instead test the conditional state left by the first measurement. For an ideal projective measurement with no intervening dynamics, the same outcome repeats.

4.3

Measurement in Other Bases

A unitary followed by a measurement asks a different question of the state.

4.3.1 Changes of basis§

Is the computational basis special? Consider

\[\ket+=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2},\qquad\ket-=\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

Both are unit vectors, and \(\braket+-=\tfrac12(1-1)=0\), so they form another orthonormal basis.

Every state can be written in this basis, and its coordinates are inner products: the coefficient of \(\ket+\) in \(\ket\psi\) is \(\braket+\psi\). For example, \(\braket+0=\braket-0=1/\sqrt2\), so

\[\ket0=\frac{\ket++\ket-}{\sqrt2}.\]

Has anything physical changed? No. Writing \(\ket0\) in the \(\pm\) basis changes its coordinates, not the state. Applying a gate does change the state. The two can look alike on paper, so it matters to say which one is meant.

The same holds for any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\): \(\ket\psi=\sum_j\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}\).

Problems

  1. What are \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) in the computational basis?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\ket+=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket-=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

  2. Add the expressions for \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) to write \(\ket0\) in that basis.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Adding gives \(\ket++\ket-=\sqrt2\ket0\). Therefore \(\ket0=(\ket++\ket-)/\sqrt2\).

  3. Writing a state in a new basis and applying a gate can use similar matrix calculations. How would you distinguish a change in description from a physical change when predicting a fixed measurement?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A coordinate change transforms both the state coordinates and the matrix representing the fixed measurement, leaving its probabilities unchanged. A physical gate changes the state relative to that fixed apparatus. For example, applying \(H\) to \(\ket0\) changes its computational-basis probabilities, while merely rewriting \(\ket0\) in the plus/minus basis does not.

4.3.2 Measurement in an orthonormal basis§

Apply \(H\) to a qubit and then measure it. Which property of the original state does the outcome reveal?

\(H\) sends \(\ket+\) to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket-\) to \(\ket1\). Write the original state as \(a\ket++b\ket-\); after \(H\) it is \(a\ket0+b\ket1\). The measurement gives \(0\) with probability \(|a|^2=|\braket+\psi|^2\) and \(1\) with probability \(|\braket-\psi|^2\). “Apply \(H\), then measure” therefore measures the state in the \(\pm\) basis.

Does this work for any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\)? Let \(U\) be the unitary whose columns are the \(\ket{u_j}\), so that \(U\ket j=\ket{u_j}\). Its inverse \(U^\dagger\) sends \(\ket{u_j}\) to \(\ket j\). Applying \(U^\dagger\) and then measuring gives outcome \(j\) with probability

\[|\braket{j}{U^\dagger\psi}|^2=|\braket{U j}\psi|^2=|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2,\]

and applying \(U\) afterward turns the resulting state \(\ket j\) into \(\ket{u_j}\).

Measurement in a basis. Measuring \(\ket\psi\) in the orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\) gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\), and leaves the state \(\ket{u_j}\).

Geometrically, \(\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}\) is the projection of \(\ket\psi\) onto the direction \(\ket{u_j}\). With the projector \(P_j=\ket{u_j}\bra{u_j}\), the probability is \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\). More generally, a projective measurement is given by projectors \(P_j\) onto orthogonal subspaces with \(\sum_jP_j=I\): outcome \(j\) has probability \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\) and leaves the state \(P_j\ket\psi/\|P_j\ket\psi\|\). When a subspace has more than one dimension, the outcome identifies the subspace, not a single vector.

Problems

  1. To measure a qubit in the basis \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\), which operation is applied before a computational-basis measurement, and why?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Apply \(H\). It sends \(\ket+\) to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket-\) to \(\ket1\), so the outcome \(0\) after \(H\) occurs exactly for the \(\ket+\) component of the original state.

  2. Measure \(\ket0\) in the basis \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\). Find the outcome probabilities and the state after each outcome.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket+0=\braket-0=1/\sqrt2\), so each outcome has probability \(1/2\). After the outcome the state is \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\).

  3. Let \(U\) be the unitary with columns \(\ket{u_j}\). Show that applying \(U^\dagger\) and then measuring in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The coordinate \(j\) of \(U^\dagger\ket\psi\) is \(\braket{j}{U^\dagger\psi}=\braket{Uj}\psi=\braket{u_j}\psi\), using \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\) and \(U\ket j=\ket{u_j}\). A computational-basis measurement of \(U^\dagger\ket\psi\) gives \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\).

4.3.3 Overlaps under unitary evolution§

What does a unitary preserve? For any two states,

\[\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\braket{\phi}{U^\dagger U\psi}=\braket\phi\psi.\]

Lengths are preserved, so total probability stays \(1\), and so are the overlaps between different states. Since measurement probabilities are squared overlaps, no operation can make two states more distinguishable than they already are.

Could we allow any matrix and rescale the result to unit length afterward? No. Rescaling by a factor that depends on the state is not linear: it would break the sum rule, and the parts of a superposition would no longer evolve independently.

Measurement, by contrast, does not preserve overlaps. The states \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) have overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), yet after the outcome \(0\) both are \(\ket0\). Measurement is the one place where the description leaves unitary evolution.

Problems

  1. What equation must \(U\) satisfy to be unitary?

    Show solutionHide solution

    For a square finite-dimensional matrix, \(U^\dagger U=I\), equivalently \(UU^\dagger=I\).

  2. The matrix \(X\) swaps two coordinates. Apply it twice and use the result to identify its inverse.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(X(a,b)^T=(b,a)^T\) and a second \(X\) returns \((a,b)^T\). Thus \(X^2=I\) and \(X^{-1}=X\).

  3. Unitary evolution is reversible, while a measurement outcome can leave several possible inputs indistinguishable. Why does this not justify describing measurement as just another unitary on the measured system alone?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A unitary on the measured system alone cannot merge distinct inputs or lose their overlap information. A conditional measurement update generally can. A larger unitary model can include the apparatus and its records, but conditioning on or discarding those records is additional to unitary evolution of the system alone.

4.4

Observables and Distinguishability

Which numbers a measurement reports, and which states it can tell apart.

4.4.1 Eigenvectors and eigenspaces§

Some states are left almost unchanged by an operation. \(Z\) leaves \(\ket0\) alone and multiplies \(\ket1\) by \(-1\). A vector \(\ket v\neq0\) with \(A\ket v=\lambda\ket v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\): \(A\) keeps its direction and multiplies it by \(\lambda\).

For \(Z\), the vectors \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) are eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\). For \(X\), which exchanges \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\), we find \(X\ket+=\ket+\) and \(X\ket-=-\ket-\), with the same eigenvalues.

What can the eigenvalues of a unitary be? A unitary preserves length, so \(|\lambda|=1\). An eigenvector of a unitary only gains a phase.

If several independent eigenvectors share an eigenvalue, every combination of them is again an eigenvector with that eigenvalue. Together they form an eigenspace.

Problems

  1. What equation defines an eigenvector and its eigenvalue?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(Av=\lambda v\) with \(v\) nonzero; \(v\) is an eigenvector and \(\lambda\) its eigenvalue.

  2. \(Z\) maps \(\ket0\) to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) to \(-\ket1\). Identify the two eigenvalues.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\ket0\) has eigenvalue \(+1\) and \(\ket1\) has eigenvalue \(-1\).

  3. If two independent eigenvectors have the same eigenvalue, what happens to their superposition under the operator? Can the eigenvalue alone distinguish the two vectors or the superposition?

    Show solutionHide solution

    If \(Av=\lambda v\) and \(Aw=\lambda w\), then \(A(av+bw)=\lambda(av+bw)\). Every nonzero superposition in that eigenspace has the same eigenvalue. Reporting \(\lambda\) cannot distinguish these vectors or their superpositions; that would require a finer measurement within the eigenspace.

4.4.2 Hermitian operators and spectral projections§

A measurement often reports a number, such as an energy, rather than a label. How do we attach numbers to outcomes? Choose a real number \(a_j\) for each outcome of a projective measurement and form

\[A=\sum_ja_jP_j.\]

The eigenspaces of \(A\) are the outcome subspaces, and its eigenvalues are the reported numbers.

Such a matrix satisfies \(A^\dagger=A\); it is Hermitian. Conversely, every Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so grouping equal eigenvalues writes it in the form \(A=\sum_ja_jP_j\), its spectral decomposition. A Hermitian matrix used in this way is an observable, and measuring it gives \(a_j\) with probability \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\).

For example, \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,5)\) has eigenvectors \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) with eigenvalues \(2\) and \(5\). Measuring \(A\) is a computational-basis measurement that reports \(2\) or \(5\).

Two observables with the same eigenspaces but different eigenvalues ask the same question and report different numbers. To measure an observable is to specify both.

Problems

  1. What makes an operator Hermitian?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It equals its adjoint: \(A^\dagger=A\).

  2. For the diagonal matrix with entries 2 and 5, identify its eigenvectors, eigenvalues, and computational-basis measurement projectors.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) are eigenvectors with eigenvalues 2 and 5. The associated projectors are \(P_0=\ketbra00=\mathrm{diag}(1,0)\) and \(P_1=\ketbra11=\mathrm{diag}(0,1)\).

  3. Two Hermitian operators can share the same eigenspaces but assign them different eigenvalues. In what sense do their projective measurements ask the same question, and in what sense do they report different values?

    Show solutionHide solution

    If their spectral projectors are the same, they distinguish the same subspaces and give the same outcome probabilities and ideal conditional projections. Assigning different eigenvalues changes the numerical labels reported, and therefore quantities such as the mean reported value, without changing the partition into measured subspaces.

4.4.3 Nonorthogonal state discrimination§

Someone hands us one qubit, prepared either in \(\ket0\) or in \(\ket+\). Can we always tell which?

Suppose a measurement could. Some outcome would then occur with certainty for \(\ket0\) and never for \(\ket+\). By the measurement postulate, \(\ket0\) would lie in that outcome’s subspace and \(\ket+\) would be orthogonal to it, so \(\braket0+=0\). But \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\). No measurement identifies the two states with certainty.

Could a cleverer procedure help, such as an operation before measuring, or an interaction that writes a record into another system? Any such operation is unitary and preserves the overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), so the records cannot become orthogonal either.

The limit is structural, not a weakness of detectors. Two pure states can be distinguished perfectly exactly when they are orthogonal. A measurement can trade wrong answers for answers that report “inconclusive,” but it cannot always be right.

Problems

  1. Can two nonorthogonal states always be distinguished perfectly from one copy?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. Perfect, always-conclusive discrimination from a single copy requires orthogonal possible pure states.

  2. Calculate \(\braket{0}{+}\). Does the result allow perfect discrimination of these two states?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket0+=(\braket00+\braket01)/\sqrt2=1/\sqrt2\). Since it is nonzero, perfect discrimination is impossible under the stated requirements.

  3. Nonorthogonal states are different, yet no measurement always identifies which one was supplied from a single copy. Why is mathematical distinctness weaker than perfect experimental distinguishability? Which requirement could you relax to obtain a different task?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Distinct nonorthogonal states still have nonzero overlap, so their possible measurement responses cannot be made perfectly disjoint in a single always-conclusive test. One may allow errors, permit an inconclusive outcome, or use more independently prepared copies. Each changes the task; finitely many copies of nonorthogonal states still do not give exact deterministic discrimination.

Check your understanding

Does an adjoint always undo an operation?

Compare your explanation

No. It transfers an operation across an inner product. It equals the inverse for unitary operators, but not for a general stretch or projection.

1. Quick check §

Does \(U^\dagger\) always equal \(U^{-1}\)?

Hint 1

Compare a projection with a unitary matrix.

Hint 2

A projection can erase a component.

Worked solution

No. The adjoint is the conjugate transpose. It equals the inverse when \(U\) is unitary. A nontrivial projection has an adjoint but no inverse on the whole space.

2. Worked problem §

Normalize \(3\ket0+4i\ket1\) and calculate the probabilities of a computational-basis measurement.

Hint 1

Compute the squared norm.

Hint 2

Use \(|3|^2+|4i|^2=25\).

Worked solution

The normalized state is \((3/5)\ket0+(4i/5)\ket1\). The probabilities are \(9/25\) and \(16/25\). Conditional on outcome 1, the resulting ray is represented by \(\ket1\); its overall phase has no observable effect.

3. Extension §

Why can no unitary map both \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) to two orthogonal output states?

Hint 1

What does a unitary preserve?

Hint 2

The input inner product is \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

Unitary transformations preserve inner products. Two orthogonal outputs have inner product zero, but these inputs have inner product \(1/\sqrt2\). Such a map cannot be unitary. This is part of the obstruction to perfect discrimination of nonorthogonal states.
