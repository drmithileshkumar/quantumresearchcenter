Chapter 04

Quantum States and Measurement

We have found how a qubit must behave: its state is a list of complex amplitudes, every operation keeps their total squared magnitude, and a measurement turns amplitudes into probabilities. These rules were derived for a single qubit, through sums over paths. To use them for larger systems and longer computations, we need a compact language, and a statement of the rules that holds for every quantum system.

We first collect the weights of a state into a vector and those of an operation into a matrix, so that sums over paths become matrix multiplication and the conditions on an operation become unitarity. We then adopt the rules as the postulates of quantum mechanics, adding what a measurement does to the state. With the postulates in place, we ask what can be learned by measurement: a unitary followed by a measurement measures in another basis, an observable attaches numbers to outcomes, and the overlap of two states limits how well they can be told apart.

The next chapter applies the postulates to systems made of several parts, where states of a new kind appear.

What can the mathematical description of a system tell us about what can be known through measurement?

A state vector holds many amplitudes, but a measurement returns a single outcome. The postulates of quantum mechanics connect the two.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Write the rules of quantum mechanics in vectors and matrices, adopt them as postulates, and derive measurement in any basis.

Before you begin

Recall the rules found for a qubit: amplitudes, the measurement rule, and the two conditions on an operation. Appendix C reviews the linear algebra.

What you should be able to do

  • Write states as vectors and operations as matrices.
  • Use inner products and adjoints, and test whether a matrix is unitary.
  • Compute measurement probabilities and post-measurement states in any orthonormal basis.
  • Explain why nonorthogonal states cannot be perfectly distinguished.

The example to follow

Calculate the same state in different bases; a change of coordinates is not itself a physical change.

Scope and assumptions

The main text treats finite-dimensional pure states and projective measurements. Mixed states and general measurements are in Appendix D.

4.1

Vectors and Matrices

Path sums describe every operation, but they grow quickly: an operation on two qubits already has sixteen arrows. A compact language groups the amplitudes of a state into one object and the weights of an operation into another, so that the sum and product rules become a single multiplication. The conditions found for the weights then become one equation, which names the operations quantum mechanics allows.

4.1.1 State vectors and ket notation§

state vector · ket

A state vector collects the amplitudes into one object. Choose an order for the basis labels, then write the coefficients in that order:

\[\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\ket0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The ket brackets name a vector; they do not perform an operation. The computational-basis probabilities are \(|\alpha|^2\) and \(|\beta|^2\), which add to one.

For two qubits ordered \(00,01,10,11\), the same convention gives four coordinates. Keep that order beside a calculation until it becomes familiar: swapping two coordinates changes the represented state unless you also change the basis labels.

Superposition is basis-dependent. The vector \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) combines two computational-basis vectors, but can itself be one vector of another basis. The physical state stays the same when we change how we describe it.

Problems

  1. Write \(\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\ket0+i\sqrt{\tfrac23}\ket1\) as a column vector, and check that its coordinates satisfy the condition for a state vector.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The vector is \(\bigl(1/\sqrt3,\;i\sqrt{2/3}\bigr)^T\). The squared magnitudes are \(1/3\) and \(|i|^2\cdot2/3=2/3\), which add to \(1\).

  2. With the two-qubit configurations ordered \(00,01,10,11\), write \((\ket{00}-\ket{11})/\sqrt2\) as a column vector, and give the probability of each configuration.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The vector is \((1/\sqrt2,\,0,\,0,\,-1/\sqrt2)^T\). The configurations \(00\) and \(11\) each have probability \(1/2\); \(01\) and \(10\) have probability \(0\).

  3. Find numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(\ket0=a\,\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}+b\,\tfrac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}\). Relative to which basis is \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) a superposition, and relative to which is it not?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Comparing coordinates: \((a+b)/\sqrt2=1\) and \((a-b)/\sqrt2=0\), so \(a=b=1/\sqrt2\). The state \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) is a superposition of \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\), but in the basis \(\{(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2,(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\}\) it is a single basis vector, while \(\ket0\) becomes a superposition. Whether a state is a superposition depends on the basis.

4.1.2 Operations as matrices§

matrix · matrix product · identity matrix · linearity

Applying an operation by path sums means writing out every arrow. Is there a more compact way? Collect the weights in a table whose entry in row \(j\) and column \(i\) is the weight of the arrow \(i\to j\). This table is a matrix \(U\), with \(U_{ji}=w(i\to j)\).

One step changes the state by \(v^\prime_j=\sum_iw(i\to j)\,v_i\). In matrix language this is \(v^\prime_j=\sum_iU_{ji}v_i\), the matrix–vector product: \(\ket{\psi^\prime}=U\ket\psi\). The sum and product rules for one step are exactly matrix multiplication.

Column \(i\) of \(U\) holds the weights of the arrows leaving \(i\), so it is the state \(U\ket i\). For the operations we have met, writing \(X\) for NOT and \(\operatorname{diag}(a,b)\) for the matrix with diagonal entries \(a,b\) and zeros elsewhere,

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1),\qquad H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Two steps, first \(U\) and then \(V\), sum over the intermediate configuration \(j\): \((VU)_{ki}=\sum_jV_{kj}U_{ji}\). This is the matrix product, with the rightmost matrix acting first. For example, \(HH=I\), the identity matrix.

Why is a matrix determined by what it does to the basis states? Because \(U(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1)=\alpha\,U\ket0+\beta\,U\ket1\): each configuration’s amplitude is carried along separately and the results add. This property, linearity, is the sum rule in another form.

Which matrices are valid operations? The two conditions on the weights of an operation decide, and their compact form needs one more tool.

Problems

  1. An operation has weights \(w(0\to0)=0\), \(w(0\to1)=i\), \(w(1\to0)=-i\), and \(w(1\to1)=0\). Write its matrix, and apply it to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    With \(U_{ji}=w(i\to j)\), the matrix is \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\). Its columns give \(U\ket0=i\ket1\) and \(U\ket1=-i\ket0\).

  2. Compute the matrices \(XZ\) and \(ZX\), and apply each to \(\ket0\). Does the order of the operations matter?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(XZ=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(ZX=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\), so \(XZ=-ZX\). \(XZ\ket0=X(Z\ket0)=\ket1\), while \(ZX\ket0=Z\ket1=-\ket1\). The matrices differ. On \(\ket0\) the two results differ only by a global phase, but in general the order of operations changes the result.

  3. A proposed rule sends \(\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\) to \(\alpha\ket0+2\beta\ket1\), rescaled so that the squared magnitudes add to \(1\). Show that the rule is not linear by comparing its action on \(\ket0\), \(\ket1\), and \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    It sends \(\ket0\) to \(\ket0\), and \(\ket1\) to \(2\ket1\), rescaled to \(\ket1\). A linear rule with these values would be the identity and would leave \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) unchanged. The rule instead gives \(\ket0+2\ket1\), rescaled to \((\ket0+2\ket1)/\sqrt5\). So it is not linear, cannot be written as a matrix, and is not a valid operation.

4.1.3 Inner products, bras, and adjoints§

inner product · norm · orthogonal · orthonormal · bra · adjoint · unitary

The conditions on an operation were \(\sum_j|w(i\to j)|^2=1\) and \(\sum_jw(i\to j)^*\,w(k\to j)=0\) for \(i\neq k\). Since \(w(i\to j)=U_{ji}\), these are statements about the columns of \(U\): each column has squared magnitudes adding to \(1\), and for two different columns, the conjugated entries of one times the entries of the other add to \(0\). Such sums deserve a name.

The inner product of two vectors is

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j.\]

Why the conjugate? So that \(\braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\psi_j|^2\) is real and nonnegative. Without it, the one-coordinate vector \((i)\) would have “squared length” \(i^2=-1\). The norm \(\|\psi\|=\sqrt{\braket\psi\psi}\) is the length of \(\ket\psi\); a state vector has norm \(1\). Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is \(0\), and a basis of orthogonal unit vectors is orthonormal.

The bra \(\bra\phi\) is the row of conjugated coordinates of \(\ket\phi\), so that the row \(\bra\phi\) times the column \(\ket\psi\) is the inner product \(\braket\phi\psi\). For example, \(\bra0=(1,0)\), and \(\braket0\psi=\alpha\) picks out the first coordinate.

The conditions on an operation now say that the columns of \(U\) form an orthonormal basis. To write this as one equation, define the adjoint \(U^\dagger\) of a matrix as its conjugate transpose: \((U^\dagger)_{ij}=U_{ji}^*\). Then \((U^\dagger U)_{ik}=\sum_jU_{ji}^*U_{jk}\) is the inner product of columns \(i\) and \(k\), and the conditions say exactly

\[U^\dagger U=I.\]

A matrix with this property is unitary, and \(U^\dagger\) is its inverse: reverse each arrow and conjugate its weight.

Is the adjoint always the inverse? No. For the matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\), which is not unitary, the adjoint is \(\operatorname{diag}(2,1)\) itself, while the inverse is \(\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1)\). In general the adjoint is characterized by \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\): it moves a matrix from one side of an inner product to the other.

Problems

  1. Compute \(\braket\phi\psi\) for \(\ket\phi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket\psi=(\ket0-i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Are the two states orthogonal?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket\phi\psi=\tfrac12\bigl(1\cdot1+(i)^*(-i)\bigr)=\tfrac12\bigl(1+(-i)(-i)\bigr)=\tfrac12(1-1)=0\). They are orthogonal. Without the conjugate, the sum would be \(\tfrac12(1+1)=1\), wrongly suggesting the two states are equal.

  2. Find the adjoints of \(\operatorname{diag}(1,i)\) and of \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). Which of the two matrices is unitary?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\operatorname{diag}(1,i)^\dagger=\operatorname{diag}(1,-i)\), and their product is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,(-i)i)=I\), so the first is unitary. For the second, the adjoint is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\), and \(A^\dagger A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&5\end{pmatrix}\neq I\), so it is not.

  3. Using \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\), prove that \((AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger\). Conclude that the product of two unitaries is unitary.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For all \(\ket\phi,\ket\psi\): \(\braket\phi{AB\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}{B\psi}=\braket{B^\dagger A^\dagger\phi}\psi\). This is the defining property of \((AB)^\dagger\), so \((AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger\). For unitaries \(U,V\): \((UV)^\dagger UV=V^\dagger U^\dagger UV=V^\dagger V=I\).

4.2

Postulates

We now collect the rules into a model for finite quantum systems. States describe preparations, unitaries describe closed-system evolution, and measurements connect the calculation to outcomes. The new ingredient is the state left after a measurement. We will also distinguish repeating a measurement from preparing a fresh state for each run.

4.2.1 State and evolution postulates§

postulate

The experiments in Chapter 3 motivated a mathematical description. We now state its rules explicitly for finite-dimensional systems. They are physical postulates, tested through their predictions, rather than deductions from classical probability.

Pure states. A pure state is a unit vector in a complex inner-product space. Vectors differing only by a global phase represent the same physical state.

Closed-system evolution. Evolution is unitary: \(\ket\psi\mapsto U\ket\psi\), with \(U^\dagger U=I\).

For a qubit, remove a global phase so its first coordinate is real and nonnegative. Then

\[\ket\psi=\cos\frac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\frac\theta2\ket1,\qquad0\leq\theta\leq\pi.\]

One angle fixes the two magnitudes; the other fixes their relative phase. At a pole, where one component vanishes, that relative phase has no effect.

A pure-state vector is not the description of every preparation or every subsystem. Classical uncertainty about preparation and entanglement with another system call for density matrices, introduced when needed and developed in Appendix D. We begin with pure states so the calculations remain visible.

Problems

  1. Normalize \(\ket0+2i\ket1\), and give the probabilities of a computational-basis measurement.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The squared norm is \(1+|2i|^2=5\), so the state is \((\ket0+2i\ket1)/\sqrt5\). The probabilities are \(1/5\) and \(4/5\).

  2. Write the state \((i\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\) in the form \(\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1\), after removing a global phase.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Multiply by the global phase \(-i\): \(-i(i\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Now the first coordinate is real and positive, \(\cos\tfrac\theta2=1/\sqrt2\), so \(\theta=\pi/2\), and \(e^{i\phi}=i\), so \(\phi=\pi/2\).

  3. Show that \(\ket\psi\) and \(e^{i\gamma}\ket\psi\) give the same probabilities for every sequence of operations followed by a computational-basis measurement. Then show that \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \((\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\) do not differ by a global phase.

    Show solutionHide solution

    A sequence of operations is a matrix \(V\), and \(V(e^{i\gamma}\ket\psi)=e^{i\gamma}V\ket\psi\) by linearity. Every coordinate is multiplied by \(e^{i\gamma}\), which has magnitude \(1\), so every probability is unchanged. If \(\ket0-\ket1=e^{i\gamma}(\ket0+\ket1)\), comparing coordinates would give \(e^{i\gamma}=1\) and \(e^{i\gamma}=-1\) at once, which is impossible.

4.2.2 The measurement postulate§

measurement postulate

The measurement rule gives the probability of each outcome. What is the state after the measurement?

Perform an ideal computational-basis measurement on a qubit and obtain \(0\), then measure it again immediately. Experiments find \(0\) again, every time. Which state gives \(0\) with certainty? Only \(\ket0\), up to a global phase. So after the outcome \(0\), the qubit is in the state \(\ket0\).

Measurement. Measuring the state \(\sum_j\alpha_j\ket j\) in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\alpha_j|^2\), and leaves the system in the state \(\ket j\).

For \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\), the outcome is \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and \(1\) with probability \(1/4\); after the outcome \(1\), the state is \(\ket1\). A single run gives a single outcome; the probabilities describe how often each outcome occurs.

This also explains why measuring between two applications of \(H\) destroys their return to the start: after the measurement the qubit is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and \(H\) applied to either gives each outcome with probability \(1/2\).

Is a measurement a closed-system unitary evolution? No. When the outcome is \(0\), it sends both \(\ket0\) and \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) to \(\ket0\). Different states merge, so a measurement cannot be undone and is not unitary.

Problems

  1. Measure \((\ket0+2i\ket1)/\sqrt5\) in the computational basis. Give the probability of each outcome and the state after it.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Outcome \(0\) has probability \(1/5\) and leaves \(\ket0\); outcome \(1\) has probability \(4/5\) and leaves \(\ket1\).

  2. A qubit in \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\) is measured twice in a row. Find the probabilities of the four sequences of outcomes \(00\), \(01\), \(10\), and \(11\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The first measurement gives \(0\) with probability \(3/4\) and leaves \(\ket0\), after which the second gives \(0\) for certain. Likewise for \(1\). So \(P(00)=3/4\), \(P(11)=1/4\), and \(P(01)=P(10)=0\).

  3. Start from \(\ket0\). Apply \(H\), measure, apply \(H\), and measure again. Find the probabilities of the four pairs of outcomes, and compare with applying \(H\) twice and measuring once.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(H\ket0=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), so the first measurement gives \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\) each and leaves \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\). Applying \(H\) to either gives an equal superposition, so the second measurement is again \(0\) or \(1\) with probability \(1/2\). All four pairs have probability \(1/4\). Without the middle measurement, \(HH\ket0=\ket0\), and the outcome is \(0\) with certainty. The measurement leaves a definite configuration, and the two paths can no longer interfere.

4.2.3 Repeated measurements on independently prepared states§

One measurement produces one outcome. To test a predicted probability, prepare the state afresh on each run and measure every copy in the same way.

If the outcome probability is \(p\), its observed fraction over \(N\) independent runs has standard deviation

\[\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}N}.\]

For \(p=1/2\), 100 runs give a standard deviation of 0.05; 10,000 give 0.005. Reducing this typical fluctuation tenfold costs a hundredfold increase in samples. This is an uncertainty scale, not a guarantee that every experiment lands within it.

Measuring one qubit repeatedly is a different experiment. Write \(\ket+=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Once a computational-basis measurement of \(\ket+\) gives zero, an immediate repeat gives zero again, assuming no intervening change. A freshly prepared \(\ket+\) is still equally likely to give either outcome. Always specify which preparation is being repeated.

Problems

  1. A preparation gives outcome \(1\) with probability \(0.3\). In \(100\) independent runs, what is the expected number of \(1\)s, and its standard deviation \(\sqrt{Np(1-p)}\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The expected count is \(Np=30\), and the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{100\cdot0.3\cdot0.7}=\sqrt{21}\approx4.6\). Counts between about \(25\) and \(35\) are typical.

  2. For \(p=1/2\), how many independent runs are needed for the standard deviation of the observed fraction, \(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\), to be at most \(0.01\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    We need \(\sqrt{0.25/N}\leq0.01\), that is, \(0.25/N\leq10^{-4}\), so \(N\geq2500\).

  3. Someone measures a single qubit, prepared in \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), a hundred times in a row, obtains \(0\) every time, and concludes that the state was \(\ket0\). What do the data actually show? Design an experiment that distinguishes \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) from \(\ket0\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The first measurement gave \(0\), which has probability \(1/2\) for this state, and left the qubit in \(\ket0\); the other ninety-nine measurements simply repeated it. The data are equally consistent with \(\ket0\). To distinguish the two, prepare fresh copies and measure each once: about half the results are \(1\) for \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\), and none for \(\ket0\). Better still, apply \(H\) to each fresh copy before measuring: \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) then always gives \(0\), while \(\ket0\) gives \(1\) half the time.

4.3

Measurement in Other Bases

The measurement postulate refers to one basis, the configurations of the system. A qubit has many other orthonormal bases, and every state can be written in any of them. A unitary applied before a measurement measures the original state in another basis, so choosing the unitary means choosing the question. Can a well-chosen unitary also make two states easier to tell apart? No: unitaries preserve inner products, and with them every overlap between states.

4.3.1 Changes of basis§

\(\pm\) basis

Is the computational basis special? Consider

\[\ket+=\frac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2},\qquad\ket-=\frac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}.\]

Both are unit vectors, and \(\braket+-=\tfrac12(1-1)=0\), so they form another orthonormal basis.

Every state can be written in this basis, and its coordinates are inner products: the coefficient of \(\ket+\) in \(\ket\psi\) is \(\braket+\psi\). For example, \(\braket+0=\braket-0=1/\sqrt2\), so

\[\ket0=\frac{\ket++\ket-}{\sqrt2}.\]

Has anything physical changed? No. Writing \(\ket0\) in the \(\pm\) basis changes its coordinates, not the state. Applying a gate does change the state. The two can look alike on paper, so it matters to say which one is meant.

The same holds for any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\): \(\ket\psi=\sum_j\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}\).

Problems

  1. Write \(\ket1\) in the \(\pm\) basis.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket+1=1/\sqrt2\) and \(\braket-1=-1/\sqrt2\), so \(\ket1=(\ket+-\ket-)/\sqrt2\).

  2. Write \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\) in the \(\pm\) basis.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket+\psi=(\sqrt3/2+1/2)/\sqrt2=(\sqrt3+1)/(2\sqrt2)\) and \(\braket-\psi=(\sqrt3-1)/(2\sqrt2)\). So \(\ket\psi=\frac{\sqrt3+1}{2\sqrt2}\ket++\frac{\sqrt3-1}{2\sqrt2}\ket-\). The squared coefficients, \((2+\sqrt3)/4\) and \((2-\sqrt3)/4\), add to \(1\).

  3. Let \(\ket{\pm i}=(\ket0\pm i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Show that these two vectors form an orthonormal basis, and write \(\ket+\) in it.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Each has squared norm \(\tfrac12(1+|i|^2)=1\), and \(\braket{+i}{-i}=\tfrac12\bigl(1+(i)^*(-i)\bigr)=\tfrac12(1-1)=0\). The coordinates of \(\ket+\) are \(\braket{+i}+=\tfrac12(1-i)\) and \(\braket{-i}+=\tfrac12(1+i)\), so \(\ket+=\frac{1-i}2\ket{+i}+\frac{1+i}2\ket{-i}\). The squared magnitudes, \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac12\), add to \(1\).

4.3.2 Measurement in an orthonormal basis§

measurement in a basis · projector · projective measurement

Apply \(H\) to a qubit and then measure it. Which property of the original state does the outcome reveal?

\(H\) sends \(\ket+\) to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket-\) to \(\ket1\). Write the original state as \(a\ket++b\ket-\); after \(H\) it is \(a\ket0+b\ket1\). The measurement gives \(0\) with probability \(|a|^2=|\braket+\psi|^2\) and \(1\) with probability \(|\braket-\psi|^2\). “Apply \(H\), then measure” therefore measures the state in the \(\pm\) basis.

Does this work for any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\)? Let \(U\) be the unitary whose columns are the \(\ket{u_j}\), so that \(U\ket j=\ket{u_j}\). Its inverse \(U^\dagger\) sends \(\ket{u_j}\) to \(\ket j\). Applying \(U^\dagger\) and then measuring gives outcome \(j\) with probability

\[|\braket{j}{U^\dagger\psi}|^2=|\braket{U j}\psi|^2=|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2,\]

and applying \(U\) afterward turns the resulting state \(\ket j\) into \(\ket{u_j}\).

Measurement in a basis. Measuring \(\ket\psi\) in the orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{u_j}\}\) gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\), and leaves the state \(\ket{u_j}\).

Geometrically, \(\braket{u_j}\psi\,\ket{u_j}\) is the projection of \(\ket\psi\) onto the direction \(\ket{u_j}\). With the projector \(P_j=\ket{u_j}\bra{u_j}\), the probability is \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\). More generally, a projective measurement is given by projectors \(P_j\) onto orthogonal subspaces with \(\sum_jP_j=I\): outcome \(j\) has probability \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\) and leaves the state \(P_j\ket\psi/\|P_j\ket\psi\|\). When a subspace has more than one dimension, the outcome identifies the subspace, not a single vector.

Problems

  1. Measure \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\) in the \(\pm\) basis. Give the probability of each outcome.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The probabilities are \(|\braket+\psi|^2=(\sqrt3+1)^2/8=(2+\sqrt3)/4\approx0.93\) and \(|\braket-\psi|^2=(2-\sqrt3)/4\approx0.07\). Measured in the computational basis, the same state gives \(3/4\) and \(1/4\).

  2. Let \(\ket{\pm i}=(\ket0\pm i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Which operations, applied before a computational-basis measurement, measure a qubit in the basis \(\{\ket{+i},\ket{-i}\}\)? Check your answer on \(\ket{+i}\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Apply \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-i)\) and then \(H\). The first operation sends \(\ket{+i}=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\) to \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2=\ket+\) and \(\ket{-i}\) to \(\ket-\); then \(H\) sends these to \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\). On \(\ket{+i}\), the outcome is \(0\) with certainty.

  3. Let \(U\) be the unitary with columns \(\ket{u_j}\). Show that applying \(U^\dagger\) and then measuring in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The coordinate \(j\) of \(U^\dagger\ket\psi\) is \(\braket{j}{U^\dagger\psi}=\braket{Uj}\psi=\braket{u_j}\psi\), using \(\braket\phi{A\psi}=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\) and \(U\ket j=\ket{u_j}\). A computational-basis measurement of \(U^\dagger\ket\psi\) gives \(j\) with probability \(|\braket{u_j}\psi|^2\).

4.3.3 Overlaps under unitary evolution§

A unitary preserves more than total probability. It preserves every pairwise overlap:

\[\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\braket\phi{U^\dagger U\psi}=\braket\phi\psi.\]

It can rotate two states together, but cannot turn a nonorthogonal pair into an orthogonal pair. That will limit both discrimination and copying. A useful calculation has revealed a constraint on every possible unitary device.

Could we use any matrix and normalize its output afterwards? Generally not. The normalization factor would depend on the input, so the resulting map would no longer be linear. A conditional measurement update does involve normalization, but it is accompanied by a probabilistic outcome. It is not a deterministic gate disguised as a formula.

Unitary evolution describes the closed system. Measurement, discarded records, and environmental noise require a broader account. Keeping the scope clear prevents us from attributing all physical processes to one kind of map.

Problems

  1. Check that \(H\) preserves the overlap of \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\). After \(H\), the states are \(H\ket0=\ket+\) and \(H\ket+=\ket0\), with overlap \(\braket+0=1/\sqrt2\). The overlap is unchanged.

  2. The matrix \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) is not unitary. Find two states whose overlap it changes.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(A\ket0=\ket0\) and \(A\ket1=\ket0+\ket1\). The overlap \(\braket01=0\) becomes \(\braket0{0+1}=1\). Two orthogonal, perfectly distinguishable states would become indistinguishable in part, so \(A\) cannot be an operation.

  3. Prove that a matrix that preserves the norm of every vector preserves every inner product. Expand \(\|\phi+\psi\|^2\) and \(\|\phi+i\psi\|^2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\|\phi+\psi\|^2=\|\phi\|^2+\|\psi\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\braket\phi\psi\), and \(\|\phi+i\psi\|^2=\|\phi\|^2+\|\psi\|^2+2\operatorname{Re}\bigl(i\braket\phi\psi\bigr)=\|\phi\|^2+\|\psi\|^2-2\operatorname{Im}\braket\phi\psi\). If \(U\) preserves every norm, it preserves the left-hand sides and \(\|\phi\|,\|\psi\|\), hence the real and imaginary parts of \(\braket\phi\psi\). So \(\braket{U\phi}{U\psi}=\braket\phi\psi\).

4.4

Observables and Distinguishability

So far a measurement has returned a label. A physical measurement usually reports a number, such as an energy, and we want an operator to describe the numbers just as projections describe the outcomes. The states that an operator only rescales, its eigenvectors, make the connection: an observable is an operator whose eigenspaces are the outcomes and whose eigenvalues are the numbers reported. We then return to the limit set by overlaps and ask how well any measurement can tell apart two states that are not orthogonal.

4.4.1 Eigenvectors and eigenspaces§

eigenvector · eigenvalue · eigenspace

To attach numbers to measurement outcomes, we need the states that an operator only rescales. \(Z\) leaves \(\ket0\) alone and multiplies \(\ket1\) by \(-1\). A vector \(\ket v\neq0\) with \(A\ket v=\lambda\ket v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\): \(A\) keeps its direction and multiplies it by \(\lambda\).

For \(Z\), the vectors \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) are eigenvectors with eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\). For \(X\), which exchanges \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\), we find \(X\ket+=\ket+\) and \(X\ket-=-\ket-\), with the same eigenvalues.

What can the eigenvalues of a unitary be? A unitary preserves length, so \(|\lambda|=1\). An eigenvector of a unitary only gains a phase.

If several independent eigenvectors share an eigenvalue, every combination of them is again an eigenvector with that eigenvalue. Together they form an eigenspace.

The outcome subspaces of a projective measurement will be the eigenspaces of the operator we are looking for.

Problems

  1. Show that \(\ket{+i}=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket{-i}=(\ket0-i\ket1)/\sqrt2\) are eigenvectors of \(Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\), and find their eigenvalues.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(Y(1,i)^T=(-i\cdot i,\;i)^T=(1,i)^T\), so \(\ket{+i}\) has eigenvalue \(+1\). \(Y(1,-i)^T=(-i\cdot(-i),\;i)^T=(-1,i)^T=-(1,-i)^T\), so \(\ket{-i}\) has eigenvalue \(-1\).

  2. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(H\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(H^2=I\), so any eigenvalue satisfies \(\lambda^2=1\): \(\lambda=\pm1\). For \(\lambda=1\), \((a+b)/\sqrt2=a\) gives \(b=(\sqrt2-1)a\), so the unit eigenvector is \(\cos\tfrac\pi8\ket0+\sin\tfrac\pi8\ket1\), since \(\tan\tfrac\pi8=\sqrt2-1\). For \(\lambda=-1\), the eigenvector is the orthogonal one, \(-\sin\tfrac\pi8\ket0+\cos\tfrac\pi8\ket1\).

  3. Prove that every eigenvalue of a unitary has magnitude \(1\), and that eigenvectors of a unitary with different eigenvalues are orthogonal.

    Show solutionHide solution

    If \(U\ket v=\lambda\ket v\) with \(\ket v\neq0\), then \(\|v\|=\|Uv\|=|\lambda|\,\|v\|\), so \(|\lambda|=1\). If also \(U\ket w=\mu\ket w\), then \(\braket vw=\braket{Uv}{Uw}=\lambda^*\mu\braket vw\). Since \(|\lambda|=1\), \(\lambda^*=1/\lambda\), so \(\lambda^*\mu=1\) only when \(\mu=\lambda\). For \(\mu\neq\lambda\), therefore, \(\braket vw=0\).

4.4.2 Hermitian operators and spectral projections§

observable · Hermitian · spectral projectors

A measurement distinguishes outcomes; an observable also assigns them numbers. For orthogonal outcome projectors \(P_j\), choose real labels \(a_j\) and form

\[A=\sum_j a_jP_j.\]

This operator is Hermitian: \(A^\dagger=A\). Conversely, a Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenspaces. Its spectral projectors define a measurement, with probability \(\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2\) for the reported eigenvalue \(a_j\).

For \(A=\operatorname{diag}(2,5)\), we measure in the computational basis and report 2 or 5. Changing those labels changes the average reported number without changing which basis directions the apparatus distinguishes.

There is one qualification: if distinct projectors receive the same number, the operator groups their subspaces into one eigenspace. Recording only that number loses the finer outcome distinction. The standard spectral measurement of \(A\) refers to these grouped eigenspaces.

Problems

  1. Is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}\) Hermitian? Find its eigenvalues.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Its conjugate transpose is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&(-i)^*\\i^*&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}\), the same matrix, so it is Hermitian. The eigenvalues satisfy \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2=2\) (the trace) and \(\lambda_1\lambda_2=1-(i)(-i)=0\) (the determinant), so they are \(0\) and \(2\): real, as for every Hermitian matrix.

  2. The observable \(Z\) reports \(+1\) for \(\ket0\) and \(-1\) for \(\ket1\). Find its average value, \(\sum_ja_jp_j\), on \((\sqrt3/2)\ket0+(1/2)\ket1\) and on \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    For the first state, \((+1)\tfrac34+(-1)\tfrac14=\tfrac12\). For the second, \((+1)\tfrac12+(-1)\tfrac12=0\).

  3. An observable on three basis states has value \(0\) on \(\ket0,\ket1\) and value \(1\) on \(\ket2\). For \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+\ket1+\ket2)/\sqrt3\), find the probabilities and the state after obtaining \(0\). Why would measuring the full basis and merely hiding the distinction between \(0\) and \(1\) be a different operation?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The projectors are \(P_0=\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1\) and \(P_1=\ket2\bra2\). Their probabilities are \(2/3\) and \(1/3\). The ideal projective measurement leaves \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) after outcome \(0\): coherence within that eigenspace survives. A full basis measurement instead leaves either \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\). Forgetting which does not restore coherence. For example, a later test for \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) succeeds with probability \(1\) in the first case and \(1/2\) in the second.

4.4.3 Nonorthogonal state discrimination§

Someone hands us one qubit, prepared either in \(\ket0\) or in \(\ket+\). Can we always tell which?

Suppose a measurement could. Some outcome would then occur with certainty for \(\ket0\) and never for \(\ket+\). By the measurement postulate, \(\ket0\) would lie in that outcome’s subspace and \(\ket+\) would be orthogonal to it, so \(\braket0+=0\). But \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\). No measurement identifies the two states with certainty.

Could a cleverer procedure help, such as an operation before measuring, or an interaction that writes a record into another system? Any such operation is unitary and preserves the overlap \(1/\sqrt2\), so the records cannot become orthogonal either.

The limit is structural, not a weakness of detectors. Two pure states can be distinguished perfectly exactly when they are orthogonal. A measurement can trade wrong answers for answers that report “inconclusive,” but it cannot always be right.

Problems

  1. Which of these pairs can be distinguished perfectly by one measurement: \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\); \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\); \(\{\ket0,\ket+\}\); \(\{\ket+,\ket{+i}\}\), where \(\ket{+i}=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Only the orthogonal pairs: \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\) and \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\). For the others, \(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\) and \(\braket+{+i}=(1+i)/2\), whose magnitude is \(1/\sqrt2\neq0\).

  2. A qubit is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket+\), each with probability \(1/2\). We measure in the computational basis and guess \(\ket+\) when the outcome is \(1\) and \(\ket0\) when it is \(0\). Find the probability of guessing correctly.

    Show solutionHide solution

    If the state is \(\ket0\), the outcome is always \(0\), and the guess is right. If it is \(\ket+\), the outcome is \(1\) with probability \(1/2\), and the guess is right only then. The success probability is \(\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac34\). A measurement in a basis tilted halfway between the two states does better, reaching \(\tfrac12\bigl(1+1/\sqrt2\bigr)\approx0.85\), but no measurement reaches \(1\).

  3. Prove that two pure states can be distinguished perfectly by a measurement exactly when they are orthogonal: give a measurement when they are, and show that none exists when they are not.

    Show solutionHide solution

    If \(\braket\phi\psi=0\), extend \(\ket\phi,\ket\psi\) to an orthonormal basis and measure in it: \(\ket\phi\) always gives the outcome \(\phi\), and \(\ket\psi\) always gives \(\psi\). Conversely, suppose a projective measurement has an outcome with projector \(P\) such that \(\ket\phi\) always gives it and \(\ket\psi\) never does. Then \(\|P\phi\|=1\), so \(P\ket\phi=\ket\phi\), and \(P\ket\psi=0\). Hence \(\braket\phi\psi=\braket{P\phi}\psi=\braket\phi{P\psi}=0\), using \(P^\dagger=P\).

Check your understanding

Does an adjoint always undo an operation?

Compare your explanation

No. It transfers an operation across an inner product. It equals the inverse for unitary operators, but not for a general stretch or projection.

1. Quick check §

Is \(Z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\) Hermitian, unitary, both, or neither? Answer the same for \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\).

Hint 1

Hermitian means \(A^\dagger=A\); unitary means \(A^\dagger A=I\).

Hint 2

Compute the conjugate transpose of each.

Worked solution

\(Z\) is both: \(Z^\dagger=Z\) and \(Z^\dagger Z=Z^2=I\). The second matrix is neither: its adjoint \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\) differs from it, and \(A^\dagger A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\neq I\).

Normalize \(3\ket0+4i\ket1\). Find the probabilities of a measurement in the computational basis and in the \(\pm\) basis.

The normalized state is \(\tfrac35\ket0+\tfrac{4i}5\ket1\). 3. Extension §

Prove that no unitary maps \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) to two orthogonal states. What does this imply for any procedure that tries to identify which of the two was prepared?

Hint 1

A unitary preserves inner products.

Hint 2

\(\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

\(\braket{U0}{U+}=\braket0+=1/\sqrt2\neq0\), so the outputs cannot be orthogonal. Any procedure built from operations followed by a measurement therefore cannot always identify the state: perfect identification would require orthogonal outputs.
