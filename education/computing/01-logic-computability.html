Chapter 01

Logic and Computability

Quantum computing proposes a new model of computation. When the idea of computation was being developed, it was necessary to define it purely as a sequence of logical operations. As computation is done by a physical device, this process has to be physical. Since quantum mechanics describes all physical processes, computation becomes quantum mechanical. How, then, does this differ from our usual notion of computing devices? To understand this, this chapter builds the necessary ideas leading up to a quantum model of computation.

We first define what computation is and describe the various models of computation and their limits. We then introduce reversible computation, which paves the way for quantum computation.

Can mathematical reasoning be given foundations whose reliability can itself be established?

The notion of computability finds its origin in establishing the reliability of mathematical reasoning. The purpose of mathematics is to provide objective grounds for establishing truth through the following scheme: choose your domain and fix your axioms and rules of inference. Now, everything related to the domain must be settled by starting with the axioms and following the rules.

For example, fix your domain to be the set of integers. Your axioms are all the axioms about integers (known as Peano's axioms of arithmetic). Let's make the statement, "The sum of two even integers is even." Starting with \(2a\) and \(2b\), the axioms allow us to write \(2a + 2b = 2(a + b)\), which shows that the sum is even. So, we are able to settle this question. Can we settle all statements that one can frame about integers?

1.1

Formal Reasoning and Decision Problems

To answer this, we must first say exactly what a proof is. Making the rules explicit then raises two questions: whether the rules themselves can be trusted, and whether following them could be left to a mechanical procedure. The second turns the question we started with into a question about procedures.

1.1.1 Axioms, inference rules, and proofs§

A proof separates two jobs: choosing assumptions and drawing consequences from them. The assumptions are axioms; the permitted steps are rules of inference. From \(P\) and “\(P\) implies \(Q\),” for example, we may infer \(Q\).

In the even-integer argument from the introduction, a reader can check each step without discovering the argument again. Make the notation and the permitted steps precise enough, and a machine can do that checking too.

This is the connection to computation: a proof becomes a finite object that a procedure can inspect. Checking establishes that the conclusion follows from the chosen axioms. Whether those axioms capture what we intended is a separate question.

Problems

  1. From the axioms \(P\), “\(P\) implies \(Q\),” and “\(Q\) implies \(R\),” with modus ponens as the only rule of inference, write a proof of \(R\). Give the justification of each line.

    Show solutionHide solution

    1. \(P\) (axiom). 2. \(P\) implies \(Q\) (axiom). 3. \(Q\) (modus ponens, lines 1 and 2). 4. \(Q\) implies \(R\) (axiom). 5. \(R\) (modus ponens, lines 3 and 4). Each line is either an axiom or follows from earlier lines by the rule, so a checker can verify the proof line by line.

  2. Prove that the sum of an even integer and an odd integer is odd, giving the justification for each step.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Let the even integer be \(2a\) and the odd integer \(2b+1\), with \(a\) and \(b\) integers (definitions of even and odd). Then \(2a+(2b+1)=2(a+b)+1\) (associativity and the distributive law). Since \(a+b\) is an integer, \(2(a+b)+1\) has the form of an odd integer (definition of odd). Hence the sum is odd.

  3. A checker accepts every line of a proof. Show by an example that the conclusion can still be false, and say exactly what the accepted proof establishes.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Take the axiom “every integer is even.” Applying it to \(3\) gives the line “\(3\) is even,” which the checker accepts as an instance of the axiom. The conclusion is false because the axiom is false. An accepted proof establishes only that the conclusion follows from the axioms by the rules. Whether the axioms are true, or at least consistent, is a separate question that the checker does not settle.

1.1.2 Hilbert’s program and consistency§

Writing rules explicitly lets us check whether an argument follows them. But are the rules themselves reliable? Could they allow us to prove both a statement and its opposite? A system that never does this is called consistent.

Hilbert wanted to put mathematics on a secure foundation. First, its assumptions and proof rules would be made explicit. Then one would show that those rules could never produce a contradiction.

He hoped to give this justification using reasoning about finite objects and concrete operations on them. These were called finitary methods. Even when a mathematical statement concerns infinitely many numbers, its written proof is finite. So, proofs and the rules for constructing them could themselves be studied.

This was a question about the reliability of mathematical reasoning. A related question concerns its automation: once the rules are explicit, can a procedure always determine whether a statement has a proof? To see the difficulty, we must distinguish checking a proof from searching for one.

Problems

  1. A system has the axioms \(P\) and “\(P\) implies not \(P\),” and the rule modus ponens. Is it consistent?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. From \(P\) and “\(P\) implies not \(P\),” modus ponens gives “not \(P\).” The system proves both \(P\) and its negation.

  2. Suppose a system also has the rule “from \(A\) and not \(A\), infer any statement \(B\).” Show that if the system is inconsistent, it proves every statement.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Inconsistency means that the system proves some \(A\) and also not \(A\). Applying the rule to these two proofs gives a proof of any statement \(B\) we choose. An inconsistent system of this kind proves everything, so its theorems carry no information.

  3. Hilbert hoped to prove that systems are consistent. Show that a consistency proof carried out inside the same system \(S\) could not settle the doubt, by showing that an inconsistent \(S\) of the kind in the previous problem proves the statement “\(S\) is consistent.”

    Show solutionHide solution

    If \(S\) is inconsistent, it proves every statement it can express, including the statement “\(S\) is consistent.” So a proof of that statement within \(S\) is exactly what an inconsistent \(S\) would also produce, and it cannot by itself show that \(S\) is consistent. This is why Hilbert sought a consistency proof using only weaker, finitary reasoning that is easier to trust than the system being justified.

1.1.3 Proof checking, proof search, and decision§

Suppose a proposed proof fails a check. We have rejected that argument, not its conclusion. Another argument may succeed.

Now suppose no proof is supplied. If candidate proofs use a fixed finite alphabet and can be checked mechanically, list strings by length and test them. Every finite proof eventually appears. If a proof exists, this search finds one.

But if no proof exists, there is no final candidate whose rejection settles the matter. The search simply continues. A decision procedure must do more: finish with a correct yes or no on every input.

Could a cleverer procedure succeed? Failure of this search strategy does not rule out all strategies. To ask that larger question, we need a precise account of what a mechanical procedure is. That is why models of computation enter the story.

Problems

  1. Strings over the alphabet \(\{a,b\}\) are listed by length, starting with the empty string, and alphabetically within each length. Write the first seven strings, and give the position of \(ab\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The list begins: the empty string, \(a\), \(b\), \(aa\), \(ab\), \(ba\), \(bb\). The string \(ab\) is fifth.

  2. A proof search lists candidate strings over a two-symbol alphabet in this order and checks each one. If the shortest proof of a statement has length \(5\), at most how many candidates are checked before a proof is found? Why can such a bound not be computed in advance for an arbitrary statement?

    Show solutionHide solution

    There are \(2^k\) strings of length \(k\), so the strings of length at most \(5\) number \(1+2+4+8+16+32=63\). The search finds a proof among the first \(63\) candidates. The bound needs the length of the shortest proof, which is unknown in advance, and if no proof exists the search never stops, so no bound applies at all.

  3. Suppose a theory is consistent, its axioms can be listed by an algorithm, and it is complete: for every sentence \(P\), it proves \(P\) or it proves not \(P\). Show that searching for a proof of \(P\) and a proof of not \(P\) in alternation is a decision procedure for provability.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Check candidates in turn, alternating between proofs of \(P\) and proofs of not \(P\). By completeness, one of them has a proof, so the search stops after finitely many steps. If it finds a proof of \(P\), then \(P\) is provable. If it finds a proof of not \(P\), then \(P\) is not provable, since by consistency the theory cannot prove both. The procedure always stops with the correct answer. The argument needs completeness, which fails for arithmetic by the incompleteness theorem.

Why introduce computation here?

Why does the possibility of checking each proposed proof not settle the decision problem?

Consider the argument

We can check a supplied proof in finitely many steps. If no proof is supplied, searching for one may continue forever. A decision procedure must also finish when there is no proof. So, a proof checker alone does not provide a decision procedure.

1.2

Models of Computation

Whether such a procedure exists can be decided only once we say exactly what a procedure is. We start from an algorithm we already trust, Euclid’s, and describe its run as a sequence of transitions between configurations. That description is exact only once the elementary steps are fixed, and fixing them leads to the Turing machine: a model simple enough to analyse, yet general enough that one machine can simulate every other.

1.2.1 Algorithms and termination§

Take a task we can finish by hand: find the greatest common divisor of \(48\) and \(18\). Euclid’s algorithm repeatedly replaces a pair by its second entry and the remainder on division:

\[(48,18)\longrightarrow(18,12)\longrightarrow(12,6)\longrightarrow(6,0).\]

It returns \(6\). The example suggests a rule, but two questions remain: why is the answer right, and why must the procedure stop?

Write \(a=qb+r\), with \(0\leq rinvariant: it stays fixed while the pair changes.

Meanwhile the nonnegative second entry strictly decreases. It cannot do that forever. Eventually the pair is \((g,0)\), whose greatest common divisor is \(g\), so we return it.

These are separate arguments. Repeating the original pair forever would preserve the correct answer but never deliver it. An algorithm needs both a reason to trust its result and a reason to expect that result.

Problems

  1. Run Euclid’s algorithm on \((91,35)\), writing each division and each pair.

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(91=2\cdot35+21\), \(35=1\cdot21+14\), \(21=1\cdot14+7\), and \(14=2\cdot7+0\). The pairs are \((91,35)\to(35,21)\to(21,14)\to(14,7)\to(7,0)\), so the greatest common divisor is \(7\).

  2. Run Euclid’s algorithm on \((21,13)\). How many divisions does it take, and why does this pair make the second entry decrease slowly?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(21=1\cdot13+8\), \(13=1\cdot8+5\), \(8=1\cdot5+3\), \(5=1\cdot3+2\), \(3=1\cdot2+1\), \(2=2\cdot1+0\): six divisions, and the greatest common divisor is \(1\). Every quotient but the last is \(1\), so each remainder is the difference of the two previous numbers, the smallest decrease possible. The entries run backward through the Fibonacci numbers \(21,13,8,5,3,2,1\).

  3. Give a procedure on pairs of positive integers that keeps the greatest common divisor unchanged at every step but never stops, and another that always stops but can return a wrong answer. What does this show about the two arguments for Euclid’s algorithm?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Replacing \((a,b)\) by \((b,a)\) keeps the greatest common divisor unchanged but never reduces anything, so it runs forever. Replacing \((a,b)\) by \((b,0)\) and returning \(b\) always stops, but from \((4,6)\) it returns \(6\), while the greatest common divisor is \(2\). Preserving the answer does not imply termination, and termination does not imply a correct answer. Euclid’s algorithm needs both arguments: the invariant and the decreasing second entry.

1.2.2 Configurations and transitions§

Suppose we pause Euclid’s algorithm at the pair \((18,12)\), just before the next division. What must we record in order to continue? We need the pair and the instruction that comes next. We do not need the earlier pairs.

The information needed to resume a computation is called its configuration. What must we include? That depends on where we allow the computation to pause.

At the start of Euclid’s repeated step, we need only the pair: the next instruction is already fixed. Halfway through a division, the pair alone would not be enough. We would also need the temporary values and a record of how far the division had progressed.

A transition is one step from a configuration to the next. Starting with a particular input, we obtain a sequence of configurations. This sequence is called a run. The algorithm supplies the transition rules and tells us when to stop.

Proof search can be described in the same way. We record the candidate being checked, the line currently under examination, and any other information needed by the checker. Each operation updates this configuration.

For a deterministic procedure, the current configuration determines the next step. Does it also determine the previous step? In Euclid’s algorithm, both \((30,18)\) and \((48,18)\) lead to \((18,12)\). So, knowing how to continue does not necessarily tell us how to go backward. We will return to this distinction when we consider reversibility.

Problems

  1. Euclid’s algorithm divides by repeated subtraction. It is paused in the middle of dividing \(48\) by \(18\), after one subtraction has given \(30\). What must the configuration record so that the computation can resume?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It must record the current value \(30\), the divisor \(18\), the number of subtractions made so far, \(1\), and the fact that the computation is in the middle of a division rather than at the start of a new step. The earlier pairs need not be recorded.

  2. Write the run of Euclid’s algorithm from \((30,18)\) as a sequence of configurations. Find two other pairs whose runs reach \((18,12)\) after one step.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The run is \((30,18)\to(18,12)\to(12,6)\to(6,0)\). Any pair \((18q+12,18)\) with \(q\geq0\) gives the remainder \(12\) on division by \(18\): for example \((48,18)\) and \((66,18)\), and also \((12,18)\), for which the quotient is \(0\).

  3. Describe all pairs from which Euclid’s algorithm reaches \((18,12)\) in one step. What does the answer imply about running the algorithm backward?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A step sends \((a,b)\) to \((b,r)\), so we need \(b=18\) and remainder \(r=12\) on dividing \(a\) by \(18\): \(a=18q+12\) for some integer \(q\geq0\). There are infinitely many such pairs, one for each \(q\). From \((18,12)\) alone we cannot recover the previous pair; we would also need the quotient \(q\), which the algorithm discards.

1.2.3 Turing machines and universal computation§

An instruction such as “divide” still hides a computation. A Turing machine gives us elementary steps explicit enough to study what any program can do.

Its tape consists of cells holding symbols from a finite alphabet, including a blank. A head reads one cell. A finite control state records the stage of the instructions. For each state and read symbol, the rule table specifies what to write, which way to move, and the next state. A designated halting state ends the run.

For example, start at the left end of a finite block of ones. Move right on a one; halt on a blank. The same finite rule table handles blocks of every finite length. There is no fixed tape bound, but any finite run visits only finitely many cells. A configuration records the tape, head position, and control state.

The decisive observation is that a rule table is itself finite data. Encode it as a string and another machine can read it and imitate its steps. A universal machine takes a program description together with an input and performs this simulation.

Changing the supplied description changes the program being simulated. A program can now receive another program—or its own description—as input. No infinite object or mysterious self-awareness is involved: it receives a finite string. This will let us turn a proposed predictor of program behavior into a program that defeats that prediction.

Problems

  1. A machine has a running state \(q\) and a halting state \(h\). In \(q\), reading \(1\), it writes \(1\), moves right, and stays in \(q\); reading a blank, it halts. Started on the leftmost \(1\) of a tape holding \(111\), list its configurations until it halts.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Number the cells \(1,2,3,\dots\). The configurations are: tape \(111\), head at cell \(1\), state \(q\); head at cell \(2\), state \(q\); head at cell \(3\), state \(q\); head at cell \(4\), which is blank, state \(q\); then the machine halts in state \(h\) with the head at cell \(4\). The tape never changes.

  2. Change the machine so that, reading a blank in state \(q\), it writes \(1\) and halts. What does the new machine compute on a block of \(n\) ones, and how many steps does it take?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It moves right over the \(n\) ones and writes a \(1\) in the first blank cell, so the block grows to \(n+1\) ones: in unary notation, it computes \(n+1\). It takes \(n\) moves and one final write, \(n+1\) steps in all.

  3. Design a machine that, started on the leftmost symbol of a block of ones, halts in a state marked “even” if the block has an even number of ones and “odd” otherwise. Give its rule table and trace it on \(111\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    Use two running states, \(E\) (an even number of ones read so far) and \(O\) (odd), starting in \(E\). In \(E\) reading \(1\): move right and enter \(O\). In \(O\) reading \(1\): move right and enter \(E\). In \(E\) reading a blank: halt in “even.” In \(O\) reading a blank: halt in “odd.” On \(111\) the states are \(E\to O\to E\to O\), and the blank is read in \(O\), so the machine halts in “odd.”

1.3

Computability and Its Limits

With a precise model, the original question can be asked precisely. First we must ask whether the model is the right one, since a limit of Turing machines matters only if no reasonable procedure escapes it; the Church–Turing thesis gives reasons to accept that none does. We then find a question that no procedure settles, whether a program halts, and carry the same argument back to proofs: no consistent system of axioms strong enough for arithmetic settles every statement about the integers.

1.3.1 The Church–Turing thesis§

We have defined a model using a tape, a head, and a finite rule table. Why use this model to discuss the limits of effective calculation? Could another reasonable model compute functions that Turing machines cannot?

Other mathematical models describe computation in different ways. Lambda calculus uses rules for substituting expressions. Recursive-function models build functions from specified basic functions and construction rules.

Despite these differences, they compute the same functions as Turing machines. A function computable in one model is computable in the others. This equivalence is a mathematical result that can be proved.

The Church–Turing thesis makes a further claim: any calculation that can be carried out by an effective mechanical method can be carried out by a Turing machine.

The phrase “effective mechanical method” is an informal idea. A Turing machine is a precisely defined object. The thesis connects the two. The agreement between different formal models supports it, but does not turn it into a theorem about an already defined class of every possible mechanical method.

This distinction matters when we establish a limitation. We can prove that no Turing machine solves a specified problem. Applying that result to effective procedures in general uses the thesis.

The thesis concerns whether a function can be computed at all. It does not say how much time or memory the computation needs.

This matters for quantum computing. In the standard quantum model, the allowed operations must themselves have effective mathematical descriptions. Under this condition, quantum computers do not compute functions that are uncomputable by Turing machines. They can change the resources needed for some tasks that are already computable. We will return to this difference when studying quantum algorithms.

Problems

  1. A programming language \(L\) can simulate every Turing machine, and a Turing machine can simulate every program of \(L\). Which functions does \(L\) compute? Is this conclusion a theorem or an instance of the Church–Turing thesis?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Exactly the Turing-computable functions. Both simulations are mathematical constructions between two precisely defined models, so the conclusion is a theorem. The thesis is not needed.

  2. Classify each statement as a theorem or as the Church–Turing thesis: (a) lambda calculus and Turing machines compute the same functions; (b) every function computable by an effective mechanical method is Turing-computable; (c) every recursive function is Turing-computable.

    Show solutionHide solution

    (a) and (c) are theorems: they compare precisely defined models. (b) is the thesis: “effective mechanical method” is an informal idea, so the statement cannot be proved as a theorem about defined objects.

  3. Show that some functions from nonnegative integers to \(\{0,1\}\) are computed by no Turing machine. Turing machines can be listed as \(M_0,M_1,M_2,\dots\), since each has a finite description.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Define \(f(n)=1\) if \(M_n\), run on input \(n\), halts with output \(0\), and \(f(n)=0\) otherwise. For every \(n\), \(f(n)\) differs from the output of \(M_n\) on \(n\), or \(M_n\) fails to produce \(0\) or \(1\) there. So \(f\) is computed by no machine in the list, that is, by no Turing machine. By the Church–Turing thesis, no effective mechanical method computes it either.

1.3.2 The halting problem§

Given a program and its input, can we always determine whether the computation will stop? We know that Euclid’s algorithm stops for its permitted inputs. We also know that a program instructed to repeat forever does not stop. Can one procedure settle this question for every program and input?

Suppose such a procedure exists. Call it \(H(P,x)\), where \(P\) is a program description and \(x\) is its input. We require \(H\) itself to finish and answer correctly on every valid pair.

Let us use \(H\) to construct another program, \(D\). Its input \(p\) is a program description. First, \(D\) calls \(H(p,p)\): it asks what happens when the program described by \(p\) receives its own description as input.

Then \(D\) takes the opposite action. If \(H\) answers “halts,” \(D\) enters an endless loop. If \(H\) answers “does not halt,” \(D\) stops. If the input is not a valid program description, we can simply let \(D\) stop.

Now let \(d\) be the description of \(D\), and give \(d\) to \(D\). What should \(H(d,d)\) answer?

If it answers “halts,” the instructions of \(D\) make this run continue forever. If it answers “does not halt,” the instructions make the run stop. In either case, the prediction is wrong.

We assumed that \(H\) was an algorithm that always finished and answered correctly. That assumption allowed us to use it as part of \(D\). But the resulting program contradicts the assumption. Therefore no such general \(H\) exists. This result is called the undecidability of the halting problem.

This does not prevent us from determining the behavior of particular programs. We already proved that Euclid’s algorithm terminates. It also permits an analyzer that sometimes answers “unknown.” What is excluded is one algorithm that always finishes with a correct definite answer for every case.

Problems

  1. Decide, by inspecting each program, whether it halts on the input \(x=5\): (a) while \(x\gt 0\), set \(x:=x-1\); (b) while \(x\neq0\), set \(x:=x-2\); (c) repeat forever: do nothing.

    Show solutionHide solution

    (a) halts: \(x\) decreases to \(0\). (b) does not halt: \(x\) takes the values \(5,3,1,-1,\dots\) and never equals \(0\). (c) does not halt. Particular programs can often be decided by inspection; the halting theorem rules out one procedure that works for every program.

  2. A proposed halting test runs a program for a million steps. It answers “halts” if the program stops and “never halts” otherwise. Give a program on which this test is wrong. Would replacing a million by a larger fixed number repair it?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A program that counts down from \(1{,}000{,}001\), one decrement per step, and then stops will outlast the test. For any fixed cutoff \(B\), a countdown longer than \(B\) gives the same counterexample. A timeout shows only that the program has not halted yet; it does not prove that it never will.

  3. Show that no algorithm decides, for every program \(P\), whether \(P\) halts when given the input \(0\). Use the undecidability of the halting problem: from a pair \((P,x)\), construct a program that ignores its own input.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Given \((P,x)\), let \(P^\prime\) be the program that ignores its input and runs \(P\) on \(x\). Then \(P^\prime\) halts on \(0\) exactly when \(P\) halts on \(x\), and \(P^\prime\) can be written down from \(P\) and \(x\) by an algorithm. If some algorithm decided halting on input \(0\), applying it to \(P^\prime\) would decide whether \(P\) halts on \(x\), for every pair. That would solve the halting problem, which is impossible. So no such algorithm exists.

1.3.3 Undecidability and incompleteness§

There are two distinct limits here. An undecidable problem has no algorithm that always terminates with the correct yes-or-no answer. An incomplete theory leaves some sentence neither provable nor refutable from its axioms. One concerns procedures; the other concerns what follows from fixed assumptions.

The Gödel–Rosser theorem applies to consistent theories whose axioms can be effectively enumerated and which contain enough arithmetic. Peano arithmetic is a familiar example of the required arithmetic strength. Such a theory has a sentence \(P\) for which neither \(P\) nor \(\neg P\) is provable. We state this theorem rather than attempt its proof here.

Adding \(P\) as an axiom settles that sentence. But if the enlarged theory still meets the theorem’s conditions, another sentence remains unsettled. The result is not that every theory is incomplete; its conditions are essential.

Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem addresses Hilbert’s consistency question. Under its arithmetic and proof-representation conditions, a consistent theory cannot prove its own standard formal statement of consistency. A stronger theory may justify a weaker one, but the stronger theory’s assumptions then become part of what we trust.

For this course, retain the distinction: making rules explicit enables mechanical checking without guaranteeing that every mathematical question can be settled. We can now turn to tasks that do admit algorithms and ask what information their implementations preserve.

Problems

  1. A theory has the single axiom \(P\) and the rule modus ponens, and its sentences are built from \(P\) and \(Q\). Is \(Q\) provable? Is not \(Q\) provable? Is the theory complete?

    Show solutionHide solution

    From \(P\) alone, modus ponens yields nothing new, so the theorems are just \(P\). Neither \(Q\) nor not \(Q\) is provable, so \(Q\) is undecided and the theory is incomplete. This toy theory is incomplete for a trivial reason: it says nothing about \(Q\).

  2. A sentence \(G\) is neither provable nor refutable in a consistent theory \(T\). Show that both \(T\) with \(G\) added as an axiom and \(T\) with not \(G\) added are consistent. You may use the fact that if \(T\) with an added axiom \(A\) proves a contradiction, then \(T\) proves not \(A\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    If \(T\) with \(G\) added were inconsistent, \(T\) would prove not \(G\), refuting \(G\), contrary to assumption. If \(T\) with not \(G\) added were inconsistent, \(T\) would prove not not \(G\), that is, \(G\), again contrary to assumption. So both extensions are consistent, and the axioms of \(T\) alone do not decide \(G\).

  3. Suppose an algorithm extends a consistent, effectively axiomatized theory of arithmetic step by step, each time adding a sentence that the current theory leaves undecided. Show that the combined theory, with all the added axioms together, is still incomplete if it is consistent.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The added axioms are produced by an algorithm, so the combined list of axioms can still be listed by an algorithm: list the original axioms and run the extension procedure, interleaving the two. The combined theory is therefore effectively axiomatized, contains arithmetic, and is consistent by assumption. The incompleteness theorem applies to it, so it too leaves some sentence undecided. Adding axioms by any algorithm can never reach completeness.

Computability and efficiency

Does a faster method for solving some problems contradict the undecidability of the halting problem?

Consider the argument

No. Faster algorithms change the resources required for tasks they can perform. The halting theorem excludes any total, always-correct algorithm for the general halting problem, regardless of how much time it is allowed to take.

1.4

From Computability to Reversibility

Undecidability concerns problems that no procedure can solve. For problems that can be solved, a different question arises: what does the procedure do to the information it is given? Can a computation always be run backward, and if not, what is lost, and must it be lost?

1.4.1 Deterministic and reversible transitions§

In a deterministic computation, each configuration has at most one next configuration. A halting configuration may have none. When the computation can continue, the current configuration fixes what happens next.

Does knowing the later configuration also tell us which earlier one produced it? Determinism does not require this.

For example, Euclid’s algorithm includes the transitions

\[(30,18)\longrightarrow(18,12),\qquad(48,18)\longrightarrow(18,12).\]

Knowing the pair \((18,12)\) lets us continue the calculation, but it does not identify which earlier pair occurred. Different configurations have merged into the same one.

To undo a step, we must be able to recover the earlier configuration from the full resulting configuration. This requires different inputs to have different outputs. Such a transition is called one-to-one. Logical reversibility requires this extra condition.

Suppose the transition also reaches every configuration in a fixed state space. It is then a bijection: each configuration has exactly one predecessor. We can define the reverse transformation everywhere on that space.

So, knowing what happens next does not necessarily tell us what happened before. We now ask whether useful computations can preserve enough information to be reversed.

Problems

  1. A rule on the configurations \(A,B,C,D\) sends \(A\to B\), \(B\to C\), \(C\to D\), and \(D\to B\). Is it deterministic? Which configurations have more than one predecessor, and which have none?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It is deterministic: each configuration has exactly one successor. \(B\) has two predecessors, \(A\) and \(D\), and \(A\) has none. The rule is not reversible.

  2. Run the same rule for six steps from each of \(A\), \(B\), \(C\), and \(D\). Which starting configurations end at the same configuration after six steps?

    Show solutionHide solution

    From \(A\): \(A,B,C,D,B,C,D\), ending at \(D\). From \(B\): \(B,C,D,B,C,D,B\), ending at \(B\). From \(C\), the run ends at \(C\), and from \(D\) at \(D\). The runs from \(A\) and from \(D\) both end at \(D\), so the configuration after six steps does not determine where the run started.

  3. Prove that a deterministic, one-to-one rule on finitely many configurations is onto, and that every run of such a rule eventually returns to its starting configuration.

    Show solutionHide solution

    A one-to-one rule sends the \(N\) configurations to \(N\) different configurations, so every configuration is reached: the rule is onto. Now follow a run \(c_0,c_1,c_2,\dots\). With finitely many configurations, some configuration repeats; let \(c_i=c_j\), with \(i\lt j\) and \(i\) as small as possible. If \(i\gt 0\), then \(c_{i-1}\) and \(c_{j-1}\) have the same successor, so by one-to-oneness \(c_{i-1}=c_{j-1}\), a repeat with a smaller first index. Hence \(i=0\): the run returns to \(c_0\).

1.4.2 Information loss in computation§

AND returns zero on three inputs: \(00\), \(01\), and \(10\). If we keep only the answer, we cannot recover which input occurred. But obtaining an answer need not mean throwing away its input. The pair \((x,f(x))\) still contains \(x\), even if \(f\) is many-to-one.

This suggests extra storage, but storage alone is not a construction. We must choose reversible elementary steps, specify the initial values of the extra bits, and clear temporary results without losing information. Chapter 2 supplies those ingredients.

The reason to pursue this is physical as well as logical. Closed-system quantum evolution is described by a unitary transformation, which has an inverse. A quantum circuit must therefore obtain useful answers while respecting reversible evolution between measurements. We have moved from “Can this task be computed?” to “How can its computation preserve the information that quantum evolution requires?”

Problems

  1. Which of these operations on two bits lose information: \((x,y)\mapsto x\land y\); \((x,y)\mapsto(x,x\oplus y)\); \((x,y)\mapsto(y,x)\); \((x,y)\mapsto x\oplus y\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(x\land y\) loses information: \(00\), \(01\), and \(10\) all give \(0\). The XOR \(x\oplus y\) alone also loses it: \(00\) and \(11\) both give \(0\). The maps \((x,x\oplus y)\) and \((y,x)\) are one-to-one: \((x,x\oplus y)\) is undone by applying it again, and the swap by swapping back.

  2. Keep both inputs and store the AND in an extra bit \(t\) that starts at \(0\): \((x,y,t)\mapsto(x,y,t\oplus(x\land y))\). List the outputs for the four inputs with \(t=0\), and show that the map can be undone.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The outputs are \(000\), \(010\), \(100\), and \(111\). Applying the map again gives \(t\oplus(x\land y)\oplus(x\land y)=t\), so every input is restored: the map is its own inverse, even though AND itself loses information.

  3. A computation maps \(n\)-bit inputs to a single output bit. Show that at least \(2^{n-1}\) inputs share an output. If the computation keeps some bits besides its output, how many must it keep, at least, to become one-to-one, and how many always suffice?

    Show solutionHide solution

    There are \(2^n\) inputs and two outputs, so by the pigeonhole principle one output is shared by at least \(2^{n-1}\) inputs. To be one-to-one, the outputs must take at least \(2^n\) different values, which needs at least \(n\) bits in total: the answer bit and at least \(n-1\) further bits. Keeping all \(n\) input bits always suffices, since the input can then be read off directly. Whether \(n-1\) kept bits are enough depends on the function.

Why retain more than the answer?

Suppose an AND calculation retains only its output. What prevents reversing it, and why would retaining both input bits change the question?

Consider the argument

If the output is zero, the input could have been 00, 01, or 10. Keeping the input bits preserves this distinction. We then need to specify operations that update the output and any temporary storage in a reversible way.

1. Quick check §

A proof search over a three-symbol alphabet has checked every candidate of length at most \(10\), including the empty string, without finding a proof. How many candidates is that, and what does the failure establish?

Hint 1

There are \(3^k\) strings of length \(k\).

Hint 2

Unprovability is a claim about proofs of every length.

Worked solution

\(1+3+\dots+3^{10}=(3^{11}-1)/2=88{,}573\) candidates. The failure shows only that the statement has no proof of length at most \(10\). A longer proof may exist, and no finite search can establish that none does.

2. Worked problem §

Run Euclid’s algorithm on \((377,233)\). Count the divisions, record each quotient, and explain separately why the answer is correct and why the run stops.

Hint 1

\(377=1\cdot233+144\).

Hint 2

These are consecutive Fibonacci numbers.

Worked solution

The remainders are \(144,89,55,34,21,13,8,5,3,2,1,0\): twelve divisions, with quotient \(1\) every time except the last, which is \(2\). The greatest common divisor is \(1\). It is correct because each step keeps the set of common divisors unchanged, an invariant. The run stops because the second entry strictly decreases through nonnegative integers. Quotients of \(1\) make that decrease as slow as possible.

Revisit the relevant explanation

3. Extension §

Make each step of Euclid’s algorithm reversible by recording its quotient. Show that \((b,r)\) and \(q\) determine the previous pair, and find how many quotients a run on \((377,233)\) must keep.

Hint 1

The previous pair was \((a,b)\) with \(a=qb+r\).

Hint 2

Use the run from the worked problem.

Worked solution

From \((b,r)\) and \(q\), the previous first entry is \(a=qb+r\), so the previous pair \((a,b)\) is recovered. A run that keeps all its quotients can be undone step by step. The run on \((377,233)\) keeps twelve quotients, eleven \(1\)s and one \(2\). Reversibility costs extra memory that grows with the length of the run.
