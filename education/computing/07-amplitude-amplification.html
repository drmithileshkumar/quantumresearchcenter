Chapter 07

Quantum Search and Amplitude Amplification

How can a process concentrate our chances of finding an answer that it can recognize but does not yet know?

Recognizing a solution and locating it are different tasks. We begin with four possibilities and a phase change that marks the desired answer without changing its probability. A second transformation makes the mark consequential. Following these operations geometrically reveals a rotation, explains why too many iterations can undo the gain, and leads from one small search to the general method of amplitude amplification.

Reading guide · preparation, aims, and assumptions

Turn a phase mark into increased success probability and determine when to stop iterating.

Before you begin

Use phase oracles, reflections, and normalized state vectors.

What you should be able to do

  • Calculate the four-item search.
  • Interpret two reflections as a rotation.
  • Include preparation and oracle costs in amplification.

The example to follow

Start with four equal amplitudes; carry the same reflection picture into the good/bad two-dimensional plane.

Scope and assumptions

The standard iteration formula assumes ideal reflections and a fixed marked subspace. The best iteration count depends on the initial success probability.

7.1

Four-Item Search

With four candidates, every amplitude can be followed without approximation.

7.1.1 Search predicates and classical queries§

Search a set of \(N\) labels using a predicate \(f(x)\) that is one on marked items. An ordinary query tests a candidate. With one uniformly located marked item, classical search needs order \(N\) queries for constant success probability; checking all items settles the worst case. Begin with \(N=4\) and mark \(11\). We count access to the predicate, while keeping state preparation and other operations visible. The task is to output a marked label, not merely estimate the number of them.

Problems

  1. What does a search predicate tell you about a candidate?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It says whether the supplied candidate satisfies the success condition: \(f(x)=1\) for marked and \(0\) otherwise.

  2. Among eight equally likely candidates, two are marked. What is the success probability of one uniformly random guess?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The success probability is \(2/8=1/4\).

  3. Why does a fast test of a candidate not automatically give a fast way to find a successful candidate? What additional structure would allow classical search to do better than inspecting unrelated possibilities?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A quick checker answers about a candidate already supplied; it does not say where a successful candidate is located. Ordering, arithmetic constraints, or another exploitable relation between candidates can guide classical search. An unstructured predicate promises no such relation, so rejecting one candidate may reveal nothing about the others.

7.1.2 State preparation and phase marking§

Two Hadamards prepare \(\ket s=(\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{10}+\ket{11})/2\). A phase oracle \(O_f\ket x=(-1)^{f(x)}\ket x\) changes the amplitudes to \((1,1,1,-1)/2\). Every squared magnitude is still one quarter. Measuring now gains nothing. The oracle has created a relative sign that a subsequent operation can use. A phase oracle may be supplied directly or built by kickback or compute–phase–uncompute; its query cost must be stated accordingly.

Problems

  1. Does changing an amplitude’s sign change its squared magnitude?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. \(|-a|^2=|a|^2\).

  2. Start with four amplitudes of \(1/2\) and negate the marked one. Calculate its probability before and after marking.

    Show solutionHide solution

    Before marking, the probability is \(|1/2|^2=1/4\). After marking, it is \(|-1/2|^2=1/4\).

  3. A phase mark leaves all immediate basis-measurement probabilities unchanged. Why can it still be a useful search operation? What must the next operation make those signs interact with?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The sign changes relative amplitudes, which a later coherent transformation can mix. Diffusion reflects amplitudes about their mean: its sums depend on the signs, so the marked amplitude can grow while others shrink. Measuring immediately after the sign flip would not exploit that distinction.

7.1.3 Reflection and final amplitudes§

Apply \(D=2\ket s\bra s-I\). For an amplitude list \(a_x\), it sends each entry to \(2\mu-a_x\), where \(\mu\) denotes the arithmetic mean of the entries. After marking, the mean is \(1/4\): the three \(+1/2\) entries become zero, and the \(-1/2\) entry becomes one. One iteration \(DO_f\) therefore finds the marked label with certainty. The operation is a reflection about the prepared direction; it is not a measurement that estimates the mean from samples.

Problems

  1. What is the reflection-about-the-mean rule for an amplitude \(a\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Replace each amplitude \(a\) by \(2\mu-a\), where \(\mu\) is the mean amplitude.

  2. For amplitudes \((-1/2,1/2,1/2,1/2)\), find the mean and apply \(2\cdot\text{mean}-a\) to each entry.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The mean is \((-1/2+1/2+1/2+1/2)/4=1/4\). The marked entry becomes \(1/2-(-1/2)=1\); each other entry becomes \(1/2-1/2=0\).

  3. For four items, one reflection after marking can make the marked amplitude one. Why does the same single iteration not generally give certainty for a larger search space? Track how the mean changes.

    Show solutionHide solution

    For one marked item among \(N\), the mean after marking is \((N-2)/(N\sqrt N)\). Diffusion makes the marked amplitude \((3-4/N)/\sqrt N\) and every unmarked amplitude \((1-4/N)/\sqrt N\). The unmarked amplitudes vanish at \(N=4\), but not for \(N>4\). Thus a single iteration generally leaves nonzero failure probability in a larger search space.

Search Geometry

Grouping marked and unmarked components reduces the analysis to a plane.

Problems

  1. What distinguishes the good subspace from the bad subspace?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The good subspace is spanned by successful labels; the bad subspace is spanned by the remaining labels.

  2. In a four-item search with only \(00\) marked, identify the good basis state and list the bad basis states.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The good basis state is \(\ket{00}\). The bad basis states are \(\ket{01}\), \(\ket{10}\), and \(\ket{11}\).

  3. Why can a search over many basis states be described as motion in a two-dimensional plane? Which symmetries of the preparation and operations keep the state inside that plane?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Uniform preparation gives equal amplitudes within each group. The oracle treats every good label alike and every bad label alike, while reflection about the uniform state preserves the span of the normalized good and bad superpositions. These two directions therefore suffice to track this trajectory, although the full register space is much larger.

7.2.2 Initial success amplitude§

Write \(\sin\theta=\sqrt{M/N}\), with \(0<\theta<\pi/2\). Then \(\ket s=\sin\theta\ket G+\cos\theta\ket B\). Its initial success probability is \(\sin^2\theta=M/N\). The geometry uses the bad direction as angle zero and the good direction as angle \(\pi/2\). This choice fixes signs in the rotation calculation. Although the underlying register may have many amplitudes, the two reflections preserve this plane, so one angle is enough to track the entire search evolution.

Problems

  1. If the initial success probability is \(p\), what is the nonnegative good amplitude?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It is \(\sqrt p\) when the normalized good component is defined with a nonnegative coefficient.

  2. For one marked item among four equally weighted items, find \(p\) and \(\sqrt p\). Use \(\sin\theta=\sqrt p\) to find \(\theta\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(p=1/4\) and \(\sqrt p=1/2\). With \(0<\theta<\pi/2\), \(\sin\theta=1/2\) gives \(\theta=\pi/6\), or \(30^\circ\).

  3. Two search problems have the same number of items but different marked fractions. Why do they begin at different angles to the bad direction? How does that alter the amount of rotation needed?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The initial angle satisfies \(\sin^2\theta=M/N\). More marked weight means a larger initial good component and an angle closer to the good axis. Each ideal iteration rotates by \(2\theta\), so the initial fraction changes both the distance to the first peak and the step size. The iteration count must account for both and for integer rounding.

7.2.3 Reflections and rotations§

The oracle negates the good component and retains the bad component: a reflection across the bad axis. The diffusion operation reflects across \(\ket s\). Acting in this order, their product increases the state’s angle from the bad axis by \(2\theta\). Reflection matrices also verify the result algebraically. A geometric drawing is useful because it explains both the increase in success probability and its eventual decrease: a rotation continues past the good axis unless we stop it.

Problems

  1. What geometric operation results from composing the two search reflections?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A rotation in the good/bad plane, by twice the angle between the reflection axes.

  2. For \(\theta=\pi/6\), start at angle \(\theta\) and add \(2\theta\) for one Grover iteration. What angle do you reach?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(\theta+2\theta=3\theta=\pi/2\), or \(90^\circ\), which is the good direction.

  3. Two reflections produce a rotation, but reversing their order reverses its direction. Why must an algorithm specify the order of the marking and preparation reflections rather than merely list them?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Reflection operations generally do not commute. Oracle then diffusion gives a rotation by \(+2\theta\) under the book’s angle convention; reversing the order gives \(-2\theta\). Listing the same two operations without their order leaves the state evolution, and hence the success probability, unspecified.

7.3

Grover Iteration

An algorithm must choose when to stop the rotation, not merely repeat a useful-looking step.

7.3.1 Amplitude evolution§

After \(k\) Grover iterations, \[\ket{\psi_k}=\sin((2k+1)\theta)\ket G+\cos((2k+1)\theta)\ket B,\qquad p_k=\sin^2((2k+1)\theta).\] The initial angle contributes one \(\theta\); each iteration contributes two more. All marked labels have equal amplitudes in this uniform-search setting, so conditional on success the result is uniform over the marked set. The success probability depends on the marked fraction, not on which labels happen to be marked.

Problems

  1. What formula gives success probability after \(k\) Grover iterations?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(p_k=\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)\), with \(\sin^2\theta\) equal to the initial marked fraction.

  2. With \(\theta=\pi/6\), evaluate \(\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)\) for \(k=0\) and \(k=1\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    For \(k=0\), \(p_0=\sin^2(\pi/6)=1/4\). For \(k=1\), \(p_1=\sin^2(\pi/2)=1\).

  3. What does the oscillation of success probability tell you about the difference between coherent amplification and a process that irreversibly accumulates successful outcomes? Why need success not grow monotonically?

    Show solutionHide solution

    A unitary rotation preserves norm and can move toward or away from the good direction. It does not permanently collect and retain a growing set of successful outcomes. Once the trajectory passes the first good-axis peak, the good component decreases; monotonic accumulation would describe a different process.

7.3.2 Iteration count and overshooting§

Choose a nonnegative integer \(k\) near \(\pi/(4\theta)-1/2\) to bring the first peak close to the good axis. For a small marked fraction, \(\theta\approx\sqrt{M/N}\), yielding order \(\sqrt{N/M}\) iterations. Integer rounding usually prevents exact certainty; the four-item, one-mark case is special. More iterations are not always better. With \(N=4,M=1\), the probabilities for \(k=0,1,2\) are \(1/4,1,1/4\). This illustrates why the stopping rule is part of the algorithm rather than an implementation detail.

Predict first. Set Search space to 4 and reset. There is one marked item; start from equal amplitudes. Predict the success probability after one iteration and after a second. Does another iteration always help?

Experiment: rotate toward the marked item

There is one marked item. A full Grover iteration rotates the state by \(2\theta\), where \(\sin\theta=1/\sqrt N\). Keep stepping after the first peak to see why overshooting matters.

Probability of finding the marked item: 6.25%.

The initial state spreads probability uniformly over all items.

After trying it: compare your explanation

One iteration gives success probability 1. A second gives \(1/4\): continued rotation can move the state away from the marked direction. Compare this with the initial probability \(1/4\).

Problems

  1. Can another Grover iteration reduce success probability?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Yes. An iteration can rotate the state past the direction of maximum success.

  2. For \(\theta=\pi/6\), compare success at \(k=1\) with success at \(k=2\) using the iteration formula.

    Show solutionHide solution

    At \(k=1\), success is \(\sin^2(\pi/2)=1\). At \(k=2\), it is \(\sin^2(5\pi/6)=1/4\).

  3. An iteration rule can increase success initially and decrease it later. What information is needed to choose a stopping point? Why is “keep improving until certain” not an adequate instruction here?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The stopping calculation needs the initial success fraction, or a procedure that handles its uncertainty, together with the iteration rule and desired error bound. A measurement to check progress generally changes the coherent state. Repeating a rotation is not an automatic improvement step, and integer step sizes need not hit certainty exactly.

7.3.3 Unknown marked fraction and verification§

If the marked fraction is unknown, a fixed peak estimate may overshoot. A standard approach chooses a random iteration count from a gradually expanding range, measures, and verifies the candidate. For \(M>0\), suitable schedules retain expected order \(\sqrt{N/M}\) query cost. If \(M=0\), verification never succeeds; an unbounded search cannot certify absence. A bounded-error decision version uses a bounded schedule and reports failure with a specified error guarantee when marked items exist. Distinguish that guarantee from an exact proof that none exists. If all items are marked, direct sampling already succeeds.

Further detail: Boyer, Brassard, Høyer, and Tapp: Tight bounds on quantum searching.

Problems

  1. What does verification check after a candidate is measured?

    Show solutionHide solution

    It evaluates the success predicate on the particular measured candidate.

  2. A measured candidate fails the predicate. Does that prove that every candidate fails? Explain with a four-item example.

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. If only \(11\) is marked and the measurement gives \(00\), verification rejects \(00\) while \(11\) remains a valid answer.

  3. Why does verifying a candidate solve a different problem from knowing how many amplification steps to use? If a run fails verification, what has it established—and what has it not established—about the existence of a marked item?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Verification establishes whether one measured label succeeds. Choosing an iteration count requires information about the initial successful weight or a schedule robust to not knowing it. Failure rejects that candidate; it does not identify the marked fraction or prove that no marked candidate exists.

7.4

General Amplitude Amplification

The same construction works with a general coherent preparation and a success test.

Uniform preparation is not essential. Let \(A\ket0=\ket\psi\), and let \(P_G\) project onto the states counted as successful. If \(0

Problems

  1. Must amplitude amplification start from a uniform superposition?

    Show solutionHide solution

    No. It can use a coherent preparation A with a defined success subspace, together with the required inverse and reflections.

  2. A preparation succeeds with probability \(1/4\). Identify its good and bad amplitude magnitudes using \(\sqrt p\) and \(\sqrt{1-p}\).

    Show solutionHide solution

    The good magnitude is \(\sqrt{1/4}=1/2\). The bad magnitude is \(\sqrt{3/4}=\sqrt3/2\).

  3. If an initial preparation already favors good answers, how should that change amplification? Which feature of the preparation matters more than the raw number of basis states?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The relevant geometric quantity is the total initial success probability \(p\), not simply the number of basis labels. A more favorable preparation changes \(\theta\) through \(\sin^2\theta=p\) and therefore the appropriate number of rotations. For small \(p\), the required rounds scale as order \(1/\sqrt p\); already high success may make further amplification unnecessary.

7.4.2 Reflection using preparation and its inverse§

Reflection about the prepared state is \[R_\psi=2\ket\psi\bra\psi-I=A(2\ket0\bra0-I)A^\dagger.\] Applied to an existing state, the rightmost inverse acts first. Pair this with \(I-2P_G\), which negates successful components. Their product rotates toward the good direction just as in search. The construction requires coherent access to \(A\), its inverse, and the success test. A destructive measurement of success is not a replacement for the coherent phase reflection.

Problems

  1. Which inverse appears in the reflection \(A(2\ketbra00-I)A^\dagger\)?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(A^\dagger\), the inverse of the unitary preparation \(A\), acts first.

  2. Apply this reflection to \(A\ket0\) by first using \(A^\dagger A=I\). Does the prepared state change sign?

    Show solutionHide solution

    \(A(2\ketbra00-I)A^\dagger A\ket0=A(2\ket0-\ket0)=A\ket0\). The prepared direction keeps its sign; directions orthogonal to it change sign.

  3. Why does implementing reflection about a prepared state require access to the preparation’s inverse? Is being able to obtain a copy of that state by an uncontrolled process enough to build the same reflection?

    Show solutionHide solution

    In this construction, \(A^\dagger\) brings the prepared direction back to the known \(\ket0\) direction, the simple reflection acts there, and \(A\) returns to the original coordinates. Samples of the prepared state alone do not supply this coherent action on arbitrary inputs or its inverse. Other implementations require additional information or access assumptions.

7.4.3 Success probability and resource cost§

For small \(p\), direct independent trials need order \(1/p\) preparations for constant success probability. Amplitude amplification needs order \(1/\sqrt p\) iterations, each using the preparation, its inverse, and a coherent success test. If their costs are \(C_A\) and \(C_G\), the resulting cost is of order \((C_A+C_G)/\sqrt p\), under the stated access assumptions. Verification and iteration selection must also be included. The quadratic improvement is in repeated use of these resources; expensive state loading or oracle construction can dominate the total runtime.

Problems

  1. What costs besides iteration count enter amplitude amplification?

    Show solutionHide solution

    The costs of initial preparation, the preparation and inverse within rounds, the coherent success test, and verification or readout also matter.

  2. Suppose each round uses 12 gates and the initial preparation uses 8. Calculate the cost of five rounds, ignoring measurement.

    Show solutionHide solution

    The total is \(8+5\times12=68\) gates, under the stated convention that 12 includes the entire cost of each round.

  3. A better initial success probability can reduce the number of amplification rounds, yet its preparation may be expensive. How would you decide whether the improved preparation saves total work?

    Show solutionHide solution

    Compare complete costs for the competing preparations. For small \(p\), a useful estimate is cost per round divided by \(\sqrt p\), plus initial preparation and final verification. A larger \(p\) helps only if the reduction in rounds outweighs any increase in preparing, reversing, and testing the state. Query count by itself is insufficient.
