5.1

Quantum Model of Computation

How do the postulates become a procedure that answers a question?

Read the chapter2 / 14

What does a computation consist of?

  1. Choose an input state \(\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}\) that encodes the question.
  2. Apply unitary steps, together one unitary \(U\).
  3. Measure: one result \(y\) per run, with probability \(|\text{amplitude}|^2\).
  4. Naming \(U\) says nothing about how it is built, or what it costs.
§5.1.1 in the book3 / 14

Can a step that changes no probability matter?

  1. Two programs from \(\ket0\): \(H,H\) and \(H,Z,H\).
  2. After the first \(H\): probabilities \(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\). \(Z\) leaves them unchanged.
  3. \(HH=I\), but \(HZH=X\).
  4. The relative sign, invisible in the middle, decides the answer.
§5.1.2 in the book4 / 14

What does one run return, and what does it cost?

  1. \(p(y)=|\bra yU\ket{\psi_{\mathrm{in}}}|^2\)
  2. Correct with probability 3/4? Repeat and take the most frequent answer; each repetition costs again.
  3. Count qubits, gates, layers, repetitions, preparation, and readout.
  4. One qubit cannot show a speedup: that needs tasks of growing size.

Single-Qubit Computation

Can one qubit carry out a complete computation?

Read the chapter6 / 14

How do we write a program?

A wire follows a qubit through time; boxes are gates; M is a measurement; a double line carries a classical bit.

§5.2.1 in the book7 / 14

Computing NOT with interference

\(\ket0\xrightarrow{H}\tfrac{\ket0+\ket1}{\sqrt2}\xrightarrow{Z}\tfrac{\ket0-\ket1}{\sqrt2}\xrightarrow{H}\ket1\)

At 0 the contributions \(+\tfrac12\) and \(-\tfrac12\) cancel; at 1 they add to one.

§5.2.2 in the book8 / 14

Predict

What if we look in the middle?

Insert a measurement after the first H. What does the program output now?

Answer

The state becomes |0⟩ or |1⟩, and Z then H give each answer half the time. Both the relative sign and the absence of a measurement are needed.

§5.2.2 in the book9 / 14

Between no sign change and a full one

  1. Phase gate: \(R_\phi\ket0=\ket0,\ R_\phi\ket1=e^{i\phi}\ket1\).
  2. \[HR_\phi H\ket0=\frac{1+e^{i\phi}}2\ket0+\frac{1-e^{i\phi}}2\ket1\]
  3. \(p(0)=\frac{1+\cos\phi}{2}\)
  4. \(\phi=0\): 0. \(\phi=\pi\): 1. \(\phi=\pi/2\): even odds.

The last gate turns a phase no measurement could see into the probabilities of the answer.

5.3

Pauli Operators

How many single-qubit operations are there, and can a few describe them all?

Read the chapter11 / 14

An operation that exchanges and flips sign

  1. \(XZ\ket0=\ket1,\quad XZ\ket1=-\ket0\).
  2. \((XZ)^2=-I\), so multiply by \(i\): \(Y=iXZ\), and \(Y^2=I\).
  3. \[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\ Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\ Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\]
  4. \(ZX=-XZ\): a product of Paulis is a Pauli times a phase.
§5.3.1 in the book12 / 14

Can four matrices describe every operator?

  1. \[A=\tfrac{a+d}2I+\tfrac{b+c}2X+\tfrac{c-b}{2i}Y+\tfrac{a-d}2Z\]
  2. For example, \(H=(X+Z)/\sqrt2\).
  3. But a circuit multiplies gates; it does not add them. No Pauli circuit turns \(\ket0\) into \(H\ket0\).
  4. A continuous family: \(R_x(\theta)=\cos\tfrac\theta2I-i\sin\tfrac\theta2X\).

Describing an operator is not the same as building it from gates.

§5.3.2 in the book13 / 14

What we found

  1. A computation is an input state, unitary steps, and a measurement read as an answer.
  2. A relative phase invisible in the middle can decide the result.
  3. Cost counts gates, qubits, layers, repetitions, and readout.
  4. One qubit runs a full computation; the phase gate sets its output probabilities.
  5. I, X, Y, Z describe every single-qubit operator, but building gates is a separate question.
