4.1

Vectors and Matrices

States become vectors, amplitudes between states become inner products, and steps become matrices, each built from the path rules.

Read the chapter2 / 24

State vectors

A state \(\ket\psi=\sum_iv_i\ket i\) gives each configuration an amplitude. Its bookkeeping rules are those of vector arithmetic, so we treat \(\ket\psi\) as a vector. A two-configuration quantum system is a qubit; a weighted sum of kets is a superposition.

Order the configurations (0 first) and list the amplitudes:

\[\ket0=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad v_0\ket0+v_1\ket1=\begin{pmatrix}v_0\\v_1\end{pmatrix}.\]

\(\ket0,\ket1\) form the computational basis. Adding states adds coordinates; multiplying by a number multiplies every coordinate.

Example: \(\tfrac35\ket0+\tfrac45\ket1\) has probabilities \(\tfrac9{25}\) and \(\tfrac{16}{25}\). The coordinates describe one state, not two outcomes at once.

Steps and larger registers as columns

The Hadamard step, written on columns:

\[H:\ \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\quad \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Each coordinate of an image is an arrow weight \(\bra jH\ket i\); the minus sign is the negative arrow \(1\to1\).

Nothing is special about two configurations. With \(00,01,10,11\) in that order,

\[\frac{\ket{00}-\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

The amplitude between two states

Write \(\braket j\psi=\psi_j\) for the amplitude of \(j\) in \(\ket\psi\). Each path to a final state \(\ket\phi\) passes through one \(j\), so

\[\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\braket\phi j\,\braket j\psi .\]

What is \(\braket\phi j\)? Normalization, \(\sum_j\braket j\psi^*\braket j\psi=1\), matches the path formula for \(\braket\psi\psi\) if we choose

\[\braket\phi j=\braket j\phi^*=\phi_j^* .\]

Inner products

Definition

The inner product (transition amplitude from \(\ket\psi\) to \(\ket\phi\)) is \(\displaystyle\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j\).

The bra \(\bra\phi\) is the conjugated row of \(\ket\phi\). Row times column performs the path sum:

\[\bra\phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_0^*&\phi_1^*\end{pmatrix},\qquad \braket\phi\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_0^*&\phi_1^*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_0\\\psi_1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Exchanging initial and final states conjugates the amplitude (conjugate symmetry):

\[\braket\psi\phi=\sum_j\psi_j^*\phi_j=\Bigl(\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j\Bigr)^*=\braket\phi\psi^* .\]

Example: for \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), \(\braket1\psi=i/\sqrt2\) but \(\braket\psi1=-i/\sqrt2\). The magnitude is unchanged; for real vectors the order does not matter.

This definition is chosen for consistency with the probability rule; its physical meaning for a general final state comes with the measurement postulate.

Length, orthogonality, and projection

The norm is \(\|\psi\|=\sqrt{\braket\psi\psi}\); normalized states are unit vectors. Vectors with \(\braket\phi\psi=0\) are orthogonal; mutually orthogonal unit vectors are orthonormal.

Split a unit vector \(\ket\psi\) into a part along a unit vector \(\ket\phi\) and a part orthogonal to it:

\[\ket\psi=\braket\phi\psi\,\ket\phi+\ket{\psi_\perp},\qquad \braket\phi{\psi_\perp}=0 .\]

The parts are perpendicular, so their squared lengths add:

\[1=|\braket\phi\psi|^2+\|\psi_\perp\|^2 .\]

Hence \(0\le|\braket\phi\psi|^2\le1\): equal to 1 when the states differ by a global phase, 0 when orthogonal. For real unit vectors \(\braket\phi\psi=\cos\theta\).

§4.1.3 in the book7 / 24

Coordinates are components

Doing the split along every basis direction gives the coordinates: \(\braket j\psi\) is the component of \(\ket\psi\) along \(\ket j\). Because the basis is orthonormal,

\[\ket\psi=\sum_j\ket j\braket j\psi,\qquad \braket\psi\psi=\sum_j|\braket j\psi|^2 .\]

The probabilities of the configurations are the squared lengths of these perpendicular components.

Example: \(\ket\psi=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\ket0+\tfrac12\ket1\) and \(\ket\phi=\ket0\). Then \(\braket0\psi=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2=\cos30^\circ\), with probability \(\tfrac34\); the remainder \(\tfrac12\ket1\) is orthogonal to \(\ket0\).

The picture is a plane in state space, not physical space. Complex vectors keep this geometry while the inner product also carries phase.

Linearity and transition matrices

A step sends each configuration to the weighted sum of its successors and a state to the same combination of these images:

\[U\ket\psi=v_0U\ket0+v_1U\ket1,\]
\[U(a\ket\phi+b\ket\psi)=aU\ket\phi+bU\ket\psi .\]

This is linearity. The transition matrix stores the arrow weights: \(U_{ji}=\bra jU\ket i\), with column \(i\) for the input and row \(j\) for the output.

Matrix–vector multiplication performs the path sums:

\[v_j^\prime=\sum_iU_{ji}v_i,\qquad \ket{\psi^\prime}=U\ket\psi .\]
§4.1.4 in the book9 / 24

Unitary matrices

Every normalized input must give a normalized output. The two conditions of Chapter 3 say:

  • each column has unit length (outgoing normalization);
  • distinct columns are orthogonal (cross-sum condition).
\[\sum_jU_{ji}^*U_{jk}=\delta_{ik}\quad(\delta_{ik}=1\text{ if }i=k,\ 0\text{ otherwise}).\]

A square matrix with orthonormal columns is unitary. Example:

\[U=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}4&-3\\3&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad U\Bigl(\tfrac35\ket0+\tfrac45\ket1\Bigr)=\ket1 .\]
§4.1.4 in the book10 / 24

Gate matrices and composition

\(X\) exchanges the basis directions, \(Z\) flips the sign of the \(\ket1\) component, \(H\) sends them to the two diagonals:

\[X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\ Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\ H=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

After a step, comparing with \(\ket\phi\) gives the amplitude

\[\bra\phi U\ket\psi=\sum_{i,j}\braket\phi j\,\bra jU\ket i\,\braket i\psi .\]

Two steps, \(U\) first and \(V\) second, give \(VU\ket\psi\). The two-step sum rule is the matrix product:

\[(VU)_{ki}=\sum_jV_{kj}U_{ji}.\]

The rightmost matrix acts first. For example \(HH=I\), the identity matrix:

\[\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]
§4.1.4 in the book11 / 24

Undoing a step: the adjoint

Reversing arrows undoes CNOT. Try it on \(\sqrt X\) (stay \(a=\tfrac{1+i}2\), switch \(b=\tfrac{1-i}2\)): its arrows look the same both ways, so reversing gives \(\sqrt X\) again and \(\sqrt X\sqrt X=X\), not \(I\). The return paths give \(a^2+b^2=0\).

Reverse and also conjugate the weights. Return paths: \(a^*a+b^*b=1\); crossing paths: \(b^*a+a^*b=0\). The step is undone.

Adjoint

\(U^\dagger\) reverses every arrow of \(U\) and conjugates its weight: \(\bra iU^\dagger\ket j=\bra jU\ket i^*\). As a matrix, \(U^\dagger\) is the conjugate transpose.

This is the rule \(\braket\phi j=\braket j\phi^*\) applied to the arrows of a step: going backward conjugates.

§4.1.5 in the book12 / 24

Unitary steps are undone by their adjoint

Apply \(U\), then \(U^\dagger\), and use the sum rule:

\[\bra iU^\dagger U\ket k=\sum_j\bra jU\ket i^*\,\bra jU\ket k .\]

This is 1 for \(i=k\) (normalization) and 0 for \(i\ne k\) (cross sum). Hence

\[U^\dagger U=I,\qquad U^{-1}=U^\dagger .\]

Moving an operator across an inner product: \(\bra\phi U\ket\psi=\braket{U^\dagger\phi}\psi\). With \(\ket\phi=U\ket\chi\):

\[\braket{U\chi}{U\psi}=\braket\chi\psi .\]

A unitary step preserves every length and angle: it moves state space rigidly, like a rotation. Example: \(H^\dagger=H\), so \(H\) undoes itself.

§4.1.5 in the book13 / 24

The adjoint is not always the inverse

Every matrix has an adjoint, but it undoes the matrix only when the matrix is unitary. Take the shear

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

\(A\) slides points sideways by their height; \(A^\dagger\) slides them upward by their horizontal position. Neither \(AA^\dagger\) nor \(A^\dagger A\) returns \(\tfrac35\ket0+\tfrac45\ket1\): they give \((2,\tfrac75)\) and \((\tfrac75,\tfrac{11}5)\).

Undoing \(A\) needs \(A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). The identity \(\bra\phi A\ket\psi=\braket{A^\dagger\phi}\psi\) still holds; the adjoint always moves an operator across an inner product. A shear stretches lengths, so it cannot be a step of a closed quantum system.

4.2

Postulates

Which of these calculations describe nature: states, closed evolution, measurement, and composite systems.

Read the chapter15 / 24

The state postulate

Multiplying every amplitude by the same \(e^{i\theta}\) changes no probability, now or after any later step, because every path sum gains the same factor. Such vectors cannot be told apart.

State postulate

A state of a finite quantum system is a unit vector of complex amplitudes, with the inner product above. Vectors differing only by a global phase represent the same physical state.

For a qubit, normalization ties the two magnitudes and the global phase can make the first amplitude real and nonnegative. One angle and one relative phase remain:

\[\ket\psi=\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0+e^{i\phi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1,\qquad 0\le\theta\le\pi .\]

At \(\theta=0\) or \(\pi\) one amplitude vanishes and no relative phase remains.

§4.2.1 in the book16 / 24

The evolution postulate

The mathematics shows that probability-preserving steps are unitary. The postulate asserts that nature uses them.

Evolution postulate

The evolution of a closed system is unitary: \(\ket\psi\mapsto U\ket\psi\) with \(U^\dagger U=I\).

With real amplitudes, unit vectors end on a circle and a unitary is a rotation:

\[U(\delta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\delta&-\sin\delta\\\sin\delta&\cos\delta\end{pmatrix}.\]

Lengths and angles between states are kept; in general \(\braket{U\chi}{U\psi}=\braket\chi\psi\).

§4.2.1 in the book17 / 24

The measurement postulate

Measurement postulate

For a normalized \(\ket\psi=\sum_jv_j\ket j\), an ideal measurement in the computational basis gives outcome \(j\) with probability \(|\braket j\psi|^2=|v_j|^2\). Conditional on that outcome, the state becomes \(\ket j\).

Both the probability and the update are physical assumptions, not consequences of path arithmetic.

Example: \(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\ket0+\tfrac12\ket1\) gives 0 with probability \(\tfrac34\), 1 with \(\tfrac14\). After outcome 1 the state is \(\ket1\), so an immediate repeat gives 1.

Measurement as projection

A state is the sum of its components along the basis directions, \(\ket\psi=\sum_j\ket j\braket j\psi\). Measurement picks one component, with probability equal to its squared length, keeps it, and rescales it to unit length.

The projector \(P_j=\ketbra jj\) keeps the component along \(\ket j\): \(P_j\ket\psi=\ket j\braket j\psi\). As a matrix it is a column times a row, e.g.

\[\ketbra00=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.\]

In this language,

\[p(j)=\|P_j\ket\psi\|^2,\qquad \ket{\psi_j}=\frac{P_j\ket\psi}{\sqrt{p(j)}}\quad(p(j)>0).\]
§4.2.2 in the book19 / 24

Measuring in other bases

Let a unitary \(U\) carry an orthonormal basis \(\ket{b_j}\) to the computational basis. Measuring in the \(b\) basis means applying \(U\) and then measuring:

\[p(j)=|\bra jU\ket\psi|^2=|\braket{U^\dagger j}\psi|^2=|\braket{b_j}\psi|^2 .\]

So \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\) is the probability of outcome \(\phi\) in any orthonormal basis containing \(\ket\phi\); afterwards the state is \(\ket\phi\).

Real example: \(\ket\psi=\cos\alpha\ket0+\sin\alpha\ket1\), \(\ket{b_0}=\cos\beta\ket0+\sin\beta\ket1\), \(\ket{b_1}=-\sin\beta\ket0+\cos\beta\ket1\) give amplitudes \(\cos(\alpha-\beta)\) and \(\sin(\alpha-\beta)\).

\(\beta=0\) is the Z measurement; \(\beta=45^\circ\) is the X measurement, with \(\ket\pm=(\ket0\pm\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Preparing \(\ket+\): X gives \(+\) with certainty, Z gives each outcome half the time. The measurement asks a different question; the state is the same.

With \(\beta=45^\circ\), \(\ket{b_1}=-\ket-\); an overall sign does not change the projector.

Evolution and measurement together

A history alternates the two kinds of change: unitary evolution between measurements, and at each measurement the observed outcome selects a component, which is rescaled to unit length.

Example: from \(\ket0\), \(H\) gives \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Uninterrupted, a second \(H\) returns \(\ket0\) with certainty. Measured in between, the state becomes \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and the second \(H\) gives equal probabilities.

The update cannot be undone: after outcome 0, an initial \(\ket0\) and an initial \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) both leave \(\ket0\).

The larger system, including the device and its record, may still evolve unitarily; the conditional description keeps only the reported branch. In the figure, vertical drops are schematic and do not mean a loss of norm or energy.

Composite systems

A complete configuration of two qubits names one configuration of each: \(00,01,10,11\). A joint state assigns an amplitude to each:

\[\ket\Psi=c_{00}\ket{00}+c_{01}\ket{01}+c_{10}\ket{10}+c_{11}\ket{11},\]

with \(\sum_{i,j}|c_{ij}|^2=1\).

Independent preparations \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) and \(c\ket0+d\ket1\): reaching 01 means 0 in \(A\) and 1 in \(B\), so its amplitude is \(ad\). This is the tensor product:

\[\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}c\\d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix},\qquad \ket i\otimes\ket j=\ket{ij}.\]

The composite-state postulate and entanglement

Composite-state postulate

The state space of a composite system is the tensor product of the state spaces of its parts, with basis \(\ket{ij}=\ket i\otimes\ket j\). Every unit vector in it is an allowed state.

Dimensions multiply: parts with \(m\) and \(n\) basis states give \(mn\). Are there allowed states that are not products? Take

\[\frac{\ket{00}+\ket{11}}{\sqrt2}.\]

A product would need \(ac\ne0\) and \(bd\ne0\), so all of \(a,b,c,d\ne0\); then \(ad\) and \(bc\) could not vanish. But this state has no 01 or 10 term. A pure joint state that is not a product is entangled.

Measuring both qubits gives 00 or 11, each half the time. A classical coin choosing 00 or 11 gives the same counts, so correlation alone does not reveal entanglement; the difference lies in the amplitudes of the whole state.

Summary

  1. A state is a vector of amplitudes; the inner product \(\braket\phi\psi=\sum_j\phi_j^*\psi_j\) is the transition amplitude, with the conjugate forced by the probability rule.
  2. A step is a matrix \(U_{ji}=\bra jU\ket i\); composition is the matrix product; probability is preserved exactly when \(U\) is unitary, and then \(U^{-1}=U^\dagger\).
  3. Postulates: unit vectors up to global phase; unitary closed evolution; measurement with probability \(|\braket\phi\psi|^2\) and state update.
  4. Composite systems live in the tensor product; states that do not factor are entangled.
