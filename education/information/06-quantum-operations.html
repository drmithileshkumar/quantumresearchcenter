Before you begin

Starting point: Chapters 3–5; operator sums and complete positivity are developed through composite examples.

Keep in view: A qubit coupled to an auxiliary qubit that is subsequently discarded.

Predict first. Does a noise probability of one always mean a maximally mixed output?

Compare your reasoning

No. A phase flip applied with certainty is a known unitary. Randomness in the applied operation, and which operation is used, matter.

6.1 Physical Constructions

Let a qubit control an initially zero auxiliary via CNOT, then ignore the auxiliary. The joint evolution is reversible, yet the qubit loses its visible interference. Irreversibility can arise from limiting which systems we retain.

6.1.1 Ancillas, interaction, and discarding§

For \(\ket\psi=a\ket0+b\ket1\), the interaction produces \(a\ket{00}+b\ket{11}\). Tracing the auxiliary out gives diag(|a|²,|b|²). The off-diagonal terms have vanished locally because the auxiliary keeps a distinguishing record. If we retain both systems and reverse CNOT, the initial pure qubit returns. Losing access to the auxiliary changes the available recovery.

Put plus into the CNOT construction. The system and auxiliary become \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\). If we keep only the system, a later \(H\) readout is fair. If we reverse CNOT first, the joint state returns to \(\ket+\ket0\), and the same readout gives zero certainly. The difference is access to the auxiliary that holds the record, not a failure of the joint unitary to have an inverse.

We call an auxiliary system an ancilla. It may be an apparatus we control or a useful model of an environment we cannot access. Discarding means predictions no longer condition on or act on that output. It need not mean physically destroying the object.

6.1.2 Reversible and irreversible descriptions§

The construction is: initialize an auxiliary state, apply a joint unitary, and discard some output systems. Resetting a qubit is an extreme example: swap it with a zero auxiliary and discard the old state. This is a valid operation on the retained system, but not an invertible unitary there. The information went into another system rather than being reversibly merged into one state.

Problems

Problem 6.1. What does the CNOT-and-discard construction do to plus and minus? Can an operation on the retained qubit recover both?

Worked solution

Both yield I/2 because their diagonal probabilities are the same. A single recovery map receives the same matrix in both cases and cannot output two different original states.

6.2 Quantum Channels

6.2.1 Operator-sum representations§

For a pure zero auxiliary, define \(K_j=\bra j_EU\ket0_E\). Discarding the auxiliary gives the operator-sum rule \(\mathcal E(\rho)=\sum_jK_j\rho K_j^\dagger\). For complete dephasing, K0=|0〉〈0| and K1=|1〉〈1|. These operators describe an unobserved environmental branch; they are not necessarily outcomes available to the user.

The notation for \(K_j\) has a concrete meaning: put the auxiliary input at zero, perform \(U\), and collect the system amplitudes associated with auxiliary output \(j\). For the CNOT construction, the zero branch keeps only the system’s zero component, so \(K_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,0)\); the one branch keeps only its one component, so \(K_1=\mathrm{diag}(0,1)\). Adding \(K_0\rho K_0^\dagger\) and \(K_1\rho K_1^\dagger\) removes exactly the off-diagonal entries.

6.2.2 Trace preservation and complete positivity§

Trace preservation requires \(\sum_jK_j^\dagger K_j=I\). Positivity must remain true even when the input is entangled with an untouched reference; this is complete positivity. The auxiliary-unitary construction guarantees it. Ordinary matrix transposition preserves positivity of a single matrix but fails this stronger requirement: partial transposition of a Bell density matrix has an eigenvalue −1/2. It cannot be a physical channel on one half of every entangled input.

To check normalization, use \(\mathrm{tr}(AB)=\mathrm{tr}(BA)\), which follows by interchanging the two summed matrix indices. Then \(\mathrm{tr}\mathcal E(\rho)=\mathrm{tr}[\rho\sum_j K_j^\dagger K_j]\). The completeness equation makes this equal to one for every normalized input. Individual branches can have trace below one; their traces are the branch probabilities.

Problems

Problem 6.2. Verify completeness for the dephasing operators K0 and K1.

Worked solution

The operators are the orthogonal projectors onto zero and one. Each squares to itself, so K0†K0+K1†K1=diag(1,0)+diag(0,1)=I.

6.3 Noise and Composition

6.3.1 Simple qubit channels§

A phase-flip channel is \((1-p)\rho+pZ\rho Z\); its off-diagonal entries are multiplied by 1−2p. An amplitude-damping channel has \(K_0=\ket0\bra0+\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket1\bra1\), \(K_1=\sqrt\gamma\ket0\bra1\). On \(\rho=\begin{pmatrix}a&c\\c^*&b\end{pmatrix}\), its output is \(\begin{pmatrix}a+\gamma b&\sqrt{1-\gamma}c\\\sqrt{1-\gamma}c^*&(1-\gamma)b\end{pmatrix}\). This explicitly moves excited population to zero while reducing coherence.

For a noisy plus state, substitute \(a=b=c=1/2\) into amplitude damping. The output is

\[\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+\gamma&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\\\sqrt{1-\gamma}&1-\gamma\end{pmatrix}.\]

At \(\gamma=0\) it is plus; at \(\gamma=1\) it is zero. At an intermediate value it generally has both uncertain populations and reduced coherence. This differs from phase-flip noise, which leaves the populations unchanged. Two noise mechanisms can therefore give the same outcome counts in one experiment and different counts in another.

For phase-flip noise on plus, the probability of a subsequent plus test is \(1-p\). Complete randomization occurs at \(p=1/2\); at \(p=1\), every signal receives the same known \(Z\), and the output is pure minus. Increasing a flip probability all the way to one is not the same as increasing ignorance all the way to its maximum.

6.3.2 Channel composition and accessible information§

Composing channels multiplies their branch operators: if the first has Kj and the second Ll, the combined operators are LlKj. A receiver cannot gain optimal distinguishability by applying the same channel to both candidate inputs: any later decision procedure could also be performed after that channel as part of an original-input measurement. This operational data-processing argument concerns optimized accessible decisions, not every arbitrarily chosen measurement statistic.

A measurement is an operation whose branch record is retained. We now describe both the outcome probabilities and the conditional state changes.

Problems

Problem 6.3. Does reversing the retained qubit alone undo complete dephasing?

Worked solution

No universal qubit-only inverse exists. Plus and minus both become I/2, so their distinction has been lost from the retained subsystem.

Transfer problem. Apply phase-flip noise with probability p twice independently. Find the net flip probability and explain the cases p=1/2 and p=1.

Worked solution

Exactly one flip occurs with probability \(2p(1-p)\); zero or two flips have no net effect since \(Z^2=I\). At p=1/2 the result is again complete dephasing. At p=1 the two known flips cancel and the net channel is the identity.

Questions and worked problems

These exercises draw on the whole chapter. Attempt each question before opening a hint, explain your reasoning, then compare it with the worked solution.

1. Quick check §

Apply the phase-flip channel \((1-p)\rho+pZ\rho Z\) with \(p=0.1\) to \(\ket+\bra+\). Give the output matrix and the probability that a \(\pm\) test returns \(+\).

Hint 1

Off-diagonal entries are multiplied by \(1-2p\).

Hint 2

\(p(+)=\frac12(\rho_{00}+\rho_{01}+\rho_{10}+\rho_{11})\).

Worked solution

The diagonal stays \((\frac12,\frac12)\) and the coherences become \(\frac12(1-0.2)=0.4\), so the output is \(\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.4\\0.4&0.5\end{pmatrix}\). Then \(p(+)=\frac12(0.5+0.4+0.4+0.5)=0.9\). A computational-basis test sees no change at all; only an interference-sensitive test reveals the noise.

2. Quick check §

Verify the completeness relation \(K_0^\dagger K_0+K_1^\dagger K_1=I\) for amplitude damping, \(K_0=\ket0\bra0+\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket1\bra1\) and \(K_1=\sqrt\gamma\ket0\bra1\). What does each branch do to \(\ket1\)?

Hint 1

\(K_1^\dagger K_1=\gamma\ket1\bra0\ket0\bra1=\gamma\ket1\bra1\).

Hint 2

\(K_0^\dagger K_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,1-\gamma)\).

Worked solution

\(K_0^\dagger K_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,1-\gamma)\) and \(K_1^\dagger K_1=\gamma\ket1\bra1=\mathrm{diag}(0,\gamma)\); their sum is \(I\), so the channel preserves trace. On \(\ket1\), branch \(K_0\) leaves \(\sqrt{1-\gamma}\ket1\) (probability \(1-\gamma\)) and branch \(K_1\) gives \(\sqrt\gamma\ket0\) (probability \(\gamma\)): the excitation either survives or decays to \(\ket0\), with the environment recording which.

3. Quick check §

The bit-flip channel is \((1-p)\rho+pX\rho X\). Find its output for \(\ket0\bra0\) and for \(\ket+\bra+\). Why is one input untouched?

Hint 1

\(X\ket0=\ket1\) and \(X\ket+=\ket+\).

Hint 2

An input unchanged by every branch is unchanged by the channel.

Worked solution

For \(\ket0\bra0\) the output is \(\mathrm{diag}(1-p,p)\): the computational-basis value becomes uncertain. For \(\ket+\bra+\), \(X\ket+\bra+X=\ket+\bra+\), so the output is \(\ket+\bra+\) for every \(p\). A channel’s effect depends on the input: bit-flip noise damages the \(Z\) basis and spares the \(\pm\) basis, while phase-flip noise does the opposite. Error correction in Chapter 14 exploits exactly this structure.

4. Worked problem §

Apply amplitude damping with parameter \(\gamma\) twice. Using the output formula, show that the result is amplitude damping with \(\gamma'=1-(1-\gamma)^2\). If each step lasts time \(\tau\) and \(1-\gamma=e^{-\tau/T}\), what is \(\gamma'\) after \(n\) steps?

Hint 1

Each application multiplies \(b\) by \(1-\gamma\) and the coherence \(c\) by \(\sqrt{1-\gamma}\).

Hint 2

The total population is preserved, so \(a\) gains what \(b\) loses.

Worked solution

One application sends \(b\to(1-\gamma)b\), \(c\to\sqrt{1-\gamma}\,c\), and \(a\to a+\gamma b\). Applying it again gives \(b\to(1-\gamma)^2b\) and \(c\to(1-\gamma)c\), with \(a\) absorbing the rest. That is damping with \(1-\gamma'=(1-\gamma)^2\), so \(\gamma'=2\gamma-\gamma^2\). After \(n\) steps, \(1-\gamma'=(1-\gamma)^n=e^{-n\tau/T}\): exponential decay of the excited population with time constant \(T\), and of the coherence with time constant \(2T\). Composition of channels is how a continuous noise process emerges from repeated short interactions.

5. Worked problem §

A system qubit controls a rotation of an ancilla in \(\ket0\): \(\ket0\ket0\mapsto\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket1\ket0\mapsto\ket1(\cos\frac\theta2\ket0+\sin\frac\theta2\ket1)\). (a) Find the operators \(K_j=\bra jU\ket0\) and check completeness. (b) Find the channel’s effect on a general \(\rho\). (c) Which phase-flip probability gives the same channel?

Hint 1

\(K_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,\cos\frac\theta2)\) and \(K_1=\mathrm{diag}(0,\sin\frac\theta2)\).

Hint 2

Compare the coherence factor with \(1-2p\).

Worked solution

(a) \(K_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,\cos\frac\theta2)\) and \(K_1=\mathrm{diag}(0,\sin\frac\theta2)\), with \(K_0^\dagger K_0+K_1^\dagger K_1=\mathrm{diag}(1,\cos^2+\sin^2)=I\). (b) The populations are unchanged and the coherences are multiplied by \(\cos\frac\theta2\). (c) This matches phase flip with \(1-2p=\cos\frac\theta2\). At \(\theta=0\) the ancilla learns nothing and the channel is the identity; at \(\theta=\pi\) it is CNOT and the channel completely dephases. A partial record causes partial loss of coherence, in proportion to how well the ancilla distinguishes the two branches.

6. Worked problem §

Model a reset to \(\ket0\) as the channel with \(K_0=\ket0\bra0\) and \(K_1=\ket0\bra1\). (a) Check completeness. (b) Show that every input density matrix is mapped to \(\ket0\bra0\). (c) Give an ancilla construction that realizes it, and explain where the input’s information goes.

Hint 1

\(K_1^\dagger K_1=\ket1\bra1\).

Hint 2

\(K_0\rho K_0^\dagger+K_1\rho K_1^\dagger=(\rho_{00}+\rho_{11})\ket0\bra0\).

Worked solution

(a) \(K_0^\dagger K_0+K_1^\dagger K_1=\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1=I\). (b) \(K_0\rho K_0^\dagger=\rho_{00}\ket0\bra0\) and \(K_1\rho K_1^\dagger=\rho_{11}\ket0\bra0\), so the output is \(\mathrm{tr}(\rho)\ket0\bra0=\ket0\bra0\). (c) Swap the system with an ancilla in \(\ket0\) and discard the ancilla. The joint swap is unitary; the input state now sits in the ancilla, so nothing is destroyed at the joint level, but it is no longer accessible on the retained system. This is amplitude damping at \(\gamma=1\).

7. Worked problem §

Do the bit-flip channel (probability \(p\)) and amplitude damping (\(\gamma\)) commute? Compute the population of \(\ket1\) after applying them in each order to \(\ket1\bra1\), and interpret the difference.

Hint 1

Bit flip then damping: \(\ket1\) survives the flip with \(1-p\), then decays with \(\gamma\).

Hint 2

Damping then bit flip: a decayed qubit can be flipped back to \(\ket1\).

Worked solution

Bit flip first: population of \(\ket1\) becomes \(1-p\), then damping multiplies it by \(1-\gamma\): result \((1-p)(1-\gamma)\). Damping first: population \(1-\gamma\); the flip then gives \((1-\gamma)(1-p)+\gamma p\), since a decayed qubit can be flipped back up. The orders differ by \(p\gamma\), so the channels do not commute. Noise models must state the order of processes; in contrast, phase flip and amplitude damping do commute, because \(Z\) commutes with both damping operators up to a sign that cancels in \(K\rho K^\dagger\).

8. Misconception §

A student proposes undoing a channel by applying the inverse of each of its operators \(K_j\). Explain why this fails for complete dephasing and for amplitude damping, and state what extra information would allow recovery in the dephasing case.

Hint 1

Is \(\mathrm{diag}(1,0)\) invertible?

Hint 2

The user does not know which branch occurred.

Worked solution

Complete dephasing has \(K_0=\mathrm{diag}(1,0)\) and \(K_1=\mathrm{diag}(0,1)\), neither invertible: each branch destroys a component. Amplitude damping’s \(K_1=\sqrt\gamma\ket0\bra1\) is also not invertible, and its \(K_0\) is invertible but applies only on one unknown branch. More fundamentally, the output is a sum over branches the user cannot identify, and different inputs (\(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) under dephasing) give the same output, so no operation on the retained system can map that output back to both. If the ancilla is kept, the joint unitary can simply be reversed; if only the ancilla’s record is learned, for dephasing it tells which branch occurred but the coherence is still lost. Recovery needs access to the system that holds the record.

9. Extension §

Compute the partial transpose of the Bell density matrix: in \(\rho_{AB}=\frac12(\ket{00}\bra{00}+\ket{00}\bra{11}+\ket{11}\bra{00}+\ket{11}\bra{11})\), transpose Bob’s labels, so that \(\ket{ab}\bra{a'b'}\mapsto\ket{ab'}\bra{a'b}\). Find the eigenvalues of the result and an eigenvector for the negative one. Why does this show that transposition is not a physical channel?

Hint 1

\(\ket{00}\bra{11}\mapsto\ket{01}\bra{10}\).

Hint 2

Try \((\ket{01}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

The partial transpose is \(\frac12(\ket{00}\bra{00}+\ket{01}\bra{10}+\ket{10}\bra{01}+\ket{11}\bra{11})\). In the order \(00,01,10,11\) it is \(\frac12\) times the swap matrix, with eigenvalues \(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\) (on \(\ket{00}\), \(\ket{11}\), and \((\ket{01}+\ket{10})/\sqrt2\)) and \(-\frac12\) on \((\ket{01}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2\). A test along that eigenvector would have probability \(-\frac12\). Transposition maps every single-qubit density matrix to a valid one, but applied to half of an entangled state it does not. Physical operations must remain valid when acting on part of a larger system: complete positivity.

10. Extension §

Alice sends Bob either \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\) with equal probability through a phase-flip channel with probability \(p\). (a) Find the output states and Bob’s best success probability using a \(\pm\) test. (b) Explain why no further processing by Bob can increase it. (c) What happens if Alice uses \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) instead?

Hint 1

Phase flip turns \(\ket+\) into \(\ket-\) with probability \(p\), so \(\ket+\) becomes the mixture \((1-p)\ket+\bra++p\ket-\bra-\).

Hint 2

Any later processing could have been included in Bob’s original measurement.

Worked solution

(a) \(\ket+\) becomes \((1-p)\ket+\bra++p\ket-\bra-\) and \(\ket-\) becomes \(p\ket+\bra++(1-p)\ket-\bra-\). A \(\pm\) test, guessing the matching input, succeeds with probability \(\max(1-p,p)\): \(1\) at \(p=0\), \(\frac12\) at \(p=\frac12\), and \(1\) again at \(p=1\), where every flip is known. (b) Any channel Bob applies before measuring, followed by his measurement, is itself a measurement on the channel output, so it cannot beat the best measurement on that output: processing cannot create distinguishability. (c) Phase flips leave \(\ket0\bra0\) and \(\ket1\bra1\) unchanged, so \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) remain perfectly distinguishable. Choosing an encoding matched to the noise is the first step toward error correction.
