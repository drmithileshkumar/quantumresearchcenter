Quantum Measurement

What can a measuring procedure reveal, and what does it leave behind?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 3–6; measurement effects and state updates are derived from circuits.

Anchor example: An ancilla-assisted measurement of a qubit.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 7.1 Measurement Procedures
  2. 7.2 General Measurements
  3. 7.3 Information and Disturbance

7.1 Measurement Procedures

A detector does two jobs: it produces an outcome and changes the system. Identical outcome frequencies can conceal different disturbances. We will describe those two jobs separately.

7.1.1 Coupling to an auxiliary system

Couple a system to a zero auxiliary with U and measure the auxiliary in basis j. The branch operator on the system is \(M_j=\bra jU\ket0\). For input ρ, the outcome probability is \(p_j=\mathrm{tr}(M_j\rho M_j^\dagger)\), and the conditional state is \(M_j\rho M_j^\dagger/p_j\). This construction is the channel picture of Chapter 6 with the environmental record now retained.

7.1.2 Outcomes and conditional operations

For a CNOT with the system as control and the zero auxiliary as target, the branch operators are \(M_0=\ket0\bra0\) and \(M_1=\ket1\bra1\). Measuring the auxiliary therefore measures the system’s computational-basis value. If the input is plus, each result has probability one half and leaves the corresponding basis state. The circuit explains the record and the loss of visible interference together.

Problem 7.1. For the noisy effect E0 above, what is p0 on plus?

Worked solution

It is the average of the two diagonal entries, 1/2. The value alone does not reveal the conditional state.

7.2 General Measurements

7.2.1 Measurement effects and probabilities

Define the effect \(E_j=M_j^\dagger M_j\). Then \(p_j=\mathrm{tr}(E_j\rho)\), every effect is positive, and \(\sum_jE_j=I\). A collection with these properties is a positive-operator-valued measure, or POVM. Effects need not be orthogonal projectors and need not have eigenvalues only zero and one. They describe outcome statistics, not the complete physical aftermath.

7.2.2 POVMs and physical implementations

For example \(E_0=\mathrm{diag}(3/4,1/4)\) and \(E_1=I-E_0\) give a noisy indication of the Z value. One implementation has \(M_j=\sqrt{E_j}\), where the square root takes square roots of the nonnegative diagonal entries. More generally the map \(\ket\psi\mapsto\sum_j\ket j M_j\ket\psi\) preserves inner products when the completeness equation holds; extending that map to a larger unitary realizes the measurement with an auxiliary. This establishes physical realizability without requiring the effects themselves to be projectors.

Problem 7.2. Verify the square-root implementation’s completeness.

Worked solution

M0†M0+M1†M1=E0+E1=I, so total outcome probability is one for every input.

7.3 Information and Disturbance

7.3.1 Outcome statistics versus state updates

After any outcome, an outcome-dependent unitary Vj changes the branch operator to VjMj but leaves Ej unchanged. For a sharp Z measurement, compare the ordinary update with an implementation that flips the system after outcome one. The first leaves the observed basis state; the second always leaves zero. Both have exactly the same initial outcome probabilities. The full collection of branch maps is called an instrument.

7.3.2 Sequential measurement and retained records

A subsequent measurement can distinguish those instruments. On input one, the ordinary Z instrument followed by another Z measurement returns one twice; the reset-after-readout instrument returns one then zero. Recording, ignoring, and conditioning on an outcome are different operations. A weakly informative measurement can preserve some coherence, but “weak” does not mean nondisturbing for every input or every accumulated sequence.

Problem 7.3. Why is a POVM insufficient to predict sequential measurements?

Worked solution

It specifies each first outcome probability, but not the conditional output state. The next measurement depends on the instrument, including any outcome-dependent transformations.

We can now optimize measurements for a concrete information task: guessing which of two known states was prepared.
