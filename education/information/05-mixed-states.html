Mixed States

How do we predict measurements when no single pure-state vector describes the preparation?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 2–4; density operators are constructed from averaged measurement probabilities.

Anchor example: Compare a random choice of basis states with one half of a Bell pair.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 5.1 Uncertain Preparations
  2. 5.2 Subsystems
  3. 5.3 Equivalent Descriptions

5.1 Uncertain Preparations

A fair coin chooses whether to prepare zero or one. A different apparatus supplies one half of a Bell pair. These procedures have different joint histories, yet every local measurement has the same statistics. We need a description based on those predictions.

5.1.1 Pure states and ensembles

A mixture selects \(\ket{\psi_j}\) with probability p_j, so a projection onto φ has probability \(\sum_jp_j|\braket\phi{\psi_j}|^2\). This is an average of probabilities, not the squared magnitude of a sum of amplitudes. In particular a fair mixture of zero and one differs from plus: the mixture gives a fair X readout, while plus gives a certain one.

5.1.2 Constructing density operators

Define \(\rho=\sum_jp_j\ket{\psi_j}\bra{\psi_j}\). The outer product is a matrix; it keeps exactly the coefficients needed for the averaged prediction \(\bra\phi\rho\ket\phi\). For a general measurement operator E, the probability is tr(Eρ), where trace means the sum of diagonal entries. A density operator is positive, meaning \(\bra v\rho\ket v\geq0\), and has trace one. The fair basis mixture is diag(1/2,1/2)=I/2.

Problem 5.1. Compute the density matrix of plus.

Worked solution

It is \(\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\). The off-diagonal entries distinguish it from I/2.

5.2 Subsystems

5.2.1 Reduced states and partial traces

For a joint state, sum over the unobserved subsystem index: \((\rho_A)_{a,a^{\prime}}=\sum_b(\rho_{AB})_{ab,a^{\prime}b}\). This partial trace is designed so every Alice-only measurement has the same prediction from ρA as from the full state. Applying it to Φ+ removes the off-diagonal joint terms because their Bob labels differ, leaving I/2.

5.2.2 Entanglement and local uncertainty

The Bell pair is globally pure but locally mixed. Local uncertainty therefore need not mean that a hidden local pure state was selected from a preferred ensemble. Alice’s reduced matrix is enough for Alice-only predictions, but it cannot predict correlations with Bob without the joint matrix. The global description carries information not contained in the separate reduced states.

Problem 5.2. Why does tracing out Bob kill |00〉〈11|?

Worked solution

The partial trace sums terms with the same Bob index on the ket and bra. Here those indices are zero and one, whose overlap is zero.

5.3 Equivalent Descriptions

5.3.1 Different ensembles for the same state

The same matrix has many decompositions: \(I/2=(\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1)/2=(\ket+\bra++\ket-\bra-)/2\). Expand the latter sum: off-diagonal entries cancel. No measurement on the system alone can reveal which of these ensemble procedures was used. An external preparation record can distinguish them, but that is extra accessible information.

5.3.2 Purification and inaccessible records

An ensemble can be represented by a pure joint state \(\ket\Psi=\sum_j\sqrt{p_j}\ket{\psi_j}\ket j_E\), where the environment labels j are orthogonal. Discarding E gives ρ because cross terms have zero environment overlap. This is a purification. It is a mathematical representation of the same local predictions, not a claim that the observer can control the purifying system. For diagonal probabilities 3/4 and 1/4, a purification is \(\sqrt3\ket{00}/2+\ket{11}/2\).

Problem 5.3. Can the density matrix identify a unique preparation ensemble?

Worked solution

No. Different ensembles can give identical matrices and hence identical predictions for every measurement on the system alone.
