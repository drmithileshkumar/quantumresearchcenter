Before you begin

Starting point: Chapter 1; probability and logarithms are developed through coding examples.

Keep in view: A biased binary source and a binary channel that sometimes flips a bit.

Predict first. Can a source of biased bits be compressed just by deleting its frequent zeros?

Compare your reasoning

No. Their positions are part of the message. An encoder must retain enough information to reconstruct the entire string, using a specified code.

2.1 Probability and Correlation

A source sends many zeros and a few ones. Can a long message use fewer bits without losing its meaning? The answer depends on the distribution of messages, the allowed errors, and how long a block we encode.

2.1.1 Joint and conditional probabilities§

For two random variables X,Y, \(p(x,y)\) is the probability of a joint event. Summing over y gives \(p(x)\). When p(x)>0, define \(p(y|x)=p(x,y)/p(x)\); equivalently joint probability is prior times conditional probability. A binary flip channel with error e has \(p(y=x|x)=1-e\) and \(p(y\ne x|x)=e\). These equations describe a distribution, not an individual deterministic event.

Take a fair input bit through a channel that flips it with probability \(e=1/4\). The four joint probabilities are \(p(0,0)=p(1,1)=3/8\) and \(p(0,1)=p(1,0)=1/8\). Adding across either input or output gives one half. Both ends look like fair coins separately, yet they agree on three quarters of trials. Their separate distributions do not determine their joint behavior.

The notation \(X\) names the uncertain input value, while \(x\) names a particular value it might take. A sum such as \(\sum_x p(x,y)\) means add the contributions for each allowed input, here \(x=0\) and \(x=1\). We use this same summing procedure later when a quantum subsystem is not observed.

2.1.2 Independent and correlated messages§

Independence means p(x,y)=p(x)p(y). A pair that is 00 or 11 with equal probability has fair individual bits but correlated joint values; observing one removes uncertainty about the other. Independent source uses are a substantive assumption. Repeated signals can have memory, and coding theorems for independent repetitions cannot be transferred to them without analysis.

Problems

Problem 2.1. For the fair-input flip channel with e=1/4, what is p(X=0 | Y=0)?

Worked solution

The joint probability is 3/8 and the output-zero probability is 1/2. Dividing gives 3/4. A fair output does not mean the input and output are independent.

2.2 Entropy and Compression

2.2.1 Information content and average length§

Among N equally likely messages, identifying one requires log₂N binary choices in the ideal counting sense. The logarithm is the inverse of exponentiation: log₂8=3. For a particular event of probability p, the information content −log₂p is additive for independent events because their probabilities multiply. Averaging defines \(H(X)=-\sum_xp(x)\log_2p(x)\), with a zero-probability term assigned zero by continuity. For probabilities 1/2,1/4,1/8,1/8, the prefix code 0,10,110,111 has average length 7/4 bits, equal to H. No codeword prefixes another, so concatenated messages can be decoded.

2.2.2 Repeated sources and compression limits§

For an independent binary source with one-probability 1/4, the entropy is h₂(1/4)≈0.811 bits per signal. A long typical string contains about a quarter ones. The number of high-probability strings grows roughly as 2^(nH), so labels of about nH bits can describe them while atypical strings get a separate treatment. Shannon’s noiseless coding theorem makes this asymptotic statement precise. It does not say that one biased bit can always be stored losslessly in 0.811 of a physical bit.

Check the prefix-code example directly. The contributions to average length are \((1/2)1\), \((1/4)2\), \((1/8)3\), and \((1/8)3\), giving \(7/4\). The corresponding information contents are 1, 2, 3, and 3 bits, so their weighted average is identical. This equality works because these probabilities are powers of one half; it is not a promise that every one-symbol source has a code attaining entropy exactly.

At the receiver, the first 0 completes a word. A first 1 requires a second bit; 10 completes the second word, while 11 requires a third. Decoding can proceed without knowing where Alice put spaces. That is the practical work done by the prefix condition.

Write \(h_2(q)=-q\log_2q-(1-q)\log_2(1-q)\) for binary entropy. At \(q=1/4\), the terms are \(1/2\) and approximately \(0.3113\), giving \(0.8113\). This notation will recur for noisy bits, mixed qubits, and entangled pairs; the task determines which probability \(q\) describes.

Problems

Problem 2.2. Can 0,01,1 be used as a prefix code?

Worked solution

No. The first word prefixes the second. A concatenation such as 01 can be ambiguous without extra framing.

2.3 Noisy Communication

2.3.1 Channels and decoding§

Sending 0 as 000 and 1 as 111 lets majority decoding correct one flipped bit. For independent flip probability e, failure means two or three flips, with probability \(3e^2(1-e)+e^3=3e^2-2e^3\). At e=0.1 this is 0.028, below 0.1, but three channel uses now transmit one information bit. Redundancy trades rate for reliability.

2.3.2 Reliable transmission and rate§

A communication rate is information bits divided by channel uses. Reliability means the block error tends to zero along a sequence of longer codes at that rate. For the independent binary symmetric channel, capacity is 1−h₂(e) bits per use. At e=1/2 it is zero because the output is independent of the input. The general coding theorem and converse require a coding argument beyond the repetition example; the example illustrates error reduction but does not attain every rate below capacity.

Why do three uses help? There are three distinct ways to flip exactly two positions: 110, 101, and 011. Each has probability \(e^2(1-e)\) because the flips are independent. Flipping all three has probability \(e^3\). The events are disjoint, so their probabilities add. The decoder fails on exactly these four events. This reasoning explains both the coefficient three and the independence assumption in the formula.

Information shared by two variables is quantified by \(I(X:Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)\), where \(H(X\mid Y)=\sum_y p(y)H(X\mid Y=y)\) is the average uncertainty left after reading the output. For a fair input through our flip channel, each output leaves error probability \(e\), so \(I(X:Y)=1-h_2(e)\). This calculation evaluates the chosen input distribution. The channel coding theorem supplies the additional assertion that suitable long codes attain rates approaching the capacity.

Classical probabilities add across mutually exclusive alternatives. Interference requires a different kind of quantity before probabilities are formed.

Sources and further study: Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication.

Problems

Problem 2.3. At e=0, what rate does the three-bit repetition code achieve?

Worked solution

Only 1/3 bit per channel use, despite a channel capacity of one. Being reliable does not make a code rate-optimal.

Transfer problem. A source emits A,B,C,D with probabilities 1/2,1/4,1/8,1/8. Compare a fixed two-bit encoding with the prefix code in the text over 1,000 independent symbols.

Worked solution

The fixed code uses 2,000 bits. The prefix code has expected length 1,750 bits, saving 250 on average. The actual length fluctuates; prefix decoding is exact for every source sequence, rather than merely for typical ones.

1. Quick check §

Compute the entropy \(H(X)\) in bits for each distribution: (a) \((\frac12,\frac12)\); (b) \((1,0)\); (c) eight equally likely values; (d) \((\frac12,\frac14,\frac14)\). Which distribution is most uncertain per symbol?

Hint 1

\(H=-\sum p\log_2p\), with \(0\log0=0\).

Hint 2

\(-\log_2\frac14=2\).

Worked solution

(a) \(1\) bit. (b) \(0\): the value is certain. (c) \(\log_28=3\) bits. (d) \(\frac12\cdot1+\frac14\cdot2+\frac14\cdot2=1.5\) bits. The eight-value uniform distribution is the most uncertain. Entropy grows with the number of possibilities and is largest when they are equally likely; a prefix code such as \(0,10,11\) attains \(1.5\) bits on average for distribution (d).

2. Quick check §

Decide whether \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent: (a) \(p(00)=0.4\), \(p(01)=0.1\), \(p(10)=0.4\), \(p(11)=0.1\); (b) \(p(00)=p(11)=0.45\), \(p(01)=p(10)=0.05\).

Hint 1

Find the marginals by summing rows and columns.

Hint 2

Independence requires \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\) for every pair.

Worked solution

(a) \(p(X=0)=p(X=1)=0.5\) and \(p(Y=0)=0.8\), \(p(Y=1)=0.2\). Every joint probability equals the product, for example \(0.5\times0.8=0.4\), so they are independent. (b) Both marginals are \((0.5,0.5)\), but \(p(00)=0.45\neq0.25\), so they are correlated: the bits agree \(90\%\) of the time. Fair marginals say nothing about whether two bits are correlated; only the joint distribution does.

3. Quick check §

Find the capacity \(1-h_2(e)\) of the binary symmetric channel for \(e=0.1\), \(e=0.5\), and \(e=0.9\). Why does the channel with \(e=0.9\) have the same capacity as the one with \(e=0.1\)?

Hint 1

\(h_2(0.1)\approx0.469\).

Hint 2

\(h_2(e)=h_2(1-e)\).

Worked solution

For \(e=0.1\), capacity is \(1-0.469\approx0.531\) bits per use. For \(e=0.5\) it is \(0\): the output is independent of the input. For \(e=0.9\) it is again \(0.531\), because \(h_2(0.9)=h_2(0.1)\). A channel that flips \(90\%\) of bits is a reliable channel followed by a known NOT, which the receiver can undo. What limits communication is uncertainty about whether a flip occurred, not the flips themselves.

4. Worked problem §

A source emits A, B, C, D with probabilities \(0.4,0.3,0.2,0.1\). (a) Check that the code A\(=0\), B\(=10\), C\(=110\), D\(=111\) is a prefix code and compute its average length. (b) Compute the entropy. (c) Decode the string 1100111010.

Hint 1

\(-\log_20.4\approx1.322\), \(-\log_20.3\approx1.737\), \(-\log_20.2\approx2.322\), \(-\log_20.1\approx3.322\).

Hint 2

Read bits from the left, stopping as soon as a codeword is complete.

Worked solution

(a) No codeword begins another codeword, so it is a prefix code. The average length is \(0.4\cdot1+0.3\cdot2+0.2\cdot3+0.1\cdot3=1.9\) bits. (b) \(H\approx0.4(1.322)+0.3(1.737)+0.2(2.322)+0.1(3.322)\approx1.846\) bits. The code is about \(0.05\) bits per symbol above the entropy; since these probabilities are not powers of one half, no one-symbol code reaches \(H\) exactly, and coding blocks of symbols can close the gap. (c) \(110\,|\,0\,|\,111\,|\,0\,|\,10\) gives C A D A B.

5. Worked problem §

Extend the repetition code to five uses: send \(00000\) or \(11111\) and decode by majority. For independent flips with \(e=0.1\), compute the failure probability and the rate. Compare with the three-use code and with the capacity \(0.531\). What does the comparison suggest about repetition codes?

Hint 1

Failure means three or more flips among five.

Hint 2

\(\binom53=10\), \(\binom54=5\).

Worked solution

Failure requires at least three flips: \(10e^3(1-e)^2+5e^4(1-e)+e^5=0.0081+0.00045+0.00001\approx0.0086\). The rate is \(1/5\). The three-use code fails with probability \(0.028\) at rate \(1/3\). Each extra pair of repetitions lowers the error but also the rate, and driving the error to zero this way drives the rate to zero. The capacity \(0.531\) says that much better codes exist: for any rate below \(0.531\), long block codes make the error as small as desired at a fixed rate. Repetition illustrates redundancy, not efficient coding.

6. Worked problem §

For a fair input bit through the flip channel with \(e=1/4\), the joint probabilities are \(3/8,1/8,1/8,3/8\). (a) Find \(p(x\mid y)\) for each output. (b) Compute \(H(X\mid Y)\) and \(I(X:Y)\). (c) Compare with a channel that copies the bit perfectly and with one whose output is a fresh fair coin.

Hint 1

Given \(Y=0\), the input is \(0\) with probability \(3/4\).

Hint 2

\(h_2(1/4)\approx0.811\).

Worked solution

(a) Given \(Y=0\), \(p(X=0\mid Y=0)=(3/8)/(1/2)=3/4\); given \(Y=1\), \(X=1\) with probability \(3/4\). (b) Each output leaves uncertainty \(h_2(1/4)\approx0.811\), so \(H(X\mid Y)\approx0.811\) and \(I(X:Y)\approx1-0.811=0.189\) bits. (c) A perfect copy gives \(H(X\mid Y)=0\) and \(I=1\) bit; an independent coin gives \(H(X\mid Y)=1\) and \(I=0\). Although Bob guesses correctly \(75\%\) of the time with this channel, it conveys less than a fifth of a bit per use, because the remaining uncertainty is large.

7. Worked problem §

A source emits \(n=100\) independent bits, each \(1\) with probability \(\frac14\). (a) Show that every string with exactly \(25\) ones has probability \(2^{-nh_2(1/4)}\approx2^{-81.1}\). (b) There are \(\binom{100}{25}\approx2^{77.7}\) such strings: what is their total probability? (c) Strings with between \(20\) and \(30\) ones number about \(2^{85.4}\) and carry probability about \(0.80\). What does this say about compression at \(n=100\) compared with the asymptotic \(81.1\) bits?

Hint 1

\((\frac14)^{25}(\frac34)^{75}=2^{25\log_2(1/4)+75\log_2(3/4)}\).

Hint 2

Total probability is the count times the probability of each string.

Worked solution

(a) \(\log_2\big[(\frac14)^{25}(\frac34)^{75}\big]=25(-2)+75(-0.415)\approx-81.1=-100\,h_2(\frac14)\). (b) \(2^{77.7}\times2^{-81.1}=2^{-3.4}\approx0.09\): the single most likely composition holds only about \(9\%\) of the probability. (c) Labelling the \(2^{85.4}\) strings with \(20\) to \(30\) ones takes about \(86\) bits and covers \(80\%\) of outputs, against \(100\) bits uncompressed. Covering more probability needs a wider window and more bits. Only as \(n\) grows does the needed label length per bit approach \(h_2(\frac14)\approx0.811\) with vanishing error; at \(n=100\) the typical set is still blurred.

8. Misconception §

A student claims: “A good compressor shortens every file.” Use counting to show that no lossless code maps every \(n\)-bit string to a strictly shorter string. How, then, do compressors work?

Hint 1

How many bit strings have length less than \(n\), including the empty string?

Hint 2

Lossless means different inputs get different outputs.

Worked solution

There are \(2^n\) strings of length \(n\), but only \(1+2+4+\cdots+2^{n-1}=2^n-1\) strings shorter than \(n\). A lossless code must give different outputs to different inputs, so it cannot fit \(2^n\) inputs into \(2^n-1\) shorter outputs: at least one string is not shortened. Compressors work by giving short codewords to likely strings and longer ones to unlikely strings, so the average length drops for a particular source. Entropy measures what is achievable on average for that source; compression is a bet on the distribution, not a shrinking of every message.

9. Extension §

A prefix code with codeword lengths \(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_m\) must satisfy the Kraft inequality \(\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}\le1\). (a) Explain it by imagining each codeword as reserving all infinite bit strings that begin with it. (b) Check it for \(0,10,110,111\). (c) Show that no prefix code has lengths \(1,1,2\), which explains why \(0,01,1\) fails.

Hint 1

A codeword of length \(\ell\) begins a fraction \(2^{-\ell}\) of all long strings.

Hint 2

The prefix condition means the reserved sets do not overlap.

Worked solution

(a) A codeword of length \(\ell\) is the beginning of a fraction \(2^{-\ell}\) of all long bit strings. Because no codeword begins another, these fractions belong to disjoint sets of strings, so they add to at most \(1\). (b) \(\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\frac18=1\): the code uses all available space, which is why its average length can equal the entropy for the matching dyadic distribution. (c) Lengths \(1,1,2\) give \(\frac12+\frac12+\frac14>1\), so no prefix code has them. In \(0,01,1\), the codeword \(0\) begins \(01\), and the string \(01\) is ambiguous: A followed by C, or B.

10. Extension §

For two random variables, \(H(X,Y)=-\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2p(x,y)\). (a) Show that \(H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y)\) when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent. (b) Compute \(H(X,Y)\), \(H(X)\), and \(H(Y)\) for the fair correlated pair that is \(00\) or \(11\) with equal probability, and for the flip channel with \(e=1/4\). (c) Interpret \(H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)\).

Hint 1

For independent variables, \(\log p(x,y)=\log p(x)+\log p(y)\).

Hint 2

For the flip channel, the four joint probabilities are \(3/8,1/8,1/8,3/8\).

Worked solution

(a) If \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\), then \(-\sum p(x,y)[\log_2p(x)+\log_2p(y)]=H(X)+H(Y)\), using \(\sum_yp(x,y)=p(x)\). (b) Correlated pair: \(H(X,Y)=1\) bit, while \(H(X)=H(Y)=1\). Flip channel: \(H(X,Y)=2\cdot\frac38\log_2\frac83+2\cdot\frac18\cdot3\approx1.061+0.75=1.811\), with \(H(X)=H(Y)=1\). (c) The difference is \(1\) bit for the correlated pair and about \(0.189\) bits for the channel: exactly \(I(X:Y)\). Correlation makes the pair less uncertain than its parts taken separately, and mutual information measures the overlap.
