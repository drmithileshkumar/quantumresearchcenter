At what rates can noisy channels transmit classical or quantum information reliably?

Before you begin

Starting point: Chapters 6, 10–14; capacity theorems are introductory and their asymptotic assumptions are stated.

Keep in view: Compare tasks performed using repeated uses of a noisy qubit channel.

Predict first. If a quarter of qubits are flagged as erased, is the unassisted quantum capacity three quarters?

Compare your reasoning

No. The unassisted qubit erasure capacity is one half in that case. Preserving unknown quantum correlations is a stronger task than recovering classical labels.

15.1 Communication Tasks

A channel sometimes transmits a qubit perfectly and sometimes replaces it with an orthogonal erasure flag. The receiver knows which uses were erased. This simple channel separates classical transmission, quantum transmission, and assisted transmission.

15.1.1 Classical and quantum messages§

Classical transmission asks the receiver to identify a message label. Quantum transmission asks an unknown state, potentially entangled with a reference, to survive the encoding, channel, and decoding. A device that measures and reprepares basis labels may be a good classical channel while destroying quantum coherence. The reliability criterion must match the requested kind of information.

Write the qubit erasure channel as \(\mathcal N_e(\rho)=(1-e)\rho\oplus e\ket{\perp}\bra{\perp}\). The direct-sum symbol means that the flag occupies a third output direction, perpendicular to both qubit basis labels. It is not a coherent superposition of a transmitted qubit and a flag: the two blocks carry classical probabilities.

For \(\rho=\ket+\bra+\) and \(e=1/4\), three quarters of uses retain the plus state exactly and one quarter return the flag. Reading only “erased or retained” need not disturb the retained qubit, because the flag is orthogonal to the whole qubit subspace. This side information locates failures, but does not supply the lost amplitudes.

15.1.2 Assistance and resource models§

Specify available assistance: pre-shared entanglement, one-way or two-way classical communication, and any other side channels. Teleportation shows that an ebit plus two classical bits can transmit a qubit, while dense coding changes classical capacity with entanglement assistance. These are different resource models, so their rates cannot be compared without their resource accounts.

Problems

Problem 15.1. Why does a known erasure differ from an unflagged bit flip?

Worked solution

The receiver knows the failed positions and can adapt decoding to that side information. The channel output alphabet includes a flag distinct from the qubit subspace.

15.2 Coding and Rates

15.2.1 Achievability and converse statements§

A rate R is achievable if a sequence of n-use codes transmits about nR bits or qubits with error tending to zero. A converse excludes rates above the capacity under the same criterion. A finite demonstration with a small error does not establish either asymptotic claim. We assume finite-dimensional memoryless channels, meaning independent uses of the same channel map, unless stated otherwise.

15.2.2 Classical and entanglement-assisted capacities§

For unassisted classical communication, define \(\chi^*(\mathcal N)\) as the maximum output Holevo information over input ensembles. General classical capacity is the regularized quantity \(C(\mathcal N)=\sup_n\chi^*(\mathcal N^{\otimes n})/n\), allowing entangled encodings and collective decoding. With unlimited prior entanglement, the classical capacity is the single-use maximum of \(I(R:B)\) after sending one half of a purification through the channel. These coding theorems are stated here, not derived by the examples; single-use simplification without assistance requires additional structure.

Choose two orthogonal classical inputs with equal priors for this channel. The output tells the bit with probability \(1-e\); on erasure, the bit remains fair. Hence \(H(X\mid Y)=e\) and \(I(X:Y)=1-e\) bits per use. This constructs the familiar information expression; achieving it reliably with long codes and excluding larger rates require the capacity theorem.

A block code chooses an input for an entire message before transmission. Its rate counts message bits divided by all channel uses, including erased positions. Counting only the successful positions after the fact would report a different resource cost. Quantum transmission also needs coherence with a reference, so the fraction of visibly successful uses alone does not establish its capacity.

Problems

Problem 15.2. For a fair classical bit through an erasure channel with e=1/2, compute the mutual information.

Worked solution

The input entropy is one bit. On half the trials the output reveals it completely; on the other half it reveals only an erasure flag, leaving one bit of uncertainty. Thus H(X|Y)=1/2 and I(X:Y)=1/2 bit per use.

15.3 Quantum Transmission

15.3.1 Coherent information and quantum capacity§

For input ρ purified by RA and output state ωRB, coherent information is \(I_c(\rho,\mathcal N)=S(B)_\omega-S(RB)_\omega\). Unassisted quantum capacity is \(Q(\mathcal N)=\sup_n n^{-1}\max_{\rho_n} I_c(\rho_n,\mathcal N^{\otimes n})\). The optimization includes correlations across inputs; one use need not attain the capacity. This is an asymptotic coding theorem. A large positive value for a particular input is useful evidence of an achievable rate, but a general converse needs the full theorem.

15.3.2 Regularization and finite-resource limitations§

For the qubit erasure channel with erasure probability e, the exact capacities are C=1−e classical bits, entanglement-assisted classical capacity 2(1−e) bits, and unassisted Q=max(0,1−2e) qubits per use. These are established channel-specific theorems. With a maximally entangled reference, \(S(B)=h_2(e)+(1-e)\) and \(S(RB)=h_2(e)+e\), so coherent information is 1−2e. At e=1/4, these three capacities are 3/4,3/2,1/2. Two-way classical assistance changes the quantum rate; do not silently use it in the unassisted claim. Finite block length, decoding effort, and experimental noise remain practical constraints.

The erasure-channel entropy formulas follow by separating its two orthogonal branches. Send one half of a Bell pair, keeping reference R. On successful transmission, RB is a pure Bell pair. On erasure, R is maximally mixed while B is a definite flag. Thus \(S(RB)=h_2(e)+e\): the first term records which branch occurred, and the second is the average entropy within the erased branch. Similarly \(S(B)=h_2(e)+(1-e)\), because the retained output is locally maximally mixed.

Subtracting cancels the classical flag entropy and gives \(I_c=1-2e\). At \(e=3/4\) this input has negative coherent information, but a capacity cannot be negative: sending no quantum information always achieves rate zero. The channel-specific capacity theorem yields \(\max(0,1-2e)\), not a negative rate or permission to replace a general regularized formula by this special example.

Communication rates describe reliability. The final chapter asks when transmitted data can also be secret from an adversary.

Sources and further study: Holevo, Capacity with general signal states; Bennett et al., Entanglement-assisted capacity; Devetak, Private classical and quantum capacity; Bennett et al., Erasure channel capacities.

Problems

Problem 15.3. Why is a single-use coherent information not generally “the quantum capacity”?

Worked solution

Block encodings can use correlations across channel inputs and improve the per-use value. The regularized maximization is essential without a theorem proving additivity for the channel.

Transfer problem. Compute the three stated erasure capacities at e=3/4. Why does the positive entanglement-assisted classical rate not contradict zero unassisted quantum capacity?

Worked solution

The classical capacity is 1/4 bit per use, entanglement-assisted classical capacity is 1/2 bit per use, and unassisted quantum capacity is zero. The positive rate concerns classical labels with prior shared entanglement; the zero rate concerns unknown quantum states without that resource. Teleportation would consume additional ebits and classical communication.

Questions and worked problems

These exercises draw on the whole chapter. Attempt each question before opening a hint, explain your reasoning, then compare it with the worked solution.

1. Quick check §

Using \(C=1-e\), \(C_E=2(1-e)\), and \(Q=\max(0,1-2e)\) for the qubit erasure channel, evaluate all three at \(e=0.1\) and \(e=\frac12\). What is special about \(e=\frac12\)?

Hint 1

Substitute directly.

Hint 2

Compare \(Q\) with \(C\) at each value.

Worked solution

At \(e=0.1\): \(C=0.9\) bits, \(C_E=1.8\) bits, \(Q=0.8\) qubits per use. At \(e=\frac12\): \(C=0.5\), \(C_E=1\), \(Q=0\). At \(e=\frac12\) the channel can still carry classical information, and even more with shared entanglement, yet it cannot carry quantum information at any positive rate without assistance. Quantum transmission is the most demanding of the three tasks.

2. Quick check §

Which kind of transmission does each task require? (a) Sending the result of a measurement; (b) sending half of a Bell pair so that Alice and Bob share entanglement; (c) transferring an unknown qubit using a pre-shared Bell pair. Name the resources each consumes.

Hint 1

Ask whether the receiver must identify a label or preserve coherence with a reference.

Hint 2

Teleportation needs classical messages and an ebit.

Worked solution

(a) Classical: the receiver must identify a label, so a measure-and-resend channel would suffice. (b) Quantum: the transmitted qubit must stay coherent with Alice’s half, so the channel must preserve entanglement, which is exactly what quantum capacity measures. (c) Classical, with entanglement assistance: two classical bits per qubit and one ebit, with no quantum channel used during the protocol. The same physical outcome, a qubit at Bob, can be achieved with very different resource accounts.

3. Quick check §

A team reports sending \(900\) message bits reliably over \(1000\) uses of an erasure channel with \(e=0.25\). Another reports \(700\). Which claim is consistent with the capacity, and what must the reported rate count?

Hint 1

\(C=1-e=0.75\).

Hint 2

Rate is message bits divided by all channel uses.

Worked solution

The capacity is \(0.75\) bits per use, so \(900\) bits in \(1000\) uses (rate \(0.9\)) cannot be sent reliably without some other resource, while \(700\) (rate \(0.7\)) is below capacity and plausible for a good code. The rate must count all channel uses, erased ones included; dividing by the \(750\) or so successful positions would overstate it. A claim above capacity signals an error or an unaccounted resource, such as feedback or shared entanglement.

4. Worked problem §

The dephasing channel applies \(Z\) with probability \(p\). (a) Show that its classical capacity is \(1\) bit per use, using inputs \(\ket0,\ket1\). (b) Send half of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\) through it and compute \(S(B)\), \(S(RB)\), and the coherent information. (c) Evaluate at \(p=0.1\). (The quantum capacity of this channel equals the single-use value found in (b).)

Hint 1

\(Z\) leaves \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) unchanged.

Hint 2

The output joint state is \((1-p)\ket{\Phi^+}\bra{\Phi^+}+p\ket{\Phi^-}\bra{\Phi^-}\).

Worked solution

(a) \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) pass unchanged, so one bit per use is sent perfectly, and a qubit cannot carry more than one bit. (b) Bob’s output is \(I/2\), so \(S(B)=1\). The joint output is \((1-p)\ket{\Phi^+}\bra{\Phi^+}+p\ket{\Phi^-}\bra{\Phi^-}\), with eigenvalues \(1-p,p\), so \(S(RB)=h_2(p)\). The coherent information is \(1-h_2(p)\). (c) At \(p=0.1\), about \(0.531\) qubits per use. Phase noise is harmless for classical bits in the computational basis but costs quantum capacity, because it damages coherence.

5. Worked problem §

Send a classical bit over the erasure channel by repetition: transmit it \(r\) times and decode from any unerased copy. (a) Find the failure probability and rate. (b) Evaluate for \(r=3\) and \(e=\frac14\). (c) Compare the rate with the capacity \(1-e\), and describe a better strategy that uses the receiver’s knowledge of erasures.

Hint 1

Failure means all \(r\) copies are erased.

Hint 2

Long block codes for erasures can approach rate \(1-e\).

Worked solution

(a) Failure requires every copy to be erased: probability \(e^r\), at rate \(1/r\). (b) For \(r=3\), \(e=\frac14\): failure \(\frac{1}{64}\approx0.016\) at rate \(\frac13\). (c) The capacity is \(0.75\), more than twice that rate. Because Bob knows which positions were erased, a long code needs only enough redundancy to fill in the missing fraction, about \(e\) of the positions, approaching rate \(1-e\) with vanishing error. Repetition wastes the information already carried by the unerased uses.

6. Worked problem §

Show that dense coding achieves the entanglement-assisted rate \(2(1-e)\) on the erasure channel. (a) What happens to each dense-coded qubit when it is erased and when it is not? (b) Combine with an outer classical erasure code to find the rate. (c) Evaluate at \(e=\frac14\) and compare with \(C=\frac34\).

Hint 1

A successful use delivers both bits; an erasure delivers a flag.

Hint 2

The receiver knows which uses were erased.

Worked solution

(a) If the qubit arrives, Bob holds both halves of the encoded Bell state and decodes two bits exactly; if it is erased, he gets the flag and learns nothing, but knows the two bits are missing. (b) Each use is a two-bit symbol erasure channel with erasure probability \(e\), and an outer erasure code over these symbols approaches rate \(2(1-e)\) bits per use. (c) At \(e=\frac14\), \(1.5\) bits per use, twice the unassisted \(0.75\). The extra bit is paid for by one ebit per use, distributed in advance.

7. Worked problem §

With free two-way classical communication, Alice can send halves of Bell pairs through an erasure channel, learn from Bob which arrived, keep only those pairs, and then teleport her qubits. (a) What rate of qubits per channel use does this give? (b) Compare with \(Q=\max(0,1-2e)\) at \(e=\frac14\) and \(e=\frac34\). (c) Why is this not a contradiction?

Hint 1

A fraction \(1-e\) of Bell halves arrive intact.

Hint 2

Teleportation needs only classical messages once the pairs are shared.

Worked solution

(a) On average \(1-e\) pairs per use survive, and each teleports one qubit using classical messages, so the rate is \(1-e\) qubits per use. (b) At \(e=\frac14\): \(0.75\) against \(Q=0.5\); at \(e=\frac34\): \(0.25\) against \(Q=0\). (c) The unassisted capacity forbids classical feedback, and without it Alice does not know which uses failed, so she must protect every qubit in advance. Feedback lets her discard failures after the fact. Capacities depend on the assistance allowed and must be compared under the same resource model.

8. Misconception §

A student argues: “On an erasure channel with \(e=\frac14\), three quarters of the qubits arrive perfectly, so the quantum capacity is \(\frac34\).” Explain why the unassisted quantum capacity is only \(\frac12\), identifying what Alice does not know when she encodes.

Hint 1

Without feedback, Alice must choose the encoding before knowing which positions will be erased.

Hint 2

Compare with the classical capacity, which is \(\frac34\).

Worked solution

The \(\frac34\) counts successful positions after the fact. Alice must encode an unknown quantum state before transmission, without knowing which positions will be erased, and the encoding cannot copy her state onto several positions (no-cloning). It must spread the state so that any typical subset of \(\frac34\) of the positions suffices, while the erased \(\frac14\) reveal nothing. The coding theorem shows this works only up to \(1-2e=\frac12\). Classical bits can be repeated freely, so the classical capacity reaches \(\frac34\); quantum information cannot.

9. Extension §

Use no-cloning to show that the erasure channel with \(e=\frac12\) has zero quantum capacity. Model the channel as an isometry that sends the input to Bob with probability \(\frac12\) and to the environment otherwise, each time leaving the other party a flag. Argue that a code allowing Bob to recover the quantum message would also let the environment recover it.

Hint 1

At \(e=\frac12\) the roles of Bob and the environment are symmetric.

Hint 2

Two parties each holding a decodable copy of an unknown state would amount to cloning.

Worked solution

At \(e=\frac12\), each use delivers the input to Bob or to the environment with equal probability, and whoever does not receive it gets a flag: the channel to Bob and the channel to the environment are identical. If some code let Bob reconstruct an unknown quantum message with fidelity near \(1\), the same decoder applied by the environment to its outputs would also reconstruct it. Together they would produce two copies of an unknown state, which no-cloning forbids. So no positive rate is possible at \(e=\frac12\), and by degrading the channel further, none for \(e>\frac12\) either, consistent with \(Q=\max(0,1-2e)\).

10. Extension §

For amplitude damping with parameter \(\gamma\) and the maximally mixed input (half of \(\ket{\Phi^+}\)), (a) find Bob’s output and \(S(B)\); (b) argue that \(S(RB)\) equals the entropy of the environment, which receives the excitation with probability \(\gamma/2\); (c) compute the coherent information \(h_2\big(\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\big)-h_2\big(\frac\gamma2\big)\) for \(\gamma=0.2\) and \(\gamma=0.5\).

Hint 1

The output of \(I/2\) is \(\mathrm{diag}\big(\frac{1+\gamma}{2},\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\big)\).

Hint 2

The total state of \(R\), \(B\), and the environment is pure, so \(S(RB)=S(E)\).

Worked solution

(a) Amplitude damping maps \(I/2\) to \(\mathrm{diag}\big(\frac{1+\gamma}{2},\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\big)\), so \(S(B)=h_2\big(\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\big)\). (b) Modelling the channel as a unitary with an environment in \(\ket0\), the joint state of \(R\), \(B\), and \(E\) is pure, so \(S(RB)=S(E)\). The environment ends in \(\ket1\) exactly when a decay occurs, which happens with probability \(\gamma\cdot\frac12\), so \(S(E)=h_2(\gamma/2)\). (c) For \(\gamma=0.2\): \(h_2(0.4)-h_2(0.1)\approx0.971-0.469=0.502\). For \(\gamma=0.5\): \(h_2(0.25)-h_2(0.25)=0\). With this input, coherent information vanishes once the environment learns as much as Bob; the quantum capacity of amplitude damping is in fact zero for \(\gamma\ge\frac12\).
