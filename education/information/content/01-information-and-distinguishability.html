1.1 Messages and Encodings

Alice sends Bob a physical object to mean either “yes” or “no.” If the two possible preparations always look identical to every permitted observation, no message has been conveyed. Information begins with distinguishable alternatives, a preparation rule, and a receiver’s procedure.

1.1.1 Physical carriers and distinguishable alternatives

A carrier might be a light pulse, a spin, or a mark on paper. The logical message is not the material itself: an encoding associates each message with a physical preparation. A decoder associates an observation with a guess. Two preparations are perfectly distinguishable for a task if an allowed observation identifies which occurred with certainty. Imperfect detectors and overlapping physical responses turn that certainty into a probability.

1.1.2 States and knowledge about states

A state description collects predictions for possible observations. It is distinct from a name assigned to the preparation and from the receiver’s knowledge of that name. If Alice chooses between two preparations secretly, Bob may need an uncertain description even though Alice knows her choice. Later, quantum theory will show that a pure state itself can have unpredictable measurement outcomes; those probabilities need not come from ignorance of a classical label.

1.2 Uncertainty

1.2.1 Possible messages and prior knowledge

Suppose Alice sends A on three quarters of trials and B on one quarter. These frequencies, or a model assigning those probabilities, are Bob’s prior information. Without observing anything, always guessing A succeeds with probability 3/4. A proposed detector must be compared with that baseline, not with a fair guess. Probabilities are nonnegative numbers summing to one over mutually exclusive exhaustive possibilities.

1.2.2 Observations and inference

Let a detector report red with probability 1/5 for A and 4/5 for B. The joint probabilities of red with A and red with B are (3/4)(1/5)=3/20 and (1/4)(4/5)=1/5. Red occurs with total probability 7/20. Among red events, the fraction from B is (1/5)/(7/20)=4/7. This division is conditioning: update the alternatives using the observation and normalize their weights. A red report favors B even though A was more likely before observation.

Problem 1.2. How well does the rule “red means B, otherwise A” perform?

Worked solution

Correct events have probability (1/4)(4/5)+(3/4)(4/5)=4/5. This improves on the prior-only success 3/4.

1.3 Information Tasks

1.3.1 Learning, preserving, and transmitting

Learning a message, preserving an unknown state, and transmitting a carrier are different tasks. A procedure that identifies a preparation may disturb the carrier. A procedure that faithfully transfers it may leave the receiver unable to identify it from a single observation. We will keep the physical transformation and the classical information extracted from it separate.

1.3.2 Resources and criteria for success

State the allowed preparations, prior probabilities, observations, output, and success criterion before comparing protocols. Resources include uses of a channel, transmitted systems, prior shared correlations, and classical messages. More complicated physical descriptions do not automatically carry more accessible information. The receiver must have a procedure that actually extracts what the task requests.

Problem 1.3. Does a carrier with many possible physical settings guarantee many readable messages?

Worked solution

No. Their responses may overlap, the receiver may have limited measurements, or the preparation and noise may prevent reliable discrimination.
