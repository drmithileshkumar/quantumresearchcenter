11.1 State Entropy

A Bell pair is specified by one pure vector, yet either half is maximally uncertain. A useful entropy must describe both facts without treating their difference as an inconsistency.

11.1.1 Eigenvalues as probabilities§

A density matrix has orthogonal eigenvectors satisfying \(\rho\ket j=\lambda_j\ket j\). Positivity makes the eigenvalues nonnegative and trace one makes them sum to one. In that basis, \(\rho=\sum_j\lambda_j\ket j\bra j\). For \(\rho=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&r\\r&1\end{pmatrix}\) with 0≤r≤1, plus and minus have eigenvalues (1±r)/2. These values characterize the state even when its preparation ensemble uses different nonorthogonal vectors.

The eigenvalue calculation can be done without a general diagonalization algorithm. Multiply \(\rho=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&r\\r&1\end{pmatrix}\) by \((1,1)^T/\sqrt2\): both components are multiplied by \((1+r)/2\). Multiplying \((1,-1)^T/\sqrt2\) gives the factor \((1-r)/2\). These are orthogonal unit vectors, so they supply the full two-dimensional spectrum.

At \(r=1\) the state is pure plus, at \(r=0\) it is \(I/2\), and at \(r=1/2\) its entropy is \(h_2(1/4)\approx0.8113\). In every case its Z counts are fair. Entropy depends on the whole matrix, not the uncertainty in an arbitrarily selected readout basis.

11.1.2 Von Neumann entropy and pure states§

Define \(S(\rho)=-\sum_j\lambda_j\log_2\lambda_j\), equivalently −tr(ρ log₂ρ), where the matrix logarithm applies the scalar logarithm in the eigenbasis. Zero eigenvalues contribute zero by continuity. A pure state has eigenvalues 1,0,… and entropy zero; I/2 has entropy one. This is not the entropy of every measurement: measuring a pure plus state in Z gives a fair classical record despite S=0.

Problems

Problem 11.1. What is the entropy of diag(3/4,1/4)?

Worked solution

It is −(3/4)log₂(3/4)−(1/4)log₂(1/4)≈0.811 bits.

11.2 Correlation Measures

11.2.1 Mutual information§

The quantum mutual information is \(I(A:B)=S(A)+S(B)-S(AB)\). For a Bell pair it is 1+1−0=2 bits. For the equal classical mixture of 00 and 11 it is 1+1−1=1. The extra quantum correlation is not a claim that measuring either half alone yields two classical bits. Measurements and assistance determine how correlations become usable.

11.2.2 Conditional entropy and negative values§

Conditional entropy is \(S(A|B)=S(AB)-S(B)\). It equals −1 for a Bell pair and zero for the classically correlated mixture. Negative conditional entropy is meaningful in resource tasks: in asymptotic quantum state merging, with classical communication available, the conditional entropy gives the net entanglement cost and a negative value represents entanglement generation. This operational theorem requires many independent copies and vanishing error; it is not an ordinary negative count of classical guesses.

Problems

Problem 11.2. Why is the Bell pair’s joint entropy zero?

Worked solution

Its joint density matrix is a rank-one projector with one eigenvalue one. The mixed local spectra do not determine the joint spectrum.

11.3 Information Inequalities

11.3.1 Relative entropy and distinguishability§

Relative entropy is \(D(\rho\|\sigma)=\mathrm{tr}[\rho(\log_2\rho-\log_2\sigma)]\), finite when the support of ρ lies inside that of σ. The support is the span of eigenvectors with nonzero eigenvalues; the condition excludes a possible component of ρ that σ assigns probability zero. It quantifies asymmetry in distinguishing state hypotheses and is not a symmetric geometric distance. For ρ=|0〉〈0| and σ=I/2 it is one; reversing them gives infinity because σ then assigns zero support to a possible input component.

11.3.2 Data processing and operational meaning§

Quantum relative entropy cannot increase under a common channel: \(D(\mathcal E(\rho)\|\mathcal E(\sigma))\leq D(\rho\|\sigma)\). This is a theorem, not proved by the examples here. Applying information inequalities to a classical label encoded as states gives the Holevo bound \(I(X:Y)\leq\chi=S(\sum_xp_x\rho_x)-\sum_xp_xS(\rho_x)\). For equal-prior pure zero and plus, the average eigenvalues are (1±1/√2)/2, so χ≈0.601 bits. It bounds every measurement’s accessible information but need not be achieved by a single-copy measurement. This is distinct from the 0.8536 decision success in Chapter 8.

Return to the zero/plus encoding. Its average matrix is \(\bar\rho=\begin{pmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/4&1/4\end{pmatrix}\). Its trace is one and its determinant is \(1/8\), so the two eigenvalues solve \(\lambda^2-\lambda+1/8=0\): they are \((1\pm1/\sqrt2)/2\). Their entropy is approximately \(0.6009\) bits. The component states are pure, making their entropy contributions zero and leaving exactly this value for \(\chi\).

Chapter 8’s decision measurement gave approximately \(0.3991\) bits, below the bound. The number \(\chi\) constrains all measurements of this ensemble; the number \(0.3991\) is the information of a particular demonstrated procedure. Neither is the entropy one of Alice’s fair classical label, because her two preparations overlap. The bound and its proof are general theorems; computing this spectrum checks their application to our example.

Entropy next becomes a physical resource rate: the number of qubits needed to preserve a repeated quantum source.

Problems

Problem 11.3. Does χ=0.601 mean each trial returns a fractional bit string?

Worked solution

No. It bounds average mutual information between the input label and the recorded outcome. Individual outcomes are ordinary labels; information is a statistical quantity.

Transfer problem. Fully dephase Alice’s half of a Bell pair. Calculate the changes in joint entropy, quantum mutual information, and conditional entropy.

Worked solution

The output is the equal mixture of 00 and 11. Local entropies stay at one each, while joint entropy rises from zero to one. Mutual information falls from two to one and conditional entropy rises from −1 to zero. The preserved Z correlations do not preserve all quantum correlations.
