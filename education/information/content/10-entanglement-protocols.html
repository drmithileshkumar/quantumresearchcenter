10.1 Teleportation

Alice has an unknown qubit and shares a Bell pair with Bob. She cannot copy the input or describe its amplitudes from one sample. Nevertheless, a joint measurement and two classical bits can transfer its state to Bob.

10.1.1 Joint measurements and outcome branches§

Use register order S,A,B. Start with \(\ket\psi_S\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB}\). Alice applies CNOT S→A, then H on S. Expansion gives

\[\frac12\sum_{m,n=0}^1\ket{mn}_{SA}X^nZ^m\ket\psi_B.\]

Each pair of outcomes m,n has probability one quarter, independent of the unknown input. This equation is the complete branch table: 00 gives ψ, 01 gives Xψ, 10 gives Zψ, and 11 gives XZψ.

10.1.2 Conditional corrections and resource consumption§

Alice sends m,n. Bob applies X when n=1 and then Z when m=1, implementing \(Z^mX^n\) and recovering ψ. Alice’s input has been measured and the shared Bell pair consumed. Before the record arrives, averaging the four Pauli branches gives I/2 on Bob. The protocol transmits one unknown qubit using one shared ebit and two classical bits; it does not send a usable message instantly.

We can see where the branch formula comes from. With \(\ket\psi=\alpha\ket0+\beta\ket1\), the state after Alice’s CNOT is

\[\frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(\alpha\ket{000}+\alpha\ket{011}+\beta\ket{110}+\beta\ket{101}\bigr).\]

Now apply \(H\) to the first label and collect terms by the first two labels. For 00, the remaining column is \((\alpha,\beta)/2\); for 01 it is \((\beta,\alpha)/2\); for 10 it is \((\alpha,-\beta)/2\); for 11 it is \((-\beta,\alpha)/2\). Each has squared length one quarter because \(|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1\). Neither measurement bit reveals either amplitude.

For example, on outcome 11 Bob holds \(XZ\ket\psi\). Applying \(X\) and then \(Z\) gives \(ZX(XZ)\ket\psi=\ket\psi\). The order is visible in the written product: the rightmost operation acts first.

Problems

Problem 10.1. For teleportation outcome m=1,n=0, what correction is required?

Worked solution

Bob holds Zψ and applies Z. Applying X instead generally gives the wrong state.

10.2 Dense Coding

10.2.1 Encoding classical messages in a shared pair§

For dense coding, begin with a Bell pair and let Alice apply X^bZ^a to her half to encode two classical bits a,b. The four resulting Bell states are orthogonal. Alice sends her physical qubit to Bob; he now holds both halves and can perform a joint measurement. Pre-shared entanglement alone would not let him read the choice.

10.2.2 Joint decoding and transmitted systems§

Decoding uses CNOT from Alice’s received qubit to Bob’s original half, followed by H on the received qubit and computational-basis measurement. The outputs are a,b under this convention, up to irrelevant global branch phases in the intermediate Bell states. The resource account is one transmitted qubit plus one consumed shared Bell pair for two classical bits. Comparing with an unassisted qubit while ignoring the prior pair would use unequal resources.

For message 10, CNOT sends the encoded state to \((\ket{00}-\ket{10})/\sqrt2=\ket-\ket0\); \(H\) on the first wire gives \(\ket{10}\). For message 11 the same decoder gives \(-\ket{11}\), whose overall minus sign changes no probability. The table can therefore be decoded with certainty. Before Alice sends her half, Bob’s local matrix is \(I/2\) for every row.

Problems

Problem 10.2. Why does dense coding not contradict a one-bit unassisted qubit bound?

Worked solution

It consumes pre-shared entanglement and uses a joint two-qubit decoder. The resource model differs from sending an unassisted qubit.

10.3 Entanglement Swapping

10.3.1 Connecting separated pairs§

If A–B and C–D are Bell pairs, a Bell measurement on B,C leaves A,D in a Bell state conditioned on its outcome. This is entanglement swapping. Expand the two pairs and group terms by the Bell basis of the middle systems to obtain four equally likely branches. The endpoints need not interact directly, but creating the initial pairs and sending the measurement record still require physical resources.

Check one swapping branch explicitly in order A,B,C,D. The initial four nonzero terms are \((\ket{0000}+\ket{0011}+\ket{1100}+\ket{1111})/2\). Project B,C onto \(\ket{\Phi^+}\). Only the first and last terms survive, giving \((\ket{00}_{AD}+\ket{11}_{AD})/(2\sqrt2)\). Its squared length is one quarter; normalization leaves a Bell pair.

The other three Bell outcomes give the other three endpoint Bell states with the same probability. Averaging all four states cancels their off-diagonal entries and gives \(I/4\), which is also the endpoints’ initial reduction. The middle measurement reorganizes conditional correlations; its unannounced outcome supplies no immediately usable endpoint state label.

10.3.2 Classical records and protocol accounting§

Without the swapping outcome, the endpoints average to I/4 and cannot select a particular Bell state. Communicating the record lets them correct or track a Pauli frame. In a chain, loss, imperfect entanglement, memories, and classical messages limit the practical rate. Ideal swapping is a protocol component, not by itself a complete scalable repeater architecture.

We next quantify uncertainty and correlations, including the striking fact that a pure pair can have uncertain individual parts.

Sources and further study: Bennett et al., Teleporting an unknown quantum state; Bennett and Wiesner, Dense coding.

Problems

Problem 10.3. Can the endpoints use a particular swapped Bell state before learning the middle result?

Worked solution

They cannot identify its Pauli frame. Their unconditioned joint state is the equal mixture of four Bell states, I/4.

Transfer problem. Alice sends only the teleportation record n and withholds m. After correcting X using n, what state does Bob have? Which input states still survive?

Worked solution

Correcting X leaves the equally likely branches \(\ket\psi\) and \(Z\ket\psi\). Their average density matrix is \((\rho+Z\rho Z)/2=\mathrm{diag}(|\alpha|^2,|\beta|^2)\). Basis states survive, while general relative phase is lost. One correction bit preserves less than an arbitrary unknown qubit.
