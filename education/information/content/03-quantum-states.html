3.1 Amplitudes

A two-path experiment can yield a certain output when both paths are available, yet a fair output when a path record is made. Ordinary probabilities attached independently to the paths cannot describe that change. We introduce amplitudes as quantities that combine before the probability is calculated.

3.1.1 Interference and the probability rule

Assign amplitudes a,b to two exclusive configuration labels 0,1. Their measured probabilities are \(|a|^2,|b|^2\), summing to one. This probability rule is a physical postulate supported by experiment; it is not derived merely from a desire for reversible algebra. The transformation H sends amplitudes (a,b) to \(((a+b)/\sqrt2,(a-b)/\sqrt2)\). Starting with (1,0), two H operations restore (1,0): the unwanted paths cancel in the second output.

3.1.2 Complex numbers and relative phase

A relative minus sign changes H’s outcome, even though it leaves the original configuration probabilities unchanged. Continuous phase changes are represented by complex numbers. Write i²=−1 and \(e^{i\phi}=\cos\phi+i\sin\phi\); its magnitude is one. For \((1,e^{i\phi})/\sqrt2\), an H readout gives zero-probability \((1+\cos\phi)/2\). Multiplying both amplitudes by the same phase changes no prediction. Relative phase is physical in this experiment; global phase is not.

Problem 3.1. Predict H applied to minus.

Worked solution

Minus has amplitudes (1,−1)/√2. The output amplitudes are (0,1), so the result is one with certainty.

3.2 State Representations

3.2.1 Vectors, kets, and inner products

A column of amplitudes is a vector. We write \(\ket\psi=a\ket0+b\ket1\), where kets name vectors and the coefficients specify their combination. The inner product \(\braket\phi\psi=c^*a+d^*b\) for \(\ket\phi=c\ket0+d\ket1\) compares vectors; the star conjugates a complex number. Its squared magnitude is the probability of the projection test onto normalized φ. Orthogonal means inner product zero.

3.2.2 Normalization and distinguishability

Normalization is \(\braket\psi\psi=1\). The states zero and one are orthogonal; zero and plus have overlap 1/√2. A zero-basis measurement on plus is fair, while the plus/minus basis distinguishes those two states perfectly. A pure vector is a preparation description, not a list of hidden outcomes for every possible measurement. Different measurement bases ask different physical questions.

Problem 3.2. For (|0〉+i|1〉)/√2, what is the H readout probability of zero?

Worked solution

It is |(1+i)/2|²=1/2. The phase π/2 gives a fair readout, unlike phase zero or π.

3.3 Evolution and Readout

3.3.1 Unitary transitions

A linear transition U maps every normalized input to a normalized output exactly when \(U^\dagger U=I\); the dagger conjugates and transposes its matrix. In a finite equal-dimensional space, this is a unitary operation with inverse U†. It preserves all inner products. H is its own inverse. This describes isolated reversible evolution; measurements and discarded systems will require a broader description.

3.3.2 Projective measurements and conditional states

For orthogonal projectors Pj summing to I, outcome j has probability \(\|P_j\psi\|^2\), and the conditional state is \(P_j\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_j}\) when p_j>0. A rank-one test keeps one direction; a higher-rank test keeps a subspace and may preserve superposition within it. We draw a circuit wire as the evolving system, a labeled gate as a unitary, and M as measurement; time runs left to right. A double line represents its classical record.

Problem 3.3. Can a unitary map both zero and one to plus?

Worked solution

No. Their original inner product is zero, while identical output vectors have inner product one. Unitary evolution preserves this distinction.
