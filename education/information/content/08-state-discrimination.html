8.1 Discrimination Tasks

Alice chooses zero or plus with equal probability. Bob knows the two possibilities but receives only one qubit. A computational-basis measurement is useful, yet it is not the best possible decision procedure.

8.1.1 Priors, decisions, and error§

Let the labels be 0 for |0〉 and 1 for |+〉. Measuring Z and guessing 0 after outcome zero, 1 after outcome one succeeds with probability 1/2+1/4=3/4. Always guessing either label succeeds only one half. The prior distribution and whether wrong or inconclusive answers are permitted define the optimization problem.

Write the Z strategy as a conditional-probability table. On input zero, its outcome is certainly zero. On input plus, outcomes zero and one each occur with probability one half. Under equal priors, the joint weights are \(p(0,0)=1/2\), \(p(0,1)=0\), and \(p(1,0)=p(1,1)=1/4\), where the first entry names the prepared label. Guessing zero at outcome zero is therefore sensible even though that outcome does not identify the input with certainty.

At outcome one, the preparation must have been plus. Certainty on some trials does not imply perfect discrimination on every trial. Allowing a declared inconclusive outcome will give a different way to exploit this distinction.

8.1.2 Orthogonal and nonorthogonal alternatives§

The overlap is \(s=|\braket0+|=1/\sqrt2\). Orthogonal states have zero overlap and can be separated by a projective measurement with certainty. These two are nonorthogonal. A measurement that identified either input with certainty would need disjoint outcome distributions despite their nonzero overlap. The quantitative optimum below shows the unavoidable error rather than merely declaring the states “similar.”

Problems

Problem 8.1. For the Z strategy, what is the posterior probability of label zero after outcome zero?

Worked solution

The outcome-zero weight from input zero is 1/2; its total weight is 3/4. The posterior is (1/2)/(3/4)=2/3. Guessing zero is best for this outcome but is not certain to be right.

8.2 Optimal Strategies

8.2.1 Minimum-error discrimination§

Before optimizing, test a whole family of readout directions. Let the zero-guess vector be \(\ket{v_\theta}=\cos\theta\ket0+\sin\theta\ket1\), and let the perpendicular vector trigger the plus guess. The correct-decision probability is

\[P_{\rm succ}(\theta)=\tfrac12\cos^2\theta+\tfrac14(\cos\theta-\sin\theta)^2=\tfrac12+\tfrac14(\cos2\theta-\sin2\theta).\]

At \(\theta=0\) we recover \(3/4\). The last bracket reaches \(\sqrt2\) at \(\theta=-\pi/8\), giving \(0.8536\). The eigenvalue argument below does more than search these directions: it proves that no two-outcome POVM, including effects that are not projectors, improves on the chosen projector.

For two density operators with priors p0,p1, guessing zero on effect E gives success \(p_1+\mathrm{tr}[E(p_0\rho_0-p_1\rho_1)]\). The matrix \(\Delta=p_0\rho_0-p_1\rho_1\) is Hermitian: it equals its conjugate transpose. An eigenvector is a direction that this matrix only scales, \(\Delta\ket v=\lambda\ket v\); the scale \(\lambda\) is its eigenvalue. A Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis. We use this mathematical fact here; for the displayed two-by-two example it can also be checked by direct multiplication. Express Δ in that basis. Choosing E as the projector onto its positive-eigenvalue directions maximizes the trace, because each direction can receive a weight between zero and one. The optimum is \((1+\|\Delta\|_1)/2\), where the trace norm sums absolute eigenvalues for a Hermitian matrix. For equal-prior zero and plus, Δ=(Z−X)/4 has eigenvalues ±1/(2√2), giving success \((1+1/\sqrt2)/2\approx0.8536\). The zero-guess direction is \(\cos(\pi/8)\ket0-\sin(\pi/8)\ket1\).

Define trace distance by \(T(\rho,\sigma)=\|\rho-\sigma\|_1/2\). For equal priors, the optimum is \((1+T)/2\). Distance zero means identical statistics for all measurements; distance one permits a certain decision. This operational meaning is more useful here than treating the norm as unexplained geometry.

For pure inputs with overlap magnitude s, choose an overall phase so the overlap is nonnegative. In their two-dimensional span write \(\ket\phi=s\ket\psi+\sqrt{1-s^2}\ket{\psi_\perp}\). The difference of their projectors has trace zero and determinant \(-(1-s^2)\), so its eigenvalues are \(\pm\sqrt{1-s^2}\). Consequently \(T=\sqrt{1-s^2}\), which also lets us evaluate the two-copy problem below.

8.2.2 Unambiguous discrimination and inconclusive results§

If mistakes are forbidden but “inconclusive” is allowed, use \(E_0=c\ket-\bra-\), \(E_1=c\ket1\bra1\), and \(E_?=I-E_0-E_1\), with \(c=1/(1+1/\sqrt2)\). E0 never fires on plus and E1 never fires on zero. The chosen c makes the remainder positive. Each input has conclusive probability c/2=1−1/√2; the rest is inconclusive. For two equal-prior pure states this success 1−s is optimal; this optimality is a theorem beyond verifying the explicit effects.

Problems

Problem 8.2. Why does E0 give no false identification on plus?

Worked solution

Minus is orthogonal to plus, so 〈+|E0|+〉=0. Positivity ensures this is a valid zero probability, not cancellation between negative probabilities.

8.3 Accessible Information

8.3.1 Measurements on encoded messages§

For an encoding ensemble \(\{p_x,\rho_x\}\) and chosen POVM, the classical joint distribution is \(p(x,y)=p_x\mathrm{tr}(E_y\rho_x)\). Its mutual information is \(I(X:Y)=\sum_{x,y}p(x,y)\log_2[p(x,y)/(p(x)p(y))]\). This measures how much the outcome reduces uncertainty about the label on average. Maximizing it over measurements defines accessible information. Maximizing decision success and maximizing mutual information need not be the same optimization.

8.3.2 Bounds on classical information extraction§

A finite-dimensional quantum carrier does not offer unrestricted access to all continuous amplitude parameters in a single shot. Entropy gives a bound on accessible classical information, called the Holevo bound, which we will state after constructing quantum entropy in Chapter 11. Here the operational calculation is already explicit: choose an effect, obtain conditional probabilities, and evaluate the task’s score. Repeated independent copies provide a different resource and can improve discrimination.

For the optimal decision basis on zero and plus, both labels have error \(q=(1-1/\sqrt2)/2\). Its classical record is a binary symmetric channel with fair input. Chapter 2 therefore gives \(I(X:Y)=1-h_2(q)\), approximately \(0.399\) bits per signal. For the original Z measurement, \(H(X)=1\), outcome zero has probability \(3/4\), and the posterior there is \((2/3,1/3)\). Outcome one identifies plus. Hence \(I(X:Y)=1-(3/4)h_2(1/3)\), approximately \(0.311\) bits.

The optimized decision measurement improves both scores in this example. That coincidence is not a general equivalence of objectives. A success probability such as \(0.8536\) is a fraction of correct guesses, while \(0.399\) bits is an average reduction in uncertainty. They have different units and answer different questions.

The distinction between orthogonal and nonorthogonal inputs also limits copying, broadcasting, and signalling.

Sources and further study: Watrous, The Theory of Quantum Information, discrimination.

Problems

Problem 8.3. If one state has prior 0.99, is a fair guess the right baseline?

Worked solution

No. Always guessing the common state already succeeds with probability 0.99. Any claimed improvement must use the specified priors.

Transfer problem. Bob receives two independent copies of the same hidden preparation, either zero or plus with equal priors. Use their overlap to compute the optimal decision success. Why is this allowed by no-cloning?

Worked solution

The joint candidates have overlap \((1/\sqrt2)^2=1/2\). The equal-prior pure-state trace distance is \(\sqrt{1-s^2}=\sqrt3/2\), so optimum success is \((1+\sqrt3/2)/2\approx0.9330\). Alice supplied two copies; Bob has not manufactured a second copy from an unknown single input.
