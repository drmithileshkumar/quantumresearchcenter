Predict first. A relative phase changes no original 0/1 probabilities. Can it change a later experiment?

Compare your reasoning

Yes. H combines amplitudes from the two configurations. Their relative phase changes the sum before its magnitude is squared.

3.1 Amplitudes

A two-path experiment can yield a certain output when both paths are available, yet a fair output when a path record is made. Ordinary probabilities attached independently to the paths cannot describe that change. We introduce amplitudes as quantities that combine before the probability is calculated.

3.1.1 Interference and the probability rule§

Attach real amplitudes a,b to the two configuration labels 0,1. For now their probabilities are a²,b², with a²+b²=1. Negative amplitudes are allowed; negative probabilities are not. The operation H maps (a,b) to \(((a+b)/\sqrt2,(a-b)/\sqrt2)\). This specifies both output amplitudes for every input and lets us inspect how two operations compose. The rule converting amplitudes to probabilities is a physical postulate supported by experiment, not a consequence of reversible algebra.

Start with a definite configuration 0, represented by the pair \((1,0)\). After one \(H\), its amplitudes are \((1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2)\). For the next step, each of those inputs contributes to each output. The output-0 contributions are \(1/2\) and \(1/2\); the output-1 contributions are \(1/2\) and \(-1/2\). Add before squaring: the resulting amplitudes are \((1,0)\), so the measured probabilities are \((1,0)\). Squaring the separate paths first would instead predict \((1/2,1/2)\), erasing the observed interference.

3.1.2 Complex numbers and relative phase§

For a complex amplitude \(z=u+iv\), the real numbers \(u,v\) specify its two components, and \(i^2=-1\). Its conjugate is \(z^*=u-iv\). Multiplication gives \(zz^*=u^2+v^2\), a nonnegative number called its squared magnitude \(|z|^2\). Thus \(|(1+i)/2|^2=1/2\), although \(((1+i)/2)^2=i/2\) is not a probability. This is why the probability rule needs the magnitude square.

An angle \(\phi\) describes a direction in this two-component plane; \(\cos\phi\) and \(\sin\phi\) are the horizontal and vertical coordinates of its unit circle. A full turn is \(2\pi\) radians. The notation \(e^{i\phi}\) abbreviates \(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi\), a unit-magnitude phase. At angles \(0,\pi/2,\pi\), it is \(1,i,-1\). We will use these concrete values before needing any further properties of trigonometry.

A relative minus sign changes H’s outcome, even though it leaves the original configuration probabilities unchanged. For amplitudes \((1,e^{i\phi})/\sqrt2\), an H readout gives outcome-zero probability \((1+\cos\phi)/2\). Multiplying both amplitudes by the same phase changes no prediction. Relative phase affects interference; global phase does not.

Problems

Problem 3.1. Predict H applied to the amplitudes (1,−1)/√2.

Worked solution

This input is called minus and has amplitudes (1,−1)/√2. The output amplitudes are (0,1), so the result is one with certainty.

3.2 State Representations

3.2.1 Vectors, kets, and inner products§

The symbols \(\ket0=(1,0)^T\) and \(\ket1=(0,1)^T\) name basis vectors. The superscript \(T\) means arrange the entries as a column. The expression \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) is the column \((a,b)^T\), so ket notation is a compact way to label configurations, not an extra physical rule. Define \(\ket+=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) and \(\ket-=(\ket0-\ket1)/\sqrt2\).

A column of amplitudes is a vector. We write \(\ket\psi=a\ket0+b\ket1\), where kets name vectors and the coefficients specify their combination. The inner product \(\braket\phi\psi=c^*a+d^*b\) for \(\ket\phi=c\ket0+d\ket1\) compares vectors; the star conjugates a complex number. Its squared magnitude is the probability of the projection test onto normalized φ. Orthogonal means inner product zero.

For an explicit inner product, take \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Its bra is the conjugated row \(\bra\psi=(1,-i)/\sqrt2\). Multiplying the row and column gives \(\braket\psi\psi=(1+(-i)i)/2=1\). Without conjugation the result would be zero, incorrectly assigning zero length to a nonzero state. A test for plus instead has amplitude \(\braket+\psi=(1+i)/2\) and probability one half.

“Basis” means a set of perpendicular unit vectors from which every vector can be built. The pair plus/minus is another basis. Applying \(H\) before computational readout measures that basis, since \(H\ket+=\ket0\) and \(H\ket-=\ket1\). The device can therefore distinguish plus from minus even though their original 0/1 probabilities coincide.

3.2.2 Normalization and distinguishability§

Normalization is \(\braket\psi\psi=1\). The states zero and one are orthogonal; zero and plus have overlap 1/√2. A computational-basis measurement on plus is fair, while the plus/minus basis distinguishes plus from minus perfectly. A pure vector is a preparation description, not a list of hidden outcomes for every possible measurement. Different measurement bases ask different physical questions.

Problems

Problem 3.2. For (|0〉+i|1〉)/√2, what is the H readout probability of zero?

Worked solution

It is |(1+i)/2|²=1/2. The phase π/2 gives a fair readout, unlike phase zero or π.

3.3 Evolution and Readout

3.3.1 Unitary transitions§

A matrix records how a linear operation moves the basis vectors. Its first column is the image of \(\ket0\), its second the image of \(\ket1\). For our operation,

\[H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Matrix multiplication means multiplying each input amplitude by its column and adding the contributions at each output. The output of a weighted sum is the same weighted sum of the separate outputs; this property is called linearity. For example \(H(1,i)^T/\sqrt2=((1+i)/2,(1-i)/2)^T\). Each output has probability one half. Applying \(H\) again recovers the input. More generally, preserving the length of every vector forces the columns to have unit length and mutually zero inner products, expressed by \(U^\dagger U=I\). The identity matrix \(I\) leaves every vector unchanged.

A linear transition U maps every normalized input to a normalized output exactly when \(U^\dagger U=I\); the dagger conjugates and transposes its matrix. In a finite equal-dimensional space, this is a unitary operation with inverse U†. It preserves all inner products. H is its own inverse. This describes isolated reversible evolution; measurements and discarded systems will require a broader description.

3.3.2 Projective measurements and conditional states§

A projector keeps a chosen component and removes perpendicular ones: \(P_0=\ket0\bra0\) maps \(a\ket0+b\ket1\) to \(a\ket0\). Its squared length is the probability \(|a|^2\). If that outcome occurs, divide by \(|a|\) to obtain a unit vector; any remaining overall phase has no observable effect. The conditional update is a measurement postulate. It is different from the reversible evolution that brought the system to the detector.

For orthogonal projectors Pj summing to I, outcome j has probability \(\|P_j\psi\|^2\), and the conditional state is \(P_j\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_j}\) when p_j>0. A rank-one test keeps one direction; a higher-rank test keeps a subspace and may preserve superposition within it. We draw a circuit wire as the evolving system, a labeled gate as a unitary, and M as measurement; time runs left to right. A double line represents its classical record.

Two gates we will use are \(X\ket0=\ket1, X\ket1=\ket0\), which exchanges labels, and \(Z\ket0=\ket0, Z\ket1=-\ket1\), which changes their relative sign. Products act from right to left: \(XZ\ket\psi\) means apply \(Z\) first, then \(X\).

The next chapter pairs configuration labels to describe two systems and discovers joint states that cannot be split into separate vectors.

Problems

Problem 3.3. Can a unitary map both zero and one to plus?

Worked solution

No. Their original inner product is zero, while identical output vectors have inner product one. Unitary evolution preserves this distinction.

Transfer problem. A source prepares either \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\) with equal probability and hides the label. Does an H readout distinguish this source from a source that always prepares \((\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\)?

Worked solution

No. H gives fair outcomes for either source, so this one readout does not distinguish them. They are still different preparations: projection onto the second source’s pure state succeeds certainly on it and only half the time on the hidden plus/minus ensemble. Agreement in one experiment is insufficient.

Questions and worked problems

These exercises draw on the whole chapter. Attempt each question before opening a hint, explain your reasoning, then compare it with the worked solution.

1. Quick check §

Let \(\ket{y_\pm}=(\ket0\pm i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). Compute \(\braket{-}{y_+}\) and \(\braket{y_+}{y_-}\), and give the probability of each projection test. Which pair is orthogonal?

Hint 1

Conjugate the coefficients of the left vector.

Hint 2

\(\bra{y_+}=(1,-i)/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

\(\braket{-}{y_+}=\frac12(1\cdot1+(-1)\cdot i)=\frac{1-i}{2}\), with probability \(|1-i|^2/4=\frac12\). \(\braket{y_+}{y_-}=\frac12\big(1+(-i)(-i)\big)=\frac12(1-1)=0\), so \(\ket{y_+}\) and \(\ket{y_-}\) are orthogonal and a suitable test distinguishes them perfectly, even though both give fair results in the computational basis and in the \(\pm\) basis.

2. Quick check §

Normalize \(3\ket0+4i\ket1\), and give its computational-basis probabilities. Then decide whether \((\ket0+\ket1)/2\) is a valid state as written.

Hint 1

The squared length is \(|3|^2+|4i|^2\).

Hint 2

A state must satisfy \(\braket\psi\psi=1\).

Worked solution

The squared length is \(9+16=25\), so the normalized state is \(\frac35\ket0+\frac{4i}{5}\ket1\), with probabilities \(\frac{9}{25}\) and \(\frac{16}{25}\). The vector \((\ket0+\ket1)/2\) has squared length \(\frac14+\frac14=\frac12\), so its “probabilities” would add to \(\frac12\); dividing by \(1/\sqrt2\) gives \(\ket+\). Only the direction of a vector describes the state, but the probability rule requires the normalized representative.

3. Quick check §

Let \(S=\mathrm{diag}(1,i)\). Check that \(S\) is unitary, compute \(S\ket+\), and find the probability of outcome zero when \(S\ket+\) is read out through \(H\).

Hint 1

\(S^\dagger=\mathrm{diag}(1,-i)\).

Hint 2

Use \(p(0)=(1+\cos\phi)/2\) for amplitudes \((1,e^{i\phi})/\sqrt2\).

Worked solution

\(S^\dagger S=\mathrm{diag}(1\cdot1,(-i)i)=I\), so \(S\) is unitary. \(S\ket+=(\ket0+i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), a relative phase \(\phi=\pi/2\). The \(H\) readout gives \(p(0)=(1+\cos\frac\pi2)/2=\frac12\). \(S\) changes no computational-basis probability, yet it moves \(\ket+\) to a state that the \(\pm\) test no longer identifies with certainty.

4. Worked problem §

A qubit is prepared in \(\ket\psi=\cos\theta\ket0+\sin\theta\ket1\) with real \(\theta\). (a) Find the probability of outcome \(+\) in a projective test onto \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\), and the conditional state. (b) Evaluate at \(\theta=\pi/8\) and \(\theta=-\pi/4\). (c) For which \(\theta\) is the outcome certain?

Hint 1

\(\braket+\psi=(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)/\sqrt2\).

Hint 2

\((\cos\theta+\sin\theta)^2=1+\sin2\theta\).

Worked solution

(a) \(p(+)=\frac12(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)^2=\frac12(1+\sin2\theta)\), and \(p(-)=\frac12(1-\sin2\theta)\). After outcome \(+\) the state is \(\ket+\) (up to phase), whatever \(\theta\) was. (b) At \(\theta=\pi/8\), \(p(+)=\frac12(1+\frac{1}{\sqrt2})\approx0.854\). At \(\theta=-\pi/4\), \(\sin2\theta=-1\), so \(p(+)=0\): the state is \(-\ket-\). (c) The outcome is certain when \(\sin2\theta=\pm1\), that is, when \(\ket\psi\) is \(\pm\ket+\) or \(\pm\ket-\). A projective test gives certainty only for states lying along one of its directions.

5. Worked problem §

Start with \(\ket0\). (a) Measure in the computational basis twice in a row: what are the probabilities of the outcome pairs? (b) Instead, first measure in the \(\pm\) basis, then in the computational basis: what is the probability of \(0\) at the end? (c) What does the comparison show?

Hint 1

After an outcome, the state is the corresponding basis vector.

Hint 2

\(\ket\pm\) each give \(0\) with probability \(\frac12\).

Worked solution

(a) Both measurements give \(0\) with certainty: the conditional state after the first is \(\ket0\). (b) The \(\pm\) measurement gives \(+\) or \(-\) with probability \(\frac12\) each, leaving \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\). Each then gives \(0\) with probability \(\frac12\), so the final probability of \(0\) is \(\frac12\). (c) An intermediate measurement in a different basis changes the later statistics: a state that would have given \(0\) with certainty gives \(0\) only half the time. Measurement in one basis disturbs the state as seen in another, which Chapter 9 turns into a constraint on gaining information.

6. Worked problem §

A three-level system is prepared in \(\ket\psi=(\ket0+\ket1+\ket2)/\sqrt3\). A projective test has two outcomes: \(P_a=\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1\) and \(P_b=\ket2\bra2\). (a) Find the outcome probabilities and conditional states. (b) After outcome \(a\), apply \(H\) to the levels \(0,1\) (leaving \(\ket2\) alone) and measure all three levels. What results? (c) What would a test with three one-dimensional outcomes have done instead?

Hint 1

\(P_a\ket\psi=(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt3\).

Hint 2

The conditional state after \(a\) is \(\ket+\) on the levels \(0,1\).

Worked solution

(a) \(p(a)=\|(\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt3\|^2=\frac23\), leaving \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\); \(p(b)=\frac13\), leaving \(\ket2\). (b) After outcome \(a\), \(H\) maps \((\ket0+\ket1)/\sqrt2\) to \(\ket0\), so the final measurement gives \(0\) with certainty. (c) A test with three separate outcomes would leave \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\) (each with probability \(\frac13\)), and \(H\) would then give \(0\) or \(1\) at random. The rank-two test answered only “is it level \(2\)?” and kept the superposition within the other two levels intact. A measurement disturbs only what it distinguishes.

7. Worked problem §

Find every unitary \(U\) with \(U\ket0=\ket+\). Show that the second column must be \(e^{i\alpha}\ket-\) for some phase \(\alpha\), and use this to explain why no unitary maps both \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) to \(\ket+\).

Hint 1

The columns of a unitary are orthonormal.

Hint 2

In two dimensions, the unit vectors orthogonal to \(\ket+\) are the multiples \(e^{i\alpha}\ket-\).

Worked solution

The first column of \(U\) is \(U\ket0=\ket+\). The second column \(U\ket1=c\ket0+d\ket1\) must be a unit vector orthogonal to \(\ket+\): \(\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(c+d)=0\) gives \(d=-c\), and normalization gives \(|c|=1/\sqrt2\). So \(U\ket1=e^{i\alpha}\ket-\), and \(U=\ket+\bra0+e^{i\alpha}\ket-\bra1\); \(\alpha=0\) gives \(H\). A unitary preserves inner products, and \(\braket01=0\), so the images of \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) must be orthogonal. They cannot both be \(\ket+\).

8. Misconception §

A student computes probabilities by squaring amplitudes without conjugating: \(p_j=v_j^2\). Apply this rule to \(\ket\psi=\frac{1+i}{2}\ket0+\frac{1-i}{2}\ket1\) and compare with the correct rule. What goes wrong, and why is the conjugate needed?

Hint 1

\(\big(\frac{1+i}{2}\big)^2=\frac i2\).

Hint 2

\(|z|^2=zz^*\) is real and nonnegative.

Worked solution

Squaring gives \(\big(\frac{1+i}{2}\big)^2=\frac i2\) and \(\big(\frac{1-i}{2}\big)^2=-\frac i2\): imaginary “probabilities” that add to \(0\). The correct rule gives \(|\frac{1+i}{2}|^2=\frac{1+1}{4}=\frac12\) for each outcome, adding to \(1\). A complex number squared keeps its phase (doubled), while \(zz^*\) removes it and leaves the squared magnitude, which is real and nonnegative. For real amplitudes the two rules agree, which is why the mistake can go unnoticed until complex phases appear.

9. Extension §

With equal priors, Bob must decide whether a qubit was prepared as \(\ket0\) or \(\ket+\). He measures in the basis \(\ket{b_0}=\cos\varphi\ket0+\sin\varphi\ket1\), \(\ket{b_1}=-\sin\varphi\ket0+\cos\varphi\ket1\), guessing \(\ket0\) on \(b_0\) and \(\ket+\) on \(b_1\). (a) Show that the success probability is \(\frac12+\frac14(\cos2\varphi-\sin2\varphi)\). (b) Find the best \(\varphi\) and the success. (c) Compare with measuring in the computational basis.

Hint 1

\(p(b_0\mid0)=\cos^2\varphi\) and \(p(b_1\mid+)=\frac12(\cos\varphi-\sin\varphi)^2\).

Hint 2

\(\cos x-\sin x\) is largest, \(\sqrt2\), at \(x=-\pi/4\).

Worked solution

(a) Success \(=\frac12\big[\cos^2\varphi+\frac12(\cos\varphi-\sin\varphi)^2\big]=\frac12\big[\frac{1+\cos2\varphi}{2}+\frac{1-\sin2\varphi}{2}\big]=\frac12+\frac14(\cos2\varphi-\sin2\varphi)\). (b) It is largest at \(2\varphi=-\pi/4\), \(\varphi=-\pi/8\), giving \(\frac12+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.854\). (c) The computational basis (\(\varphi=0\)) gives \(\frac12+\frac14=0.75\). The best test uses directions placed symmetrically around the two states. No test reaches \(1\), because \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) are not orthogonal; Chapter 8 shows that \(0.854\) is optimal among all measurements.

10. Extension §

The text states that a linear operation preserving the length of every vector must preserve all inner products. Prove it using the identities \(\mathrm{Re}\braket uv=\frac14\big(\|u+v\|^2-\|u-v\|^2\big)\) and \(\mathrm{Im}\braket uv=\frac14\big(\|u-iv\|^2-\|u+iv\|^2\big)\). Then deduce that the columns of such a matrix are orthonormal.

Hint 1

Apply \(U\) to \(u\pm v\) and \(u\pm iv\) and use linearity.

Hint 2

The columns are the images of the orthonormal vectors \(\ket0,\ket1\).

Worked solution

By linearity \(U(u\pm v)=Uu\pm Uv\) and \(U(u\pm iv)=Uu\pm iUv\). Since \(U\) preserves every length, each of the four squared norms in the identities is unchanged when \(u,v\) are replaced by \(Uu,Uv\). Hence both the real and imaginary parts of \(\braket uv\) are preserved: \(\braket{Uu}{Uv}=\braket uv\). The columns of \(U\) are \(U\ket0\) and \(U\ket1\), and since \(\braket00=\braket11=1\) and \(\braket01=0\), the columns are unit vectors with zero inner product. Preserving lengths alone forces the whole geometry to be preserved.
