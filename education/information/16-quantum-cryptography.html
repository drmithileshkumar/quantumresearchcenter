Quantum Cryptography

How can observed quantum statistics support a claim of secrecy?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 7–11 and 15; security definitions and classical processing are introduced in context.

Anchor example: Use a prepare-and-measure key-distribution protocol to identify the information an eavesdropper might obtain.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

16.1 Key Distribution

Alice and Bob want a shared random key that an eavesdropper cannot predict. Quantum transmission can help them estimate how much information may have leaked, but detection of disturbance alone is not a security proof.

16.1.1 Preparation and basis choices

In ideal BB84, Alice chooses a random bit and independently chooses the Z or X basis, sending zero/one or plus/minus accordingly. Bob independently chooses one of those bases. They later announce bases over an authenticated public channel and retain only matching-basis rounds. The transmitted bits remain secret; the basis announcements need not be secret. About half of the ideal rounds survive this sifting when bases are equally likely.

16.1.2 Entanglement-based descriptions

An equivalent ideal description lets Alice prepare a Bell pair, send one half, and measure her own half in a chosen basis. Her outcome prepares the corresponding state remotely, conditioned on the record. This connects key distribution to entanglement verification and error correction. It does not remove source or detector assumptions: an actual prepare-and-measure implementation must meet the conditions used in its proof.

Problem 16.1. Why is the intercept-resend error 1/4 rather than 1/2?

Worked solution

Half of Eve’s basis choices match and cause no bit error on ideal sifted rounds. In the other half, Bob is wrong half the time, giving (1/2)(1/2)=1/4.

16.2 From Data to a Key

16.2.1 Parameter estimation and error correction

They publicly reveal a randomly chosen subset to estimate error rates and discard those test bits from the key. Remaining discrepancies are reconciled by classical communication; that public transcript leaks information and must be included in the security account. Finite samples only bound unobserved errors with a stated failure probability. Zero observed errors in a small sample is not proof of a noiseless or attack-free channel.

16.2.2 Privacy amplification

Privacy amplification compresses the reconciled string with a publicly chosen hash so that the shorter result is nearly uniform and independent of the adversary’s information. The output length depends on a proved entropy bound after accounting for leakage and finite-data penalties. A public hash seed need not be secret, but it must satisfy the assumptions of the chosen extractor or hashing theorem. Choosing an arbitrary short checksum is not a privacy-amplification proof.

Problem 16.2. Can Alice and Bob keep the publicly revealed test bits as secret key?

Worked solution

No. Those bit values are public. They estimate channel statistics and must be excluded from the secret output.

16.3 Security Assumptions

16.3.1 Eavesdropping models and authentication

Consider the intercept-resend attack: Eve chooses Z or X at random, measures, and resends her result. On sifted rounds she chose the wrong basis with probability one half; in those rounds Bob’s bit is wrong with probability one half. The resulting error rate is 1/4. This calculation explains one observable disturbance, but coherent attacks can interact jointly with signals and preserve quantum memory. Security must be proved against the declared adversary class. An authenticated classical channel is essential: otherwise an attacker can impersonate both parties.

16.3.2 Finite data and composable security goals

A composable secrecy goal compares the joint key-adversary state with an ideal uniform independent key: \(\frac12\|\rho_{KE}-\tau_K\otimes\rho_E\|_1\leq\epsilon_{\rm sec}\), with abort probability handled in the protocol’s formal definition. Correctness separately bounds the probability that Alice and Bob disagree. In an ideal asymptotic single-photon BB84 model with suitable symmetry and one-way processing, the familiar rate expression is \(1-2h_2(Q)\) per sifted bit when positive; it is not a universal finite-device key formula. Actual security accounts for preparation, detectors, loss, authentication, error correction, finite statistics, and information leaked during processing. The cited proof explains the ideal reduction; a protocol demonstration alone does not certify a secure implementation.

Problem 16.3. Why is secrecy alone insufficient if Alice’s and Bob’s keys differ?

Worked solution

A key must also be correct for its intended use. Composable definitions separately control disagreement and deviation from uniform independence, with a stated total failure budget.

The course has moved from distinguishable messages to the operational limits of learning, preserving, transmitting, and protecting quantum information. Each rate or security claim depends on the same discipline: define the task, permitted resources, assumptions, and error criterion before asserting what is possible.

Sources and further study: Shor and Preskill, Security of BB84; Renner, Security of Quantum Key Distribution.
