Keep in view: Use a prepare-and-measure key-distribution protocol to identify the information an eavesdropper might obtain.

Predict first. Do zero observed test errors prove that a finite raw key is secret?

Compare your reasoning

No. A sample supports a statistical bound under a specified protocol model. Authentication, reconciliation leakage, and privacy amplification remain necessary.

16.1 Key Distribution

Alice and Bob want a shared random key that an eavesdropper cannot predict. Quantum transmission can help them estimate how much information may have leaked, but detection of disturbance alone is not a security proof.

16.1.1 Preparation and basis choices§

In ideal BB84, Alice chooses a random bit and independently chooses the Z or X basis, sending zero/one or plus/minus accordingly. Bob independently chooses one of those bases. They later announce bases over an authenticated public channel and retain only matching-basis rounds. The transmitted bits remain secret; the basis announcements need not be secret. About half of the ideal rounds survive this sifting when bases are equally likely.

On a matching-basis round, an undisturbed signal gives Bob Alice’s bit with certainty. On a mismatched round, it gives a fair bit. For example, sending zero and measuring X gives plus or minus each with probability one half; those outcomes encode Bob’s recorded values 0 and 1. The random bit, preparation basis, measurement basis, and recorded outcome are four distinct variables.

For 1,000 ideal signals with independent fair basis choices, the expected number of matching rounds is 500. The realized number fluctuates. This is a sifted raw string, not yet a secret final key: parameter tests, reconciliation, verification, and privacy amplification still consume data or reduce its length.

16.1.2 Entanglement-based descriptions§

An equivalent ideal description lets Alice prepare a Bell pair, send one half, and measure her own half in a chosen basis. Her outcome prepares the corresponding state remotely, conditioned on the record. This connects key distribution to entanglement verification and error correction. It does not remove source or detector assumptions: an actual prepare-and-measure implementation must meet the conditions used in its proof.

Problems

Problem 16.1. If the basis choices are independent and fair, what fraction of rounds are sifted, and what is the undisturbed error rate among those rounds?

Worked solution

The two matching choices, ZZ and XX, have probability 1/4 each, so half the rounds survive on average. In the ideal undisturbed model those rounds have zero bit error. This is not yet a final secret-key rate.

16.2 From Data to a Key

16.2.1 Parameter estimation and error correction§

They publicly reveal a randomly chosen subset to estimate error rates and discard those test bits from the key. Remaining discrepancies are reconciled by classical communication; that public transcript leaks information and must be included in the security account. Finite samples only bound unobserved errors with a stated failure probability. Zero observed errors in a small sample is not proof of a noiseless or attack-free channel.

16.2.2 Privacy amplification§

Privacy amplification compresses the reconciled string with a publicly chosen hash so that the shorter result is nearly uniform and independent of the adversary’s information. The output length depends on a proved entropy bound after accounting for leakage and finite-data penalties. A public hash seed need not be secret, but it must satisfy the assumptions of the chosen extractor or hashing theorem. Choosing an arbitrary short checksum is not a privacy-amplification proof.

A small classical example shows why reconciliation leakage counts. Suppose the raw key is one of the four equally likely strings 00, 01, 10, 11. Publishing its parity leaves only two possibilities: either 00 or 11 for parity zero, or 01 or 10 for parity one. The adversary’s uncertainty has dropped from two bits to one. Keeping both bits and calling them secret would ignore the public message.

Likewise, publicly hashing an entirely known string cannot make it secret. Privacy amplification needs uncertainty that remains against the adversary’s actual information, including any stored quantum systems. The entropy bounds and hashing theorem specify how much of that uncertainty can safely become key. The reconciliation example explains a leakage mechanism; it does not supply the full quantum security bound.

Problems

Problem 16.2. Can Alice and Bob keep the publicly revealed test bits as secret key?

Worked solution

No. Those bit values are public. They estimate channel statistics and must be excluded from the secret output.

16.3 Security Assumptions

16.3.1 Eavesdropping models and authentication§

Consider the intercept-resend attack: Eve chooses Z or X at random, measures, and resends her result. On sifted rounds she chose the wrong basis with probability one half; in those rounds Bob’s bit is wrong with probability one half. The resulting error rate is 1/4. This calculation explains one observable disturbance, but coherent attacks can interact jointly with signals and preserve quantum memory. Security must be proved against the declared adversary class. An authenticated classical channel is essential: otherwise an attacker can impersonate both parties.

Check intercept-resend on a sifted Z round. If Alice sent zero, Eve’s Z measurement returns zero and her resend causes no error. Eve’s X measurement returns plus or minus; either resend gives Bob a fair Z result. Averaging Eve’s equally likely choices gives error \(\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=1/4\). By symmetry the same holds for one and for sifted X rounds.

16.3.2 Finite data and composable security goals§

A composable secrecy goal compares the joint key-adversary state with an ideal uniform independent key: \(\frac12\|\rho_{KE}-\tau_K\otimes\rho_E\|_1\leq\epsilon_{\rm sec}\), with abort probability handled in the protocol’s formal definition. Correctness separately bounds the probability that Alice and Bob disagree. In an ideal asymptotic single-photon BB84 model with suitable symmetry and one-way processing, the familiar rate expression is \(1-2h_2(Q)\) per sifted bit when positive; it is not a universal finite-device key formula. Actual security accounts for preparation, detectors, loss, authentication, error correction, finite statistics, and information leaked during processing. The cited proof explains the ideal reduction; a protocol demonstration alone does not certify a secure implementation.

In the ideal asymptotic expression, at \(Q=0.05\), \(h_2(Q)\approx0.2864\), leaving \(1-2h_2(Q)\approx0.4272\) secret bits per sifted bit before any additional implementation costs. At the intercept-resend rate \(Q=1/4\), the expression is negative and supplies no positive key rate. This illustrates the stated ideal bound; it neither models arbitrary hardware nor turns a finite error estimate into a security proof.

The course has moved from distinguishable messages to the operational limits of learning, preserving, transmitting, and protecting quantum information. Each rate or security claim depends on the same discipline: define the task, permitted resources, assumptions, and error criterion before asserting what is possible.

Sources and further study: Shor and Preskill, Security of BB84; Renner, Security of Quantum Key Distribution.

Problems

Problem 16.3. Why is secrecy alone insufficient if Alice’s and Bob’s keys differ?

Worked solution

A key must also be correct for its intended use. Composable definitions separately control disagreement and deviation from uniform independence, with a stated total failure budget.

Transfer problem. In the ideal intercept-resend model, Eve attacks a fraction f of signals independently. Find the expected sifted error rate. Can the same observed error determine f for an arbitrary attack?

Worked solution

An attacked sifted round has error 1/4; unattacked ideal rounds have zero error. Thus the model predicts Q=f/4, for example Q=0.05 when f=0.2. This inference uses the particular independent intercept-resend model. General attacks and device noise can produce the same observed error, so Q does not identify a unique adversary strategy.

Questions and worked problems

These exercises draw on the whole chapter. Attempt each question before opening a hint, explain your reasoning, then compare it with the worked solution.

1. Quick check §

In BB84, Alice and Bob each choose the \(Z\) basis with probability \(0.9\) and the \(X\) basis with probability \(0.1\), independently. What fraction of rounds is sifted? Why might they prefer this to fair basis choices, and what must they still be able to do with the rarely used basis?

Hint 1

Matching probability is \(0.9^2+0.1^2\).

Hint 2

Security relies on testing both bases.

Worked solution

Matching occurs with probability \(0.81+0.01=0.82\), compared with \(0.5\) for fair choices, so far fewer rounds are discarded. The \(X\) rounds are still essential: they are where an attack that measures in \(Z\) shows up as errors. With biased choices, Alice and Bob need enough \(X\)-basis data to estimate its error rate with the required confidence, which the analysis must account for.

2. Quick check §

Evaluate the ideal asymptotic key rate \(1-2h_2(Q)\) per sifted bit for \(Q=0.02\) and \(Q=0.11\). What does the second value suggest about the tolerable error rate in this model?

Hint 1

\(h_2(0.02)\approx0.141\).

Hint 2

\(h_2(0.11)\approx0.500\).

Worked solution

At \(Q=0.02\): \(1-2(0.141)\approx0.717\) secret bits per sifted bit. At \(Q=0.11\): \(1-2(0.500)\approx0\). In this ideal one-way model, no key can be extracted once the error rate reaches about \(11\%\). Finite data, detector imperfections, and authentication costs reduce the usable rate and the tolerable error further.

3. Quick check §

A sifted raw key of \(6\) uniformly random bits is reconciled by publishing the parities of the pairs \((1,2)\), \((3,4)\), and \((5,6)\). How many bits of uncertainty does Eve have left, assuming she knew nothing else? How long a secret key can be extracted?

Hint 1

Each published parity removes one bit of uncertainty.

Hint 2

Choose one bit from each pair.

Worked solution

Each independent parity halves the possible strings, so Eve’s uncertainty falls from \(6\) bits to \(3\). Taking the first bit of each pair gives \(3\) bits that are uniform and independent of the published parities, so a \(3\)-bit key can be extracted. Every bit leaked in public reconciliation must be subtracted from the final key length.

4. Worked problem §

Eve intercepts every signal, always measures in \(Z\), and resends her result. (a) Find the error rate on sifted \(Z\) rounds and on sifted \(X\) rounds. (b) What is the average sifted error rate with fair basis choices? (c) How can Alice and Bob detect this attack more effectively than by the average alone, and how much does Eve learn?

Hint 1

In \(Z\) rounds Eve’s measurement does not disturb the state.

Hint 2

In \(X\) rounds Bob receives a \(Z\) eigenstate and measures \(X\).

Worked solution

(a) On \(Z\) rounds Eve measures the right basis, resends the same state, and causes no error. On \(X\) rounds she resends \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and Bob’s \(X\) measurement is a fair coin: error \(\frac12\). (b) The average is \(\frac14\), the same as the random-basis attack. (c) Estimating the error separately for each basis shows \(0\) in \(Z\) and \(\frac12\) in \(X\), a clear signature. Eve learns every \(Z\) key bit and nothing about \(X\) key bits. Basis-resolved error estimates constrain attacks more tightly than a single average.

5. Worked problem §

In an intercept-resend attack, Eve measures every signal in the intermediate basis \(\ket{b_0}=\cos\frac\pi8\ket0+\sin\frac\pi8\ket1\), \(\ket{b_1}=-\sin\frac\pi8\ket0+\cos\frac\pi8\ket1\), guessing bit \(0\) on \(b_0\), and resends the state she found. (a) For Alice’s \(\ket0\), find the probability that Eve guesses correctly and the error Bob sees in a \(Z\) measurement. (b) Compare with the random \(Z\)/\(X\) intercept-resend attack. (c) What does the comparison show?

Hint 1

\(|\braket{b_0}0|^2=\cos^2\frac\pi8\approx0.854\).

Hint 2

Bob errs if Eve resends \(\ket{b_0}\) and he finds \(1\), or \(\ket{b_1}\) and he finds \(0\).

Worked solution

(a) Eve guesses correctly with probability \(\cos^2\frac\pi8\approx0.854\). Bob’s error is \(\cos^2\frac\pi8\sin^2\frac\pi8+\sin^2\frac\pi8\cos^2\frac\pi8=2(0.854)(0.146)=0.25\). The basis is equally inclined to \(Z\) and \(X\), so \(X\) rounds behave the same way. (b) The random-basis attack also causes \(25\%\) error, but Eve then guesses correctly only \(\frac12\cdot1+\frac12\cdot\frac12=75\%\) of the time. (c) For the same disturbance, a cleverer measurement gives Eve more information. Security proofs must bound what any attack can learn for a given error, not analyse one convenient attack.

6. Worked problem §

Alice and Bob test \(n=100\) randomly chosen sifted bits and find no errors. (a) If the true error rate is \(Q\), what is the probability of seeing no errors? (b) What is the largest \(Q\) that remains consistent with this observation if they accept a failure probability of \(10^{-3}\)? (c) What must the security analysis use in place of “\(Q=0\)”?

Hint 1

\(P(\text{no errors})=(1-Q)^n\).

Hint 2

Solve \((1-Q)^{100}=10^{-3}\).

Worked solution

(a) \((1-Q)^{100}\). (b) Setting \((1-Q)^{100}=10^{-3}\) gives \(Q=1-10^{-0.03}\approx0.067\). Any error rate up to about \(6.7\%\) could produce zero observed errors with probability at least \(10^{-3}\). (c) The analysis must use this upper bound (or a sharper one), together with its failure probability, as the error parameter. With \(Q\approx6.7\%\), the ideal key rate is \(1-2h_2(0.067)\approx0.29\) instead of \(1\). Finite data costs key length even when nothing goes wrong.

7. Worked problem §

A raw key \(x_1x_2x_3x_4\) is uniformly random, and Eve has learned only the parity \(x_1\oplus x_2\). (a) Show that \(x_1\) is uniform and independent of Eve’s information. (b) Give a \(3\)-bit key that is perfectly secret, and explain why \((x_1,x_2,x_3)\) would not be. (c) How does this illustrate privacy amplification?

Hint 1

For each value of the parity, \(x_1\) is still \(0\) or \(1\) with equal probability.

Hint 2

\(x_2=x_1\oplus(x_1\oplus x_2)\).

Worked solution

(a) For either value of \(x_1\oplus x_2\), there are two equally likely pairs, one with \(x_1=0\) and one with \(x_1=1\), so \(x_1\) is uniform and independent of the parity. (b) \((x_1,x_3,x_4)\) is uniform and independent of what Eve knows, so it is perfectly secret. \((x_1,x_2,x_3)\) is not: Eve can compute \(x_1\oplus x_2\) from it, so only \(8/2=4\) of its \(8\) values are possible given her knowledge. (c) Privacy amplification maps the raw key to a shorter string chosen to be independent of the adversary’s information; when Eve knows \(k\) independent parities of an \(n\)-bit uniform key, \(n-k\) secret bits can be extracted. Quantum security proofs supply the corresponding bound when Eve holds quantum information.

8. Misconception §

A student claims: “BB84 is secured by physics, so Alice and Bob do not need an authenticated classical channel.” Describe an attack by an adversary who controls both the quantum channel and an unauthenticated classical channel, and explain what authentication provides.

Hint 1

Can Eve pretend to be Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob?

Hint 2

No-cloning protects the quantum signals only against an outsider.

Worked solution

Eve runs one BB84 session with Alice, pretending to be Bob, and another with Bob, pretending to be Alice, using the unauthenticated channel to answer each party’s basis announcements and tests. Each session shows no disturbance, because Eve is a legitimate endpoint in each. She ends up with one key shared with Alice and another with Bob, and can read and re-encrypt every message. The quantum part guarantees only that an outsider cannot learn a key without causing errors; authentication guarantees that the outsider is not the other endpoint. It requires a short pre-shared secret, so QKD expands a shared key rather than creating one from nothing.

9. Extension §

In the entanglement-based description, suppose Eve copies the \(Z\) value of the photon in transit with a CNOT onto her own qubit, so that Alice, Bob, and Eve share \((\ket{000}+\ket{111})/\sqrt2\). (a) What error rate do Alice and Bob see in \(Z\) rounds, and what does Eve know? (b) Compute \(\langle X_AX_B\rangle\) for this state, and the error rate in \(X\) rounds. (c) What principle does the result illustrate?

Hint 1

\(X_A X_B\) maps \(\ket{000}\) to \(\ket{110}\).

Hint 2

An \(X\)-round error means Alice’s and Bob’s \(\pm\) results differ.

Worked solution

(a) In \(Z\) rounds the three outcomes always agree: no error, and Eve knows every \(Z\) bit. (b) \(X_AX_B\ket{000}=\ket{110}\) and \(X_AX_B\ket{111}=\ket{001}\), both orthogonal to the state, so \(\langle X_AX_B\rangle=0\). Alice’s and Bob’s \(X\) outcomes are uncorrelated, giving error \(\frac12\) in \(X\) rounds. (c) Complementarity: Eve’s perfect knowledge of \(Z\) forces Alice’s and Bob’s \(X\) correlation to vanish. Low error in one basis together with low error in the complementary basis is what limits an eavesdropper, which is why BB84 tests both.

10. Extension §

The six-state protocol uses the \(Z\), \(X\), and \(Y\) bases, with eigenstates \(\ket0,\ket1\), \(\ket\pm\), and \((\ket0\pm i\ket1)/\sqrt2\), chosen with equal probability by Alice and Bob. (a) What fraction of rounds is sifted? (b) In an intercept-resend attack where Eve picks one of the three bases at random, what sifted error rate results? (c) Compare with BB84’s \(\frac14\), and explain the trade-off.

Hint 1

Eve picks the wrong basis with probability \(\frac23\).

Hint 2

A wrong-basis resend gives Bob a fair result in his basis.

Worked solution

(a) Bases match with probability \(\frac13\). (b) Eve chooses the right basis with probability \(\frac13\) (no error) and a wrong one with probability \(\frac23\), after which Bob’s result is a fair coin, so the error is \(\frac23\cdot\frac12=\frac13\). (c) The attack causes \(33\%\) error instead of \(25\%\), so it is easier to detect and the protocol tolerates somewhat more noise. The price is a lower sifting rate, \(\frac13\) instead of \(\frac12\), and a third basis to implement. Using more mutually unbiased bases makes eavesdropping more conspicuous at the cost of efficiency.
