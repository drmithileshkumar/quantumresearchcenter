Before you begin

Keep in view: Attempt to copy two different nonorthogonal states with one fixed device.

Predict first. Can Alice use her choice of Bell-pair measurement basis to send Bob a bit without a classical message?

Compare your reasoning

No. Both choices give Bob I/2 when her outcome is not announced.

9.1 Copying Restrictions

Suppose a machine promises to duplicate any unknown pure state. Test it on two nonorthogonal inputs. Preserving their overlap before and after the operation exposes the contradiction.

9.1.1 No-cloning§

Assume a fixed reversible machine maps \(\ket\psi\ket0\ket e\) to \(\ket\psi\ket\psi\ket{e_\psi}\) and similarly for φ. Unitarity requires \(\braket\psi\phi=(\braket\psi\phi)^2\braket{e_\psi}{e_\phi}\). Taking magnitudes gives s≤s² because the environment overlap is at most one. For \(0<s<1\) this is impossible. Including an environment makes the argument apply to deterministic physical channels as well as a bare two-register unitary.

Test the proposed machine on zero and plus. Their input overlap is \(s=1/\sqrt2\). Two exact copies would have overlap \(s^2=1/2\). Any output apparatus states could only multiply this by a magnitude at most one, so they cannot raise it back to \(1/\sqrt2\). A reversible physical description preserves the full overlap; the proposed behavior cannot have one.

There is also a direct linearity check for the basis copier. CNOT maps \(\ket0\ket0\) to \(\ket{00}\) and \(\ket1\ket0\) to \(\ket{11}\). It must therefore map \(\ket+\ket0\) to \((\ket{00}+\ket{11})/\sqrt2\), whereas two plus copies contain four terms with amplitude one half. A machine correct on the two basis inputs fails on their superposition.

9.1.2 No-broadcasting and mixed states§

Broadcasting asks only that each output marginal equal the supplied mixed state, allowing correlations between outputs. This is weaker than producing an independent tensor-square copy. A set of density operators can be broadcast by one channel exactly when they commute pairwise, meaning \(\rho\sigma=\sigma\rho\) for every pair, a theorem cited below. Commuting states share a diagonal basis; measuring that classical label and copying it gives both requested marginals. Noncommuting ensembles cannot be treated as merely unknown classical distributions.

Problems

Problem 9.1. Why does the overlap proof not forbid copying zero and one?

Worked solution

Their overlap is zero, so the preservation equation reads 0=0. A CNOT with a zero target realizes the copying.

9.2 Locality Constraints

9.2.1 Local operations and remote statistics§

Let Alice apply a local trace-preserving operation with operators Kj. Bob’s expectation of any observable B afterward is \(\sum_j\mathrm{tr}[(K_j^\dagger K_j\otimes B)\rho_{AB}]=\mathrm{tr}[(I\otimes B)\rho_{AB}]\). The completeness relation makes it unchanged. Therefore Alice’s unconditioned local operation cannot alter Bob’s local outcome distribution. Shared entanglement does not remove the need for communication.

9.2.2 No-signalling and conditional predictions§

Conditioning on a particular Alice outcome can change Bob’s predicted state. This does not contradict the previous calculation: conditioning selects a branch rather than summing all branches. Bob needs the classical record to identify which of his trials belongs to that branch. Sorting data afterward can reveal strong correlations without transmitting a message before that record arrives.

For the Bell pair, let Alice choose between a Z measurement and an X measurement. Conditioned on her Z outcome, Bob has zero or one. Conditioned on her X outcome, he has plus or minus. But the two averages are identical:

\[\tfrac12\ket0\bra0+\tfrac12\ket1\bra1=\tfrac12\ket+\bra++\tfrac12\ket-\bra-=I/2.\]

Bob cannot learn Alice’s chosen basis by a more clever local measurement: all effects give \(\mathrm{tr}(EI/2)\) in either case. This is stronger than checking only his Z counts. Alice’s record can later sort the trials into distinct ensembles without changing the unconditioned matrix Bob had before that record arrived.

Problems

Problem 9.2. Does deleting Alice’s measurement record restore Bob’s knowledge of her outcome?

Worked solution

No. It removes the conditioning information. Bob retains the unconditioned reduced state and cannot infer which branch occurred from an arbitrary single local measurement.

9.3 Scope of Impossibility

9.3.1 Restricted input sets§

The cloning argument allows orthogonal inputs because s=0 is consistent. CNOT copies computational-basis states into a zero target, but on plus it creates a Bell state instead of two plus states. A known fixed state can be prepared repeatedly from its description. The impossibility concerns one device acting exactly and deterministically on an unknown member of an unrestricted nonorthogonal set.

A known commuting family shows why the qualifications matter. Suppose the possible inputs are \(\rho_q=q\ket0\bra0+(1-q)\ket1\bra1\). Measure Z and write the result into two output registers. The joint output is \(q\ket{00}\bra{00}+(1-q)\ket{11}\bra{11}\). Each marginal is \(\rho_q\), so this broadcasts the family without knowing \(q\).

The output is generally not \(\rho_q\otimes\rho_q\): its bits always agree, whereas independent samples sometimes disagree. Broadcasting asks for correct marginals and permits these correlations. Exact independent copying would impose an additional demand that this construction does not meet.

9.3.2 Approximate and probabilistic alternatives§

Approximate cloning can trade fidelity against disturbance, and probabilistic exact cloning is possible for suitable restricted linearly independent sets with a declared failure outcome. Neither contradicts deterministic no-cloning. A security or impossibility argument must specify which resources, input sets, approximation, and conditioning are allowed. Proving a restricted impossibility is useful only if the actual protocol satisfies those restrictions.

Entanglement protocols respect these constraints by explicitly consuming shared pairs and sending classical or quantum systems.

Sources and further study: Barnum et al., Noncommuting mixed states cannot be broadcast.

Problems

Problem 9.3. Would a copier for a known single state violate the theorem?

Worked solution

No. There are no conflicting nonorthogonal alternatives to preserve. A known preparation circuit can produce as many copies as its resources permit.

Transfer problem. For the commuting family \(\rho_q\), compare the broadcast output with \(\rho_q\otimes\rho_q\) at q=1/2. Which measurement distinguishes them statistically?

Worked solution

The broadcast output gives only 00 and 11, each with probability one half. The independent tensor-square gives all four bit pairs with probability one quarter. Measure both outputs in Z and compare agreement rates: one versus one half. Both constructions have exactly the same individual marginals.

Questions and worked problems

These exercises draw on the whole chapter. Attempt each question before opening a hint, explain your reasoning, then compare it with the worked solution.

1. Quick check §

For which of these input sets can one fixed unitary (with any environment) copy every member exactly? (a) \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\); (b) \(\{\ket+,\ket-\}\); (c) \(\{\ket0,\ket+\}\); (d) \(\{\ket0,-\ket0\}\). Use the condition \(s\le s^2\) on the overlap magnitude.

Hint 1

The overlap condition allows only \(s=0\) or \(s=1\).

Hint 2

\(\ket0\) and \(-\ket0\) are the same physical state.

Worked solution

(a) and (b) are orthogonal pairs, \(s=0\), so copying is allowed; a CNOT copies (a), and \(H\) followed by CNOT and \(H\otimes H\) copies (b). (c) has \(s=1/\sqrt2\), and \(s\le s^2\) fails: no cloner exists. (d) has \(s=1\): the two vectors describe one physical state, and re-preparing it is trivially possible. Only sets of mutually orthogonal (or identical) states can be cloned deterministically.

2. Quick check §

Can one channel broadcast both members of each family? (a) \(\mathrm{diag}(0.7,0.3)\) and \(\mathrm{diag}(0.2,0.8)\); (b) \(\mathrm{diag}(0.7,0.3)\) and \(\ket+\bra+\). Decide by checking whether the matrices commute.

Hint 1

Diagonal matrices always commute.

Hint 2

Compute \(\rho\sigma\) and \(\sigma\rho\) for (b).

Worked solution

(a) Both are diagonal, so they commute and can be broadcast: measure \(Z\) and write the result into two registers. (b) \(\mathrm{diag}(0.7,0.3)\,\ket+\bra+=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.7\\0.3&0.3\end{pmatrix}\), while the reverse product is \(\frac12\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0.3\\0.7&0.3\end{pmatrix}\). They differ, so the pair does not commute and, by the broadcasting theorem, no channel gives both outputs the correct marginals for both inputs.

3. Quick check §

Alice and Bob share \(\ket{\Phi^+}\). Alice resets her qubit to \(\ket0\) (swap with a fresh ancilla and discard). Find the new joint state and Bob’s reduced state. Has Alice’s drastic operation signalled anything to Bob?

Hint 1

Discarding Alice’s original qubit leaves Bob with its partner’s reduced state.

Hint 2

The new joint state is a product.

Worked solution

After the reset, Alice holds \(\ket0\), unentangled with Bob, and Bob’s qubit is the reduced state of the original pair: \(\rho_B=I/2\). The joint state is \(\ket0\bra0\otimes I/2\). Bob’s statistics are exactly what they were before, even though Alice destroyed the entanglement. Any trace-preserving local operation leaves the other party’s reduced state unchanged, which is the content of no-signalling.

4. Worked problem §

CNOT copies basis states. Use it as an attempted cloner on \(\ket\psi=\cos\theta\ket0+\sin\theta\ket1\) with a blank target. (a) Find the overlap of the output with the ideal two copies \(\ket\psi\ket\psi\), and the fidelity (its squared magnitude). (b) Find the fidelity of one output qubit’s reduced state with \(\ket\psi\). (c) Evaluate both at \(\theta=0\) and \(\theta=\pi/4\).

Hint 1

The output is \(\cos\theta\ket{00}+\sin\theta\ket{11}\).

Hint 2

Each output qubit has reduced state \(\mathrm{diag}(\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta)\).

Worked solution

(a) \(\braket{\psi\psi}{\text{out}}=\cos\theta\cos^2\theta+\sin\theta\sin^2\theta=\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta\), so the fidelity is \((\cos^3\theta+\sin^3\theta)^2\). (b) Each output is \(\mathrm{diag}(\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta)\), with fidelity \(\bra\psi\rho\ket\psi=\cos^4\theta+\sin^4\theta\). (c) At \(\theta=0\) both are \(1\): basis states are copied perfectly. At \(\theta=\pi/4\) (\(\ket+\)) the joint fidelity is \(\big(2\cdot2^{-3/2}\big)^2=\frac12\) and each output has fidelity \(\frac12\): the outputs are maximally mixed rather than copies. A copier designed for one basis does poorly on superpositions.

5. Worked problem §

Alice and Bob share \(\ket\Psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}(\ket{00}+\ket{01}+\ket{11})\). Alice measures in the \(\pm\) basis and does not report the result. (a) Find the probability of each of her outcomes and Bob’s conditional state. (b) Compute Bob’s probability of \(0\) averaged over her outcomes, and compare with his probability before her measurement.

Hint 1

\((\bra+\otimes I)\ket\Psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}(\ket0+2\ket1)\).

Hint 2

\((\bra-\otimes I)\ket\Psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}\ket0\).

Worked solution

(a) Outcome \(+\) has probability \(\frac16(1+4)=\frac56\) and leaves Bob in \((\ket0+2\ket1)/\sqrt5\); outcome \(-\) has probability \(\frac16\) and leaves Bob in \(\ket0\). (b) Bob’s probability of \(0\) is \(\frac56\cdot\frac15+\frac16\cdot1=\frac13\). Before Alice measured, it was \(|\alpha_{00}|^2+|\alpha_{10}|^2=\frac13\). The conditional states are very different, but the average is the same, as the completeness argument of Section 9.2.1 guarantees for every local operation.

6. Worked problem §

Show that perfect cloning would allow signalling. Alice and Bob share many Bell pairs; for each pair, Alice measures either all in \(Z\) or all in \(X\), to send one bit. Suppose Bob could make \(10\) copies of his qubit. What would he observe on the copies in each case, and how could he read Alice’s choice? What does this say about the relation between no-cloning and no-signalling?

Hint 1

If Alice measured \(Z\), Bob’s qubit is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\); if \(X\), it is \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\).

Hint 2

Measure all copies in \(Z\) and see whether they agree.

Worked solution

If Alice measured \(Z\), Bob’s qubit is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and ten copies measured in \(Z\) all give the same result. If she measured \(X\), his qubit is \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\), and ten \(Z\) measurements give independent fair bits, all agreeing with probability only \(2\cdot2^{-10}\approx0.002\). So Bob could read Alice’s choice, and hence a message, instantly, with an error under \(1\%\). Since no-signalling holds for every local operation, a perfect cloner cannot exist. No-cloning is part of what keeps quantum correlations from carrying messages without communication.

7. Worked problem §

For the commuting family \(\rho_q=q\ket0\bra0+(1-q)\ket1\bra1\), the broadcaster measures \(Z\) and writes the result into two registers. For \(q=0.8\), compute the probability that the two outputs agree, and compare with two independent samples from \(\rho_q\otimes\rho_q\). Find the mutual information between the two outputs in each case.

Hint 1

The broadcast outputs always agree.

Hint 2

Independent samples agree with probability \(q^2+(1-q)^2\).

Worked solution

The broadcast output \(0.8\ket{00}\bra{00}+0.2\ket{11}\bra{11}\) gives agreeing bits with probability \(1\), while independent samples agree with probability \(0.64+0.04=0.68\). The broadcast outputs share \(h_2(0.2)\approx0.722\) bits of mutual information (each output determines the other), while independent copies share none. Both have the correct marginal \(\rho_q\). Broadcasting reproduces each part correctly but correlates the parts, which is why it is weaker than copying.

8. Misconception §

A student argues: “No-cloning says a quantum state cannot exist in two places, so teleportation, which makes Bob’s qubit equal to Alice’s input, must violate it.” Explain why it does not, describing what happens to Alice’s system during the protocol.

Hint 1

After Alice’s measurement, what state does her input qubit hold?

Hint 2

Is there ever a moment when two systems both hold the unknown state?

Worked solution

In teleportation Alice measures her input qubit together with her half of the Bell pair. After the measurement, her qubits are in definite basis states that carry no information about the input amplitudes. Bob’s qubit becomes the input state only after he applies the correction selected by her classical message. At no time do two systems both hold the unknown state, so nothing is copied: the state is transferred, and the original is consumed. No-cloning forbids duplication, not relocation.

9. Extension §

A measure-and-prepare cloner measures \(Z\) and prepares two copies of the outcome state. (a) Find the fidelity of each copy with the input for \(\ket0\), \(\ket1\), \(\ket\pm\), and \((\ket0\pm i\ket1)/\sqrt2\). (b) Average over these six inputs. (c) Compare with the known optimal universal cloner, whose copies have fidelity \(\frac56\) for every input.

Hint 1

For \(\ket+\), each copy is \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\) with probability \(\frac12\).

Hint 2

Fidelity of \(\ket j\) with \(\ket\psi\) is \(|\braket j\psi|^2\).

Worked solution

(a) For \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) the copies are perfect: fidelity \(1\). For the other four inputs, the copies are \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\) at random, each with fidelity \(\frac12\) with the input. (b) The average is \(\frac16(1+1+4\cdot\frac12)=\frac23\). (c) The optimal universal cloner of Bužek and Hillery reaches \(\frac56\) for every input, without favoring a basis. Approximate cloning is possible, but with fidelity strictly below \(1\); better cloners must keep the copies entangled rather than measuring first.

10. Extension §

Construct a probabilistic exact cloner for \(\{\ket0,\ket+\}\) from unambiguous discrimination (Chapter 8): on a conclusive outcome, prepare two copies of the identified state; otherwise declare failure. (a) What is its success probability? (b) Why does it not violate no-cloning? (c) The optimal probabilistic cloner for two states with overlap \(s\) succeeds with probability \(1/(1+s)\). Evaluate it and compare.

Hint 1

Unambiguous discrimination succeeds with probability \(1-s\).

Hint 2

No-cloning forbids deterministic exact copying.

Worked solution

(a) With \(s=1/\sqrt2\), unambiguous discrimination succeeds with probability \(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\approx0.293\), and on success Bob knows the state and prepares as many copies as he likes. (b) The procedure sometimes fails and says so; no-cloning forbids only an exact copier that always succeeds on every member of a nonorthogonal set. (c) \(1/(1+s)\approx0.586\), the Duan–Guo bound, which is achieved by a cloner that does not first identify the state. Asking for exactly two copies, rather than full knowledge, roughly doubles the success probability.
