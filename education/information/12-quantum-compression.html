Quantum Compression

How many qubits are needed to preserve the messages of a quantum source?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 2, 5, and 11; typical subspaces and fidelity are introduced in context.

Anchor example: Compress repeated signals drawn from a biased qubit ensemble.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 12.1 Quantum Sources
  2. 12.2 Typical Subspaces
  3. 12.3 Compression Limits

12.1 Quantum Sources

A source emits pure quantum signals independently, but its average state has eigenvalues 3/4 and 1/4. Sending one qubit per signal works. Can a long block be stored in fewer qubits while preserving the signals, including their coherence with other systems?

12.1.1 Source ensembles and average states

An ensemble \(\{p_x,\ket{\psi_x}\}\) has average state \(\rho=\sum_xp_x\ket{\psi_x}\bra{\psi_x}\). For independent identical uses, the average block state is \(\rho^{\otimes n}\). The eigenbasis of ρ need not coincide with the source’s prepared states. Compression should preserve the quantum signal, not merely the classical label of an eigenbasis measurement. Measuring each signal first can destroy the information the task asks us to preserve.

12.1.2 Faithful recovery and fidelity

For a pure target ψ and recovered state σ, the squared-fidelity convention is \(F=\bra\psi\sigma\ket\psi\), equal to one for exact recovery. For general states use \(F(\rho,\sigma)=(\mathrm{tr}\sqrt{\sqrt\rho\sigma\sqrt\rho})^2\). We specify the square because another common convention takes its square root. Preserving entanglement with a reference is a strong recovery criterion: the encoder should not secretly measure and reconstruct an incompatible approximation.

Problem 12.1. For n=3 and eigenvalue 1/4 for label one, what total probability lies on strings with zero or one ones?

Worked solution

It is \((3/4)^3+3(1/4)(3/4)^2=54/64=0.84375\). These four strings span a space that can be labeled with two qubits, but the discarded weight is still substantial. This is a finite illustration, not a high-fidelity asymptotic guarantee.

12.2 Typical Subspaces

12.2.1 Repeated sources and typicality

In the eigenbasis of ρ, each n-letter string has probability \(\lambda_{j_1}\cdots\lambda_{j_n}\). Its information per letter is the average of −log₂λj. For independent repetitions, most weight lies on strings whose information per letter is within δ of S(ρ). Their span is the typical subspace. Each typical string has probability at least \(2^{-n(S+\delta)}\), so there are at most \(2^{n(S+\delta)}\) of them. Their total probability tends to one as n grows.

12.2.2 Encoding and decoding

Assign orthogonal compressed labels to the typical eigenstrings and extend this assignment linearly. The decoder reverses that embedding. A projection distinguishing typical from atypical space can be part of the construction, but individual typical strings must not be measured: their superpositions need to remain coherent. For atypical outcomes use a failure procedure. The high retained weight supplies the fidelity guarantee as the block length grows.

Problem 12.2. Why not measure the typical string and send its index classically?

Worked solution

That would preserve the eigenbasis label but destroy superpositions of different strings and their coherence with a reference. Quantum compression preserves the state in the subspace.

12.3 Compression Limits

12.3.1 Schumacher compression

Schumacher compression states that, for a finite-dimensional memoryless quantum source under the standard asymptotically faithful quantum recovery criterion, the entropy S(ρ) is the compression rate in qubits per signal. Rates above it are achievable and rates below it cannot maintain asymptotically faithful recovery. The typical-space argument explains achievability’s geometry; the full converse and fidelity analysis are theorem-level results cited below. For eigenvalues 3/4,1/4 the limiting rate is about 0.811 qubits per signal.

12.3.2 Rates, errors, and assumptions

At finite n, integer dimensions, atypical probability, and circuit costs matter. Entropy does not promise that a three-signal block fits faithfully into 2.433 physical qubits. If the source has memory, is unknown, or must preserve every possible input exactly rather than typical source behavior, the stated theorem does not apply unchanged. A compressible source also does not imply that an arbitrary computation can discard most of its qubits safely.

Problem 12.3. What compression rate follows for the maximally mixed qubit source?

Worked solution

Its entropy is one, so there is no asymptotic reduction below one qubit per signal under faithful quantum recovery.

Typical subspaces also explain how many weakly entangled pairs can supply a smaller number of nearly perfect Bell pairs.

Sources and further study: Schumacher, Quantum coding.
