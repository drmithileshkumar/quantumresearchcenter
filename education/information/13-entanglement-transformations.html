Entanglement Transformations

Which shared states can be converted into others using only local actions and classical messages?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 4–5 and 10–12; Schmidt coefficients and majorization are developed through conversions.

Anchor example: Compare conversion of a partially entangled pure pair into Bell pairs and the reverse task.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 13.1 Allowed Operations
  2. 13.2 Pure-State Resources
  3. 13.3 Mixed-State Resources

13.1 Allowed Operations

Alice and Bob share √p|00〉+√(1−p)|11〉, with p at least one half. Can local actions turn this into a Bell pair? The answer changes depending on whether success must be certain, failures may be declared, or many copies are available.

13.1.1 Local operations and classical communication

Local operations and classical communication, abbreviated LOCC, allow each party to act on their own systems and exchange classical outcomes. They do not include sending a quantum system or applying a joint gate across the separation. Local unitaries can change basis but cannot alter the amount of pure-state entanglement. Measurements can change it in selected branches, so branch probabilities must be included.

13.1.2 Schmidt coefficients and conversion criteria

A pure joint state can be written \(\sum_j\sqrt{\lambda_j}\ket{u_j}\ket{v_j}\) in orthonormal local bases. This Schmidt form follows by diagonalizing a reduced density matrix and choosing corresponding partner vectors; the λj are its eigenvalues. Nielsen’s deterministic pure-state conversion criterion is \(\lambda(\psi)\prec\lambda(\phi)\) for conversion ψ→φ: each partial sum of the largest input coefficients must be no larger than the corresponding output sum. The sums agree at one. Thus (1/2,1/2) can become (p,1−p), but the reverse is impossible deterministically for p>1/2.

Problem 13.1. At p=0.9, what is the filter’s success probability?

Worked solution

It is 2(0.1)=0.2. The successful state is maximally entangled, but four fifths of trials fail, so selecting successes does not create free entanglement.

13.2 Pure-State Resources

13.2.1 Concentration

A probabilistic filter on Alice gives a concrete alternative. Set \(K=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{(1-p)/p},1)\). On success it changes the joint state to \(\sqrt{1-p}(\ket{00}+\ket{11})\), a Bell pair after normalization, with probability 2(1−p). The complementary failure operator \(L=\mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{(2p-1)/p},0)\) satisfies K†K+L†L=I. At p=3/4, half the trials succeed and half leave a product state. The successful branch alone would overstate the resource gain.

13.2.2 Dilution and asymptotic rates

For many independent copies of a bipartite pure state, concentration produces Bell pairs at asymptotic rate h₂(p); dilution uses Bell pairs at the same asymptotic rate to prepare the state, with errors tending to zero and classical communication available. The typical Schmidt strings carry this rate, closely related to compression. This asymptotic reversibility differs from the single-copy deterministic restriction. It does not provide an exact finite-block conversion with fractional Bell pairs.

Problem 13.2. Can a product state with Schmidt vector (1,0) become a Bell pair deterministically by LOCC?

Worked solution

No. The first majorization inequality would require 1≤1/2. The filter also gives zero success as p approaches one.

13.3 Mixed-State Resources

13.3.1 Distillation and formation

A mixed state is separable if it is a convex mixture of product states; otherwise it is entangled. Distillable entanglement asks for the best Bell-pair rate obtainable from many copies by a specified LOCC protocol class. Entanglement cost asks how many Bell pairs are needed to create those copies. An inaccessible environmental record can make reversing a mixed-state preparation much harder than reversing a pure-state conversion.

13.3.2 Irreversibility and limitations

For mixed states, creation and distillation rates need not agree. Bound entanglement gives examples that are entangled but cannot supply Bell pairs by the allowed distillation operations. These are theorem-level existence statements, not consequences of the two-coefficient filter. Always specify whether conversion is deterministic, stochastic, approximate, asymptotic, and one-way or two-way assisted. “More entangled” is not a complete ordering of all state-conversion tasks.

Problem 13.3. Why does an asymptotic rate not determine a one-copy conversion probability?

Worked solution

The rate describes collective protocols on many copies with vanishing approximation error. A finite-copy protocol has different success and exactness constraints.

Preserving entanglement under noise leads to quantum error correction, where the environment must not learn the logical state.

Sources and further study: Nielsen, Conditions for entanglement transformations; Bennett et al., Concentrating partial entanglement; Horodecki et al., Mixed-state entanglement and irreversibility.
