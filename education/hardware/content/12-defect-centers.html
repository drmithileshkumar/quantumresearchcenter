12.1 Localized States

A localized electron can be a processor, a nearby nucleus a memory, and an emitted photon a connection. The useful object is then a small quantum node.

12.1.1 Defects and spin levels

A defect changes the local electronic structure of a crystal. The nitrogen-vacancy center in diamond is one example with an electronic spin manifold and optical transitions. Two selected spin levels define the qubit; other levels are not removed by this choice. Magnetic fields and spectral selectivity help isolate the transition, and their stability affects the computational phase.

12.1.2 Initialization and microwave control

Optical pumping can polarize the electronic spin through state-dependent relaxation pathways. Microwave pulses rotate a selected spin transition. Fluorescence contrast supplies a measurement signal, though collecting enough information may require repetition or more elaborate readout. Optical excitation also changes the environment of nearby memories; the reset procedure must be evaluated as part of the node, not only on the electron alone.

Problem 12.1. Two independent required photons are each collected with probability 0.1. What is the collection contribution to success?

Worked solution

It is 0.1² = 0.01 before detector efficiency, protocol acceptance, or indistinguishability is included. This is a two-photon example, not a formula for every heralding protocol.

Further study: Bernien et al., heralded entanglement between solid-state qubits.

12.2 Spin Registers

12.2.1 Nuclear memories

A nearby nuclear spin can retain information while the electron participates in optical attempts. Hyperfine coupling makes each spin’s transition frequency depend on the other. This supplies conditional control but also means that uncontrolled electron changes can disturb the nucleus. A memory lifetime measured with an idle electron need not describe a memory exposed to repeated optical reset.

12.2.2 Conditional operations

In a simplified secular two-spin model, \(H=\hbar A Z_eZ_n/4\), where \(A\) is an angular frequency and fast nonsecular terms have been neglected. The phase of a nuclear superposition evolves differently for the two electron states. Resonant selective pulses or timed evolution can turn that difference into an entangling gate. Calibration must identify the relevant coupling and compensate unwanted single-spin phases.

Problem 12.2. What is the relative conditional phase between even and odd parity under the stated interaction?

Worked solution

The energies are \(\pm\hbar A/4\), so their phase difference has magnitude \(At/2\). Single-spin phases and the sign convention must be tracked when specifying a target gate.

12.3 Optical Interfaces

12.3.1 Readout and heralded entanglement

Photons from two nodes can interfere in a way that erases information about which node emitted them. A suitable detection event then heralds entanglement. This requires indistinguishable optical wavepackets and a specified protocol; a detector click alone is not a universal entanglement witness. Collection loss reduces success rate, while distinguishability and false detections can reduce the conditional state fidelity.

12.3.2 Collection efficiency and scaling

A scalable network needs reproducible optical transitions, collection interfaces, stable interference, local memories, and control of unwanted spin interactions. Local register size and remote connection rate are separate resources. The experiment should report failed attempts and waiting time along with successful-state fidelity. Postselection cannot turn a rare high-quality event into a fast deterministic connection.

Problem 12.3. Why benchmark a memory during link attempts?

Worked solution

Optical excitation and reset can perturb the memory. Its idle coherence time does not capture those repeated disturbances; the relevant experiment reproduces the actual network duty cycle.
