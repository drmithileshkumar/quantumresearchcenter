4.1 Interaction Models

Two qubits can each be well controlled yet remain computationally separate. To entangle them, the energy or transition of one must depend on the state of the other, or both must interact through a shared mediator.

Predict before calculating. Two devices perform the correct truth table on 00, 01, 10, and 11. Must their two-qubit gates agree?

Compare your reasoning

No. Their phases can differ. A gate must also act correctly on superpositions; this chapter tracks the conditional phase.

4.1.1 Conditional energies

Use the basis order 00, 01, 10, 11. For \(H=\hbar JZ\otimes Z/2\), multiplying the two Z eigenvalues gives energies \(+\hbar J/2,-\hbar J/2,-\hbar J/2,+\hbar J/2\). Each basis amplitude therefore gains its corresponding energy phase.

Could those phases come from separate one-qubit operations? Local diagonal phases have the form \(\phi_{xy}=a_x+b_y\). Their combination \(\phi_{00}-\phi_{01}-\phi_{10}+\phi_{11}\) cancels identically. For the interaction it equals \(-2Jt\), which is generally nonzero modulo \(2\pi\). This invariant identifies the conditional phase that local phase corrections cannot remove. It also explains why a basis-state population test misses the interaction completely: every basis population remains unchanged.

4.1.2 Exchange interactions

Apply \(XX+YY\) to the four basis states. On 00 and 11 the X and Y contributions cancel. On 01 and 10 they add, giving \(2\ket{10}\) and \(2\ket{01}\). Thus \(H=\hbar g(XX+YY)/2\) acts as \(\hbar gX\) in the one-excitation subspace.

Exponentiating that two-state matrix gives \(\ket{01}\mapsto\cos(gt)\ket{01}-i\sin(gt)\ket{10}\). Full population exchange occurs at \(gt=\pi/2\), but the phase \(-i\) matters when other basis amplitudes are present. At half that time, the result is entangled. If \(g/(2\pi)=5\) MHz, those times are 50 ns and 25 ns. This convention uses \(g\) as an angular frequency; the Heisenberg exchange energy used for semiconductor spins in Chapter 11 is defined differently.

Problem 4.1. For the exchange model, when is population fully transferred from 01 to 10?

Worked solution

At gt=π/2, the state is −i|10〉. Half that duration produces equal populations with a relative phase.

4.2 Entangling Gates

4.2.1 Controlled phases

For \(Jt=\pi/2\), the ZZ evolution is \(e^{-i\pi/4}\operatorname{diag}(1,i,i,1)\). A subsequent \(R_z(-\pi/2)\) on each qubit supplies, relative to its 00 entry, factors \((1,-i,-i,-1)\). Multiplying entries leaves \(\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,-1)\): controlled-Z, apart from a common phase.

To see the entanglement, apply CZ to \(\ket{++}\). The amplitudes become \((1,1,1,-1)/2\). A product state would require the determinant of its two-by-two amplitude table to vanish. Here it is \((-1-1)/4=-1/2\), so the state cannot factor. These are two distinct checks: the phase calculation certifies the intended unitary, and the amplitude table demonstrates what it can do to a superposition.

4.2.2 Mediated interactions

A mediator allows qubits to interact without a direct data-data term. Consider a harmonic mode with lowering operator \(a\) and a force depending on a data observable \(S\). In a rotating description, \(H/\hbar=gS(ae^{-i\delta t}+a^\dagger e^{i\delta t})\). On an S eigenstate of value \(s\), the oscillator is displaced by an amplitude proportional to \(s(g/\delta)(1-e^{i\delta t})\).

At \(T=2\pi/|\delta|\), that displacement returns to zero for every \(s\). The closed path can still leave a phase proportional to \(S^2\), which contains a two-qubit interaction when \(S\) is a sum of single-qubit observables. The exact coefficient and sign depend on the force convention. The robust requirement is that the final mediator state not reveal the logical branch; Chapter 9 develops a specific motion-mediated model.

Problem 4.2. Can separate Z rotations erase every ZZ conditional phase?

Worked solution

No. They change individual phases but leave the nonlocal combination unchanged modulo 2π.

4.3 Gate Performance

4.3.1 Residual entanglement

Suppose an intended superposition instead ends as \((\ket{00}\ket{e_0}+\ket{11}\ket{e_1})/\sqrt2\). When the mediator is not measured, the data state has off-diagonal element \(\bra{00}\rho\ket{11}=\langle e_1|e_0\rangle/2\). This follows by expanding the outer product and tracing over a complete mediator basis.

If the two mediator states are identical, the overlap is one and coherence remains. Orthogonal mediator states give zero overlap and remove the interference. For oscillator coherent states separated by displacement \(\Delta\alpha\), the magnitude of the overlap is \(e^{-|\Delta\alpha|^2/2}\). Even a small failure to close a phase-space trajectory can therefore produce an observable contrast reduction, despite correct data populations.

4.3.2 Speed and unwanted coupling

Reducing the gate duration \(t\) can lower relaxation exposure, approximately \(t/T_1\), while requiring a stronger control. A simple illustrative competition is \(\epsilon(t)=at+b/t^2\), with \(a\) in s⁻¹ and \(b\) in s². The first term models decoherence; the second models an unwanted off-resonant process growing with drive strength for a fixed pulse area.

Differentiating gives \(d\epsilon/dt=a-2b/t^3\), so this toy model has minimum at \(t=(2b/a)^{1/3}\). It is not a universal pulse-design law, but exposes why “as fast as possible” need not mean “as accurate as possible.” A useful optimization must also respect spectator coupling, permitted simultaneous gates, leakage, and the control hardware’s bandwidth.

Problem 4.3. Why must an auxiliary oscillator close its trajectory?

Worked solution

If its final state depends on the data, tracing it out reduces coherence. Closing removes the distinguishing record while allowing a geometric phase to remain.

We now have candidate gates. Initialization and measurement determine whether their inputs and outputs can be used reliably.

Worked model check. A mediator ends with coherent-state displacement difference 0.2 between two data branches. What fraction of their coherence magnitude remains?

Worked solution

The overlap magnitude is \(e^{-0.2^2/2}=e^{-0.02}\simeq0.9802\). Correct populations would not expose this contrast loss.
