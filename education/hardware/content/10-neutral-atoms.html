10.1 Atom Arrays

An atom excited to a large electronic orbit can shift the excitation energy of its neighbour. We can use that shift to make a pulse conditional.

10.1.1 Optical traps and loading

A tightly focused laser produces an optical trapping potential for a neutral atom. Arrays of such traps provide labelled sites, but loading may leave vacancies. Imaging locates occupied sites and rearrangement assembles a useful array. These operations are part of initialization: a missing atom is not simply a qubit in state zero, and occupancy must be tracked separately from internal state.

10.1.2 Internal-state encoding

Long-lived internal states store the qubit, initialized by optical pumping and manipulated with microwave or Raman transitions. A Raman process uses two optical fields to drive an effective transition through an intermediate level. Detuning can reduce intermediate-state occupation, but spontaneous scattering and laser phase noise remain. Trap motion and spatially varying light shifts also affect the qubit frequency.

Problem 10.1. If sites load independently with probability 0.6, what is the probability that ten prescribed sites are occupied?

Worked solution

It is 0.6¹⁰ ≈ 0.00605. This toy calculation explains why rearrangement can matter even when individual loading looks reasonably successful.

10.2 Rydberg Gates

10.2.1 Excitation and blockade

A laser couples \(\ket1\) to a Rydberg state \(\ket r\). When both atoms are excited, the interaction shifts \(\ket{rr}\) by energy \(V\). Define the angular-frequency blockade shift \(B=V/\hbar\). If \(|B|\gg\Omega\), excitation of the second atom is strongly suppressed. In a simple detuned two-level model the maximum unwanted transfer is \(\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+B^2)\); actual multilevel gate errors require a fuller model.

10.2.2 Conditional phase accumulation

A representative sequence excites the control with a π pulse, applies a 2π pulse to the target transition, then de-excites the control. Whether the target completes its cycle depends on blockade. The resulting diagonal phases can realize an entangling controlled phase after single-qubit phase corrections. Follow all four computational inputs, including pulse phases, before calling the sequence CZ; blockade alone does not specify a complete gate.

Problem 10.2. For \(B=20\Omega\), bound transfer in the simple blockade model.

Worked solution

The maximum is \(1/401\approx0.00249\). It is not the total error of a Rydberg gate: decay, pulse errors, and other levels remain.

10.3 Readout and Scaling

10.3.1 Detection and atom loss

Fluorescence detects atoms, and state-selective removal can convert an internal-state question into an occupancy question. Such readout may be destructive. Other schemes preserve atoms, with their own scattering and crosstalk budgets. Loss during a circuit must be distinguished from a legitimate measurement result; detecting it can supply a useful error flag but does not recover the missing quantum state automatically.

10.3.2 Rearrangement and parallel control

Larger arrays require uniform illumination, stable traps, controlled Rydberg excitation, and reliable loading. Finite Rydberg lifetime penalizes slow gates, while imperfect blockade penalizes strong drives. Parallel gates can interact with one another. An architecture must specify which pairs can operate simultaneously, how vacant sites are replaced, and whether a replacement interrupts nearby stored information.

Problem 10.3. Why should detected atom loss not be recorded as measurement zero?

Worked solution

That would merge an erasure with a valid state outcome and bias the data. Record occupancy or a separate loss flag and define how the computation handles it.

In a semiconductor, electrostatic confinement plays the role of the trap, while electron spin supplies the two states.
