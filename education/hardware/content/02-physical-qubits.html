2.1 Energy Levels

A device has energy levels E0, E1, and E2. We want to rotate only the lowest two. The third level matters even when the circuit drawing has only one wire: a drive can accidentally put amplitude there.

Predict before calculating. A drive is resonant with the first transition of an equally spaced ladder. Is that enough to isolate two levels?

Compare your reasoning

No. The next transition has the same frequency. Nonlinearity, selection rules, or a different control strategy are needed.

2.1.1 Stationary states and frequencies

The dot over an amplitude denotes its instantaneous rate of change. A stationary energy state keeps the same outcome probabilities while its complex amplitude turns in phase. We take the Schrödinger evolution law as a physical postulate, then calculate what it predicts for the device; it is not derived from circuit notation.

Why does an energy difference set an oscillation frequency? For a stationary level \(j\), the equation \(i\hbar\dot c_j=E_jc_j\) says that its amplitude changes at a rate proportional to its energy. A function whose derivative is proportional to itself is an exponential, giving \(c_j(t)=c_j(0)e^{-iE_jt/\hbar}\). In a two-level superposition the ratio of amplitudes gains phase \(e^{-i(E_1-E_0)t/\hbar}\).

Thus \(\omega_{01}=(E_1-E_0)/\hbar\) is an angular frequency, whereas \(f_{01}=(E_1-E_0)/h\) counts cycles per second. Adding the same energy to every level multiplies the whole state by a common phase and changes no probabilities. A 5 GHz splitting produces a full relative-phase cycle in 0.2 ns. The frequency describes phase evolution, not a classical particle jumping back and forth between levels.

2.1.2 The two-level approximation

A real device has more than two states. Let \(P=\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1\) project onto the chosen pair, and \(Q=I-P\) onto the rest. Split the state into \(P\ket\psi\) and \(Q\ket\psi\). The Schrödinger equation gives

\[i\hbar\,\frac{d(P\ket\psi)}{dt}=PHP\ket\psi+PHQ\ket\psi.\]

The first term is the desired qubit model. The second is its coupling to omitted levels; declaring a qubit does not set this term to zero. If the omitted transition is far from the drive frequency and weakly coupled, its amplitude may remain small.

For equally spaced levels, \(\omega_{12}=\omega_{01}\), so frequency selection cannot separate the first two transitions. Define anharmonicity \(\alpha=\omega_{12}-\omega_{01}\). Selective pulses generally require drive and spectral-width scales small compared with the relevant unwanted detunings, not merely small compared with the carrier frequency.

Problem 2.1. If E1−E0=hf, what relative phase accumulates in time 1/(2f)?

Worked solution

The excited component gains exp(−iπ)=−1 relative to the ground component. This is half a cycle, not half a radian.

2.2 Qubit Encodings

2.2.1 Computational subspaces

The encoding is an assignment of physical states to logical labels. It can use energy levels, different paths of one photon, or states of several particles. Once chosen, the same physical operator may have different logical meanings. An energy shift on one state of a two-path photon acts as a relative phase; losing that photon removes it from the entire encoded subspace.

For a three-level device, a measurement that resolves all levels reports \(p_0,p_1,p_2\), with sum one. Define leakage \(L=p_2\). A procedure that discards level-2 outcomes instead reports \(p_1/(1-L)\). If \((p_0,p_1,p_2)=(0.45,0.45,0.10)\), the retained sample looks exactly balanced although one tenth of the population leaked. Reporting both the unconditional probabilities and the retained fraction prevents that ambiguity.

2.2.2 Leakage and selection rules

A drive couples levels through an operator \(D\). Its transition strength depends on \(\bra mD\ket n\), the corresponding matrix entry in the energy basis. If this entry vanishes, that particular control does not directly couple those states. A different field or broken symmetry can restore the coupling, so a selection rule must name the operator and the symmetry involved.

For one unwanted transition with detuning \(\Delta\) and drive strength \(\Omega\), use the two-state matrix \(H/\hbar=(\Delta Z+\Omega X)/2\). Squaring it gives \(H^2/\hbar^2=(\Delta^2+\Omega^2)I/4\), because \(XZ+ZX=0\). The exponential then yields a transition probability bounded by \(\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)\). At \(|\Delta|=10\Omega\), the bound is \(1/101\). Multiple unwanted levels, pulse edges, and repeated gates can invalidate this isolated-transition estimate.

Problem 2.2. Does a zero computational-basis error guarantee no leakage?

Worked solution

No. If leaked outcomes are ignored or misclassified, the reported bit statistics can conceal population outside the chosen subspace.

2.3 Effective Dynamics

2.3.1 Hamiltonians and rotating frames

With \(Z=\ket0\bra0-\ket1\bra1\), energies \(0,\hbar\omega_{01}\) give \(H_0=\hbar\omega_{01}(I-Z)/2\). Dropping the common energy leaves \(-\hbar\omega_{01}Z/2\). This sign follows from labeling level 1 as the higher-energy state.

A rotating frame removes a chosen reference evolution. Write \(\ket\psi=R(t)\ket{\psi_r}\), where \(R=e^{-iH_{\rm ref}t/\hbar}\). Differentiating this product gives

\[H_r=R^\dagger HR-i\hbar R^\dagger\dot R.\]

For \(H_{\rm ref}=-\hbar\omega_d Z/2\), the free part becomes \(-\hbar(\omega_{01}-\omega_d)Z/2\). The difference frequency is the detuning. The frame is a coordinate choice; changing the physical drive frequency is an experimental action. Keeping those statements separate prevents fictitious gates from appearing in a calculation.

2.3.2 Testing the approximation

Test the truncated model against predictions that it did not fit. Estimate a transition frequency with weak spectroscopy, calibrate a rotation at one amplitude, then predict its response at another amplitude and detuning. A systematic amplitude-dependent frequency shift or an extra oscillation can reveal omitted levels or drive corrections.

Also ask how an error accumulates. An unwanted amplitude of size \(\epsilon\) has population \(|\epsilon|^2\) after one isolated pulse. If successive pulses add that amplitude with the same phase, after \(N\) pulses it can be of order \(N\epsilon\), giving population of order \(N^2|\epsilon|^2\) while this remains small. Random phases can produce different scaling. A small one-pulse leakage value is therefore insufficient to predict a long circuit without a phase and reset model.

Problem 2.3. Why is an equally spaced oscillator not automatically a selective two-level qubit?

Worked solution

A resonant field can couple several neighboring transitions at the same frequency. A usable encoding needs additional nonlinearity, selection, or control structure.
