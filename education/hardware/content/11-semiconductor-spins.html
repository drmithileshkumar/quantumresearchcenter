11.1 Spin Qubits

Can an electrical device use a spin to store information and charge motion only when it needs to operate on that information?

11.1.1 Confinement and Zeeman splitting

Gate electrodes create a small confining potential called a quantum dot. A single electron has orbital levels and two spin states split by a magnetic field. Their separation is \(\Delta E=|g|\mu_B B\), where \(g\) is the material-dependent effective factor. Label the lower selected spin state zero and the higher one one; the sign of \(g\) determines their physical orientation.

11.1.2 Spin encodings

One electron can encode one qubit. Other encodings use two or more spins to trade extra particles for different control or noise properties. Initialization may use relaxation or spin-selective tunnelling to a reservoir. The device must resolve spin and unwanted orbital or valley states sufficiently well; merely counting electrons does not establish a clean two-level computational subspace.

Problem 11.1. For \(|g|=2\), \(B=0.5\) T, and \(\mu_B/h\approx14\) GHz/T, find the spin frequency.

Worked solution

The frequency is 2 × 14 × 0.5 = 14 GHz. This is an illustrative effective-g model, not a universal value for semiconductor devices.

11.2 Electrical Control

11.2.1 Spin rotations

A resonant oscillating magnetic field rotates a spin. Electrical driving can also move the electron through a magnetic-field gradient or exploit spin–orbit coupling, converting electrical motion into spin control. This convenience couples the qubit to charge fluctuations as well. Drive strength, detuning, heating, and leakage need the same experimental separation introduced in the control chapters.

11.2.2 Exchange gates

Allowing neighbouring electron wavefunctions to overlap produces exchange. For dimensionless spin operators \(\mathbf S=(X,Y,Z)/2\), write \(H=J\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2\), with \(J\) an energy. The singlet and triplet energies differ by \(J\). A pulse with \(Jt/\hbar=\pi\) implements SWAP up to a global phase; half that area gives an entangling square root of SWAP.

Problem 11.2. If \(J/h=10\) MHz, how long is a SWAP exchange pulse?

Worked solution

\(Jt/\hbar=2\pi(10^7)t=\pi\), so \(t=50\) ns; the square-root pulse takes 25 ns in the ideal constant-exchange model.

11.3 Readout and Integration

11.3.1 Spin-to-charge conversion

Spin-to-charge conversion maps one spin state to a different tunnelling or charge configuration. A nearby charge sensor then detects it. Pauli blockade provides another conversion mechanism in suitable double dots. The conversion can fail even if the charge detector is excellent, so quote conversion and detector errors separately. Reservoir temperature and timing also influence initialization and readout.

11.3.2 Disorder, wiring, and uniformity

Charge noise changes exchange, nuclear spins can fluctuate the local magnetic field, and device disorder changes parameters from dot to dot. Material purification can address some noise sources but does not solve wiring or uniformity. Dense fabrication is useful only if gates, reservoirs, sensors, and calibration can support simultaneous operation throughout the array. Always compare usable connectivity, not just geometric density.

Problem 11.3. Can perfect charge detection guarantee perfect spin readout?

Worked solution

No. The spin-to-charge conversion may have failed before detection. The measured assignment error combines both stages and preparation errors unless independently calibrated.

A crystal defect can also localize an electronic spin, with an optical transition offering a route between stationary and flying information.
