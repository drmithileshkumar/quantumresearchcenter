5.1 Initialization

Two preparations produce overlapping detector signals. Choosing a threshold gives a bit, but it also gives mistakes. Readout is therefore a calibrated statistical decision linked to a physical disturbance.

Predict before calculating. If an instrument reports the initial bit correctly, does that establish that the same qubit is ready for another gate?

Compare your reasoning

No. Assignment describes the reported value. The measurement can also disturb, relax, or leak the state.

5.1.1 Thermal preparation

A system in thermal equilibrium assigns level j a weight proportional to \(e^{-E_j/(k_BT)}\), where \(k_B\) converts temperature to energy. This Boltzmann law is a statistical-physics input. For energies \(0,\hbar\omega\), normalizing the two weights gives

\[p_1=\frac{e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}}{1+e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}}=\frac1{1+e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}}.\]

At energy-to-temperature ratio five, \(p_1\simeq0.00669\). The state is mostly ground, not exactly ground. For a 5 GHz transition, \(hf/k_B\) is about 0.240 K. Whether a cold stage reaches the required effective qubit temperature must be tested by population measurements; imperfect thermal contact or unwanted radiation can invalidate the equilibrium assumption.

5.1.2 Active reset

Waiting for relaxation prepares the ground state only as quickly as the relaxation dynamics allow. Active reset instead measures and conditionally drives, or transfers excitation into a dissipative auxiliary. For an ideal measurement followed by an ideal X on outcome one, either input basis state ends in zero. Entropy is carried away through the recorded result and environment.

Errors change this calculation. If the initial excited population is \(p\), a symmetric assignment error \(e\), followed by a perfect conditional X, leaves excited population e: either a ground input is wrongly flipped or an excited input is wrongly left alone. Feedback latency also allows relaxation between measurement and correction. Thus a good assignment number alone is insufficient; test the complete reset and the delay until the next gate. Report leakage separately from residual excited population.

Problem 5.1. At ΔE/kBT=5, estimate thermal excited population.

Worked solution

It is 1/(1+e⁵)≈0.00669. This is an equilibrium two-level prediction, not a universal device initialization error.

5.2 Measurement Signals

5.2.1 State-dependent response

A readout device converts the qubit into a signal. For an integrated signal \(s\), let \(f_0(s)\) and \(f_1(s)\) be its probability densities for the two preparations. A threshold partitions possible signal values into guesses. Overlap between the distributions creates assignment errors even if the electronics records s perfectly.

For equally likely states and Gaussian signals of means \(\mu_0,\mu_1\), equal width \(\sigma\), the symmetric threshold lies at their midpoint. Moving a threshold improves one assignment error at the expense of the other. With unequal priors or unequal noise, the optimum threshold changes. A histogram should therefore show the calibrated preparations, units or normalization of s, and the decision rule, rather than just two colored peaks.

5.2.2 Assignment and backaction

Let \(e_0\) be the probability of reporting one after preparing zero, and \(e_1\) the probability of reporting zero after preparing one. If the true excited population is p, enumerate the two ways to report one:

\[p_{\rm report}(1)=e_0(1-p)+(1-e_1)p=e_0+(1-e_0-e_1)p.\]

For \(p=0.8\) and \(e_0=e_1=0.02\), the observed fraction is 0.788. Inverting this relation is meaningful only if the calibration applies to the same experiment and \(1-e_0-e_1\) is not too small.

Assignment and backaction are separate. An apparatus may correctly report the initial state but leave it disturbed or leaked afterward. To test reuse, examine a subsequent measurement or operation, including the time between the two.

Problem 5.2. If e0=e1=1/2, can the assignment relation be inverted?

Worked solution

No. The output is always a fair bit independent of p, so the detector contains no information about the population.

5.3 Readout Design

5.3.1 Integration and discrimination

Suppose a time record consists of independent samples with state-dependent mean separation \(\delta\) and noise variance \(\sigma_0^2\) per sample. Averaging M samples keeps the mean separation unchanged but reduces the variance to \(\sigma_0^2/M\). The separation in units of noise therefore grows as \(\sqrt M\).

This explains why integration can improve discrimination. It does not justify arbitrarily long readout: the state may relax while the signal is acquired, samples may be correlated, and amplifier noise may drift. If a state changes halfway through acquisition, the average mixes two responses and need not belong to either calibrated Gaussian. Choose the integration window using a model that includes those dynamics, then validate assignment and disturbance independently.

5.3.2 Repeated measurement and reuse

An ideal repeatable Z measurement has operators \(P_0=\ket0\bra0\) and \(P_1=\ket1\bra1\). A first outcome j leaves \(P_j\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_j}\), so an immediate identical measurement returns j with certainty. This prediction depends on there being no intervening dynamics or measurement-induced transition.

In hardware, disagreement between repeated reports can arise from assignment error, physical transitions, or leakage. Measure the joint probabilities of the two reports as a function of their separation; a single repeatability percentage cannot separate those mechanisms. When a measurement is used within error correction, include neighboring-qubit disturbance as well. A measurement that looks acceptable at the end of a computation may be unsuitable halfway through it.

Problem 5.3. Does a destructive detector with perfect labels qualify as reusable nondemolition readout?

Worked solution

No. It may identify the original state exactly while consuming the carrier. Reuse and preservation are separate requirements.

Noise changes the state even when we intend to do nothing. The next chapter separates energy loss from phase uncertainty.

Worked model check. A detector has e0=0.03 and e1=0.07. It reports one on 48% of trials. Infer the excited population under a valid fixed assignment model.
