17.1 Connectivity

The same logical circuit can require very different physical operations on different connection graphs. Architecture makes that difference explicit.

Predict before calculating. Does a graph with many possible interactions guarantee all those gates can run simultaneously?

Compare your reasoning

No. Shared channels, crosstalk, and mediator constraints can forbid logically independent gates from overlapping.

17.1.1 Local and nonlocal interactions

Represent qubits by vertices and available pair interactions by edges. This graph describes permitted local operations, not every detail of their speed or simultaneous availability. To apply a two-qubit gate between logical states separated by d edges along a path, one simple strategy moves one state through \(d-1\) SWAPs, performs the gate, then moves it back.

A SWAP can be decomposed into three directed CNOTs when those primitives and directions are available. This restore-the-mapping strategy adds \(6(d-1)\) CNOTs. For d=4, that is 18 extra gates. A compiler can sometimes avoid the return trip by tracking a new placement, so this is an explicit strategy’s cost rather than a lower bound. Native gates, direction reversal, transport, and spectator constraints change it.

17.1.2 Routing and scheduling

A schedule must respect both logical dependencies and shared hardware. Two gates on disjoint edges can be logically parallel, yet a common control channel or crosstalk rule may forbid their overlap. If M requested gates all require one bus, their total duration is at least the sum of their bus occupancies.

For independent operations with durations \(t_j\), a dependency chain gives another lower bound: its summed duration. The schedule must satisfy the larger relevant bounds, together with initialization, measurement, and reset waits. Longer idle periods add the noise exposure of Chapter 6. A connectivity advantage should therefore be assessed through a compiled timed schedule, not just average graph distance or a claim of all-to-all access.

Problem 17.1. On a line A–B–C, how can an A–C interaction be made local?

Worked solution

Swap A and B, then interact the state now at B with C. A restoring SWAP is needed only if the later schedule requires the original placement. In a CNOT decomposition one SWAP costs three CNOTs.

17.2 Modular Systems

17.2.1 Photonic links

A module stores and processes qubits locally, while photonic interfaces attempt remote entanglement. Suppose each required photon is successfully delivered and detected with probability η. A specified two-photon protocol with accepted-pattern factor q then has per-attempt success \(s=q\eta^2\), before additional faults.

For independent attempts, success first occurs at n with probability \(s(1-s)^{n-1}\). Summing n times this distribution gives mean attempts \(1/s\). An attempt period τ gives mean wait \(\tau/s\). Propagation, heralding acknowledgment, reset, and local resource preparation contribute to τ or constrain pipelining. Improving local gate speed does not automatically improve a link limited by this waiting process.

17.2.2 Communication and memory

Memory must survive the random wait for a connection. If its coherence decays as \(e^{-t/T_2}\), averaging over independent attempts gives

\[\langle C\rangle=\sum_{n=1}^\infty s(1-s)^{n-1}e^{-n\tau/T_2}=\frac{s e^{-\tau/T_2}}{1-(1-s)e^{-\tau/T_2}}.\]

For \(s=0.01\) and \(\tau/T_2=0.001\), this is about 0.909. The exponential evaluated only at the mean wait gives a different value because coherence is nonlinear in time. This toy model excludes reset-induced disturbance and dependence between attempts. Multiple links that must all be ready add a waiting-and-storage problem beyond one geometric distribution.

Problem 17.2. A link succeeds with probability 0.02 per 10 μs attempt. Find its mean waiting time.

Worked solution

It is 10 μs/0.02 = 500 μs under independent trials, with no extra reset or communication delay. A memory requirement cannot be inferred from this mean alone; long waits also occur.

17.3 Scaling Design

17.3.1 Fabrication and yield

If each of N required sites is independently usable with yield y, full yield is \(y^N\). For \(y=0.999\) and N=1000, it is only about 0.368. This calculation does not say that most processors are useless: spare sites, rerouting, and defect-aware codes can tolerate some defects.

Those remedies require connectivity and control resources. A missing site in a line can break a route; the same defect in a richly connected layout may be bypassed. Correlated fabrication defects also invalidate the independent-yield assumption. Model the spatial defect distribution and how the chosen architecture compiles around it. Report usable calibrated sites and interactions, rather than lithographic site count alone.

17.3.2 Architecture-aware resource counts

Attach a physical schedule to the Bell-pair circuit introduced in Chapter 1. Include two resets, one single-qubit pulse, routing if needed, the entangling gate, basis rotations for verification, acquisition, and any return to a usable state. Distinguish the per-shot duration from the total time needed for adequate statistics.

If the shot cycle is 100 μs and 10,000 shots are needed, acquisition alone takes one second. A conditional protocol accepting one shot in ten needs about ten seconds for the same accepted count, before calibration and retries with different durations. Logical resource studies add encoding, repeated checks, decoding, and auxiliary-state preparation. The correct accounting starts from an output task and traces each required physical operation into time and resource use.

Problem 17.3. For 1,000 independent components of yield 0.999, estimate perfect-array yield.

Worked solution

It is 0.999¹⁰⁰⁰ ≈ 0.368. Redundancy and repairable connectivity can be essential even when individual yield is high.
