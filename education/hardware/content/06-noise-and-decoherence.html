6.1 Relaxation

An excited-state population decays, while a superposition can lose its interference even without losing energy. These two experiments motivate distinct timescales rather than one universal “qubit lifetime.”

Predict before calculating. If the excited population decays to one half, must the interference contrast also be one half?

Compare your reasoning

No. Relaxation acts differently on populations and off-diagonal coherence. Additional dephasing can change the contrast further.

6.1.1 Energy loss and T1

We will use a density matrix to include statistical mixtures as well as pure states. For a known pure state, \(\rho=\ket\psi\bra\psi\); for randomly prepared states, average these outer products with their preparation probabilities. Diagonal entries are basis populations, and off-diagonal entries describe the coherence seen in rotated-basis measurements.

Assume an excited system has constant decay rate \(\Gamma_1\): in a short time \(dt\), a fraction \(\Gamma_1dt\) of the surviving excited population decays. Then \(p_1(t+dt)=p_1(t)(1-\Gamma_1dt)\), or \(\dot p_1=-\Gamma_1p_1\). Solving gives \(p_1(t)=p_1(0)e^{-t/T_1}\), where \(T_1=1/\Gamma_1\).

For a superposition, zero-temperature amplitude damping is described by \(K_0=\ket0\bra0+\sqrt\eta\ket1\bra1\), \(K_1=\sqrt{1-\eta}\ket0\bra1\), with \(\eta=e^{-t/T_1}\). Summing \(K_j\rho K_j^\dagger\) multiplies the excited population by \(\eta\) and the off-diagonal amplitude by \(\sqrt\eta\). This square root explains why population decay alone contributes a coherence decay time \(2T_1\).

6.1.2 Thermal excitation

At nonzero temperature, allow upward rate \(\Gamma_\uparrow\) and downward rate \(\Gamma_\downarrow\). The population equation is \(\dot p_1=\Gamma_\uparrow(1-p_1)-\Gamma_\downarrow p_1\). Setting the derivative to zero gives \(p_{\rm eq}=\Gamma_\uparrow/(\Gamma_\uparrow+\Gamma_\downarrow)\). Subtract this constant and solve the remaining exponential:

\[p_1(t)=p_{\rm eq}+[p_1(0)-p_{\rm eq}]e^{-(\Gamma_\uparrow+\Gamma_\downarrow)t}.\]

Preparing both ground and excited states lets us estimate the common equilibrium and rate. A fit that forces equilibrium to zero can bias the inferred lifetime. Equilibrium detailed balance gives \(\Gamma_\uparrow/\Gamma_\downarrow=e^{-\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}\), but nonequilibrium excitation need not obey that relation.

Problem 6.1. Can T2 exceed 2T1 in the stated exponential model?

Worked solution

No. The pure-dephasing rate is nonnegative. An apparent violation suggests inconsistent fits, definitions, or a model outside these assumptions.

6.2 Dephasing

6.2.1 Frequency noise and T2

A fluctuating splitting produces phase \(\phi(t)=\int_0^t\delta\omega(s)ds\). Each individual run may remain pure, but averaging runs multiplies the coherence by \(\langle e^{i\phi}\rangle\). For a zero-mean Gaussian phase of variance v, this average is \(e^{-v/2}\), so phase spread reduces the measured interference contrast.

Independent exponential pure dephasing contributes \(e^{-t/T_\phi}\). Combining it with the amplitude-damping factor gives

\[\frac1{T_2}=\frac1{2T_1}+\frac1{T_\phi}.\]

For \(T_1=100\) μs and \(T_\phi=100\) μs, \(T_2=66.7\) μs. This relation assumes the stated independent Markovian channels. Slowly varying frequency offsets can instead produce a Gaussian envelope, so a fitted Ramsey time \(T_2^*\) need not be a single microscopic rate.

6.2.2 Echo and noise timescales

For a frequency offset that is nearly constant during one trial, free evolution accumulates phase \(\delta\omega t\). Insert an X π pulse halfway through. Since \(XZX=-Z\), the sign of the frequency term reverses in the toggling description, giving phase \(\delta\omega(t/2)-\delta\omega(t/2)=0\).

The cancellation fails for rapidly changing noise because the two halves no longer see the same offset. More generally the phase is \(\int y(t)\delta\omega(t)dt\), where the modulation y switches between +1 and −1 at each π pulse. This formula links pulse timing to noise timescales. Echo does not reverse energy already lost to the environment, and imperfect π pulses add their own errors. Compare echo and Ramsey under the same preparation and readout calibration.

Problem 6.2. Does echo reverse a spontaneous emission event?

Worked solution

No. Emission transfers energy and information to the environment. A mid-sequence π pulse does not recover the emitted quantum state.

6.3 Error Models

6.3.1 Leakage and correlated errors

A Pauli error remains inside the qubit’s two-dimensional space; leakage does not. A leaked level may respond differently to later gates and may persist until a dedicated removal step. Modeling every failure as a random X, Y, or Z can therefore miss long-lived memory in the error process.

Correlations matter even for errors confined to the qubit. Independent flips on two qubits have simultaneous probability \(p^2\). A shared event that flips both with probability p has the same single-qubit flip probabilities but a much larger joint probability. Measuring marginals cannot distinguish them. Use simultaneous sequences and joint outcomes, and state whether the model describes a single gate, an idle interval, or an entire repeating cycle.

6.3.2 From a model to a prediction

A noise model earns its use by predicting a measurement. Start with \(\ket+\), wait t, and measure X. Under the exponential relaxation and pure-dephasing model, \(\langle X\rangle=e^{-t/T_2}\), so \(P(+)=\tfrac12[1+e^{-t/T_2}]\). At \(t=T_2\), this gives about 0.684, not the excited-state survival \(e^{-1}\).

With detuning, the X expectation also contains \(\cos(\Delta t)\); the apparent oscillation and its contrast have separate causes. Readout assignment then transforms the final probabilities according to Chapter 5. Keeping those steps separate lets an experiment distinguish frequency error, coherence loss, and detector contrast. Fitting every reduced oscillation to “decoherence” would confound all three.

Problem 6.3. After ten identical overrotations by ε, what coherent angle error remains?

Worked solution

10ε if the rotations share the same axis and no other action changes the accumulation. A probability estimate requires applying that rotation to the specified input.
