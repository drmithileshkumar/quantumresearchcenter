Every platform so far stores its qubits in matter and uses light as a control or a messenger. Light barely interacts with anything, which is why a photon can travel far without losing its coherence. The same weakness makes it hard for one photon to control another. Can photons carry the computation itself?

We first describe light as a set of modes, each an oscillator, and encode a qubit in which of two modes holds a single photon. Beam splitters and phase shifts then give single-qubit gates through interference of paths. Asking what two photons do at a beam splitter reveals the one effect passive optics offers between photons, two-photon interference. Detecting extra photons and keeping only accepted outcomes turns that effect into heralded entangling operations. Finally we ask what photon loss, detector efficiency, and probabilistic sources cost, and how many sources and switches a reliable supply of photons needs.

Our first question is how a single photon can hold a qubit.

13.1 Optical Qubits

A photon can take two paths coherently, and a beam splitter mixes those paths. How do we describe photons precisely enough to say what state a source makes and what a beam splitter does? Treating each pattern of the light field as an oscillator gives modes and creation operators. Encoding a qubit in two modes then reduces single-qubit gates to interference between paths.

13.1.1 Encoding and sources

What exactly holds a photon? Light in a given path, polarization, frequency band, and time window behaves as one harmonic oscillator. Each such pattern of the field is called a mode, and its excitation number is the number of photons in it. The empty field is the vacuum \(\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\). The raising operator of a mode, here called its creation operator \(a^\dagger\), adds one photon: \(a^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\) is one photon in that mode.

How can one photon encode a qubit? Put it in one of two modes \(0\) and \(1\), for example two paths: \(\ket{0_L}=\ket{1,0}\) and \(\ket{1_L}=\ket{0,1}\), where the entries count photons in each mode. A general qubit is \((a\,a_0^\dagger+b\,a_1^\dagger)\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\), with \(|a|^2+|b|^2=1\). This is dual-rail encoding. Both logical states contain one photon, so neither is the vacuum.

What must a source do? It must prepare exactly one photon in the intended mode. Vacuum and extra photons lie outside the two-dimensional encoding. Suppose a source produces vacuum with probability \(v\), and the readout simply discards trials with no click. The retained trials can look excellent, while the unconditional success is at most \(1-v\). So the probability of preparing a photon is a separate figure from the quality of the qubits that remain.

Predict before calculating. If a dual-rail photon is lost, has the qubit become logical zero?

Compare your reasoning

No. Both logical values contain one photon. Vacuum is outside the intended code space.

13.1.2 Interference and phase control

How do we rotate a dual-rail qubit? With a beam splitter, which mixes two modes. Choose the real balanced convention with mode matrix \(B=2^{-1/2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\). Its columns are orthonormal, so it preserves the total one-photon probability. A photon entering the first rail leaves with equal amplitudes in the two outputs.

What turns those amplitudes into a chosen outcome? A phase plate in the second path multiplies its amplitude by \(e^{i\phi}\). A second identical beam splitter then gives output amplitudes \((1+e^{i\phi})/2\) and \((1-e^{i\phi})/2\), with probabilities \(\cos^2(\phi/2)\) and \(\sin^2(\phi/2)\). This is the interference of amplitudes from quantum computing, carried out by physical paths. Loss, drift in path length, and imperfect overlap of the modes reduce the contrast, and they must be told apart from an intended phase shift.

Problem 13.1. What happens to a dual-rail qubit if its photon is lost?

Worked solution

The state leaves the one-photon subspace and becomes vacuum in the two rails. This is not the logical zero, which still contains one photon.

13.2 Photonic Gates

Single-photon gates need only linear optics. What can two photons do to each other when they never interact? At a beam splitter, two identical photons interfere as a pair, which is the only joint effect passive optics gives. Detecting extra photons and keeping only accepted outcomes then converts that effect into an entangling operation that succeeds with some probability.

13.2.1 Two-photon interference

What happens when one photon enters each input of a balanced beam splitter? Track creation operators. A photon entering a mode leaves in the output modes \(c\) and \(d\) with the amplitudes of the corresponding column of \(B\), so \(a^\dagger\mapsto(c^\dagger+d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\) and \(b^\dagger\mapsto(c^\dagger-d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\). Creation operators of different modes commute, \(c^\dagger d^\dagger=d^\dagger c^\dagger\), because photons are bosons. Multiplying out,

\[a^\dagger b^\dagger\ket{\mathrm{vac}}\mapsto\tfrac12[(c^\dagger)^2-(d^\dagger)^2]\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=\frac{\ket{2,0}-\ket{0,2}}{\sqrt2}.\]

The two terms with one photon in each output cancel. The factor \(\sqrt2\) comes from \((c^\dagger)^2\ket{\mathrm{vac}}=\sqrt2\ket{2,0}\), by the raising rule. So identical photons always leave together. This is Hong–Ou–Mandel interference.

What if the photons differ? If some other property, such as arrival time, labels which photon is which, the cancelling alternatives become distinguishable and need not cancel. We quote the result for pure wavepackets with overlap \(\mu\): the probability of one photon in each output is \((1-|\mu|^2)/2\). Indistinguishability is therefore a measurable control requirement.

13.2.2 Measurement-induced operations

Can beam splitters alone entangle two dual-rail qubits? We quote a known result: passive linear optics on its own gives no deterministic entangling gate between such qubits. Detection can supply what is missing. Add extra photons in ancillary modes, apply a linear-optical unitary \(U\), detect the ancillary modes, and keep only an accepted outcome \(m\). The data then undergo the conditional operator \(K_m=\bra mU\ket{\mathrm{ancilla}}\).

When does this act as a gate? The accepted output is \(K_m\ket\psi/\sqrt{p_m}\), with \(p_m=\|K_m\ket\psi\|^2\). For the same heralded unitary \(U_0\) to act on every logical input with the same success probability \(s\), we need \(K_m=\sqrt s\,U_0\) on the code space. Otherwise keeping only accepted outcomes distorts the amplitudes or favours some inputs. Knill, Laflamme, and Milburn proved that such heralded gates, with additional resources, give a scalable route to computation; that theorem goes beyond this small calculation.

Problem 13.2. What is the ideal coincidence probability for one identical photon in each balanced input?

Worked solution

Zero: expanding the creation operators cancels the term with one photon in each output. For fully distinguishable photons the probability is one half.

13.3 Detection and Architecture

Heralded gates and readout both depend on detectors, and every photon can be lost on the way. How does loss enter the statistics, and how can probabilistic sources supply photons on demand? Treating an imperfect detector as loss followed by ideal detection gives the click probabilities. Running many sources in parallel and switching out a successful photon then trades source count against switching loss.

13.3.1 Loss and heralding

How does an imperfect detector change the statistics? Model it as a loss with transmission \(\eta\), followed by ideal detection. For \(n\) independent photons, no detection occurs with probability \((1-\eta)^n\), so at least one photon is detected with probability \(1-(1-\eta)^n\). A threshold detector reports only whether it clicked, while a number-resolving detector counts photons.

What does loss do to a dual-rail qubit? It moves the state to vacuum. The loss can be flagged only when a later measurement checks that a photon is still present, and a final detection destroys the photon in any case. Dark counts, clicks without a photon, can also turn an absent photon into an apparent success. Source probability, transmission, detection efficiency, false clicks, and the accepted outcomes each affect the rate and the conditional fidelity differently, so each is counted separately.

13.3.2 Resource states and multiplexing

Can unreliable sources supply photons on demand? If one source succeeds with probability \(s\), then \(M\) independent sources all fail with probability \((1-s)^M\). At least one photon is available with probability \(1-(1-s)^M\). At \(s=0.1\), ten sources give 0.6513, before any routing loss. Running sources in parallel this way is called multiplexing.

What does routing cost? Suppose that selecting the successful photon takes \(\lceil\log_2M\rceil\) switching stages, each with transmission \(\eta_s\). In a simplified balanced tree, the delivered availability is \([1-(1-s)^M]\eta_s^{\lceil\log_2M\rceil}\). More sources raise production, while deeper switching loses photons.

The same trade-off appears at larger scale. One photonic architecture prepares large entangled states of many photons in advance, called resource states, and computes by measuring their photons one at a time. The resource states are assembled by small heralded entangling measurements called fusions, each of which can fail. Every failed fusion costs photons, time, and delay-line storage, so the architecture is set by sources, interferometers, switches, delays, and detectors together.

Problem 13.3. If independent sources succeed with probability 0.1, what is the chance that at least one of ten succeeds?

Worked solution

It is 1 − 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.6513, before switching loss. Multiplexing improves availability but does not eliminate the cost of routing the successful photon.
