3.1 Driven Transitions

Prepare zero and apply a resonant field for a variable time. The measured excited population oscillates. This experiment turns an abstract X rotation into a calibrated duration and field amplitude.

Predict before calculating. A detuned pulse never reaches full inversion. Could waiting for another oscillation peak necessarily fix it?

Compare your reasoning

No. Detuning tilts the rotation axis and imposes a population ceiling. The calculation below separates that ceiling from the oscillation period.

3.1.1 Resonance and Rabi oscillations

For an electric-dipole drive, the field adds energy \(-D E(t)\), where D is the dipole operator and E the field at the device. Its off-diagonal entry \(-\bra1D\ket0 E(t)\) couples the two amplitudes. A sinusoidal field has positive- and negative-frequency components. In a frame rotating near the transition, one varies slowly at the difference frequency while the other rotates near twice the carrier frequency. Keeping the slow component produces the effective Hamiltonian below; neglecting the fast one is an approximation whose validity must be checked.

A field oscillating near the energy splitting can exchange population between the two levels. After transforming to a drive frame and dropping terms oscillating much faster than the response, choose pulse phase zero so that

\[H_r/\hbar=\tfrac12(-\Delta Z+\Omega X),\qquad\Delta=\omega_{01}-\omega_d.\]

This rotating-wave approximation requires the drive and detuning scales to be small compared with the carrier frequency, and assumes other transitions remain negligible. On resonance, \(X^2=I\), so separating even and odd powers in the exponential gives \(U=I\cos(\Omega t/2)-iX\sin(\Omega t/2)\). Starting in zero therefore gives

\[\ket\psi(t)=\cos(\Omega t/2)\ket0-i\sin(\Omega t/2)\ket1.\]

The observed excited population is \(P_1=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)\). The oscillation comes from coherent evolution of amplitudes. The Hamiltonian is the physical assumption; the population curve follows by calculation.

3.1.2 Pulse area and phase

For a resonant pulse with fixed axis but changing strength, the Hamiltonians at different times commute. Divide the pulse into short rotations; their angles add. The final angle is the pulse area, \(\theta=\int\Omega(t)dt\), and \(P_1=\sin^2(\theta/2)\). A π pulse transfers zero to one; a π/2 pulse creates equal populations.

In the convention used here, phase \(\phi\) sets the drive term to \(\hbar\Omega(\cos\phi X+\sin\phi Y)/2\). Starting at zero, a pulse gives \(\cos(\theta/2)\ket0-i e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)\ket1\). Phase changes the relative amplitude even when the immediate populations are unchanged. If \(\Omega/(2\pi)=1\) MHz, a constant π pulse lasts 0.5 μs. Changing the phase halfway through generally changes the axis; then adding scalar areas alone is not enough.

Problem 3.1. What is the π/2-pulse duration at a 5 MHz Rabi rate?

Worked solution

50 ns. The angular rate is 2π·5 MHz, so Ωt=π/2.

3.2 Control Errors

3.2.1 Detuning and amplitude error

With detuning, the rotation axis tilts out of the equatorial plane. Let \(\Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\). Squaring the Hamiltonian again reduces its exponential to sine and cosine, giving

\[P_1(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega_R^2}\sin^2(\Omega_Rt/2).\]

Detuning both increases the oscillation frequency and reduces its maximum height. At \(\Delta=\Omega\), the population never exceeds one half. Retiming a pulse cannot remove that ceiling.

A fractional amplitude error \(\epsilon\) instead changes a resonant intended π pulse to angle \(\pi(1+\epsilon)\). Its transfer probability is \(\cos^2(\pi\epsilon/2)\), so its population failure is approximately \(\pi^2\epsilon^2/4\). This quadratic population response can hide a first-order coherent angle error, which becomes more evident in repeated phase-sensitive sequences.

3.2.2 Pulse shaping and leakage

A pulse is finite in time, so it contains more than one frequency. In weak excitation of an unwanted transition, first-order evolution gives an amplitude proportional to \(\int_0^T\Omega(t)e^{i\Delta t}dt\). For a rectangular pulse, integrating the exponential gives \(\Omega T e^{i\Delta T/2}\operatorname{sinc}(\Delta T/2)\), where \(\operatorname{sinc}x=\sin x/x\).

Shortening the pulse broadens its frequency response. Smoothly turning a pulse on and off can reduce spectral sidelobes, but does not remove all leakage or errors. The integral is a weak-excitation estimate, not the exact resonant π-pulse dynamics. Near a neighboring transition, multilevel simulation is needed. The useful comparison is between the pulse spectrum and all transitions coupled by its physical control operator.

Problem 3.2. If |Δ|=Ω, can a square pulse invert perfectly?

Worked solution

No. The maximum excited probability in this model is Ω²/(Ω²+Δ²)=1/2.

3.3 Gate Calibration

3.3.1 Rotation experiments

Calibrate a π pulse by measuring a range of pulse durations, not by assuming the first large signal is perfect inversion. Fit the oscillation frequency and offsets, then test the predicted π and π/2 durations in new sequences. Repeating an imperfect pulse magnifies its coherent angle error.

For example, two intended π/2 rotations about X should act as one π rotation. Replacing the second by a pulse with phase shifted by π reverses its axis and should undo the first. Both tests can have the same individual pulse populations but different final populations. They therefore test coherence and phase calibration as well as amplitude. Repeat at several delays to detect a frequency mismatch, and check whether the result changes when a neighboring qubit is driven.

3.3.2 Frames and virtual phases

Define \(R_z(\lambda)=e^{-i\lambda Z/2}\). Direct multiplication gives \(R_z(\lambda)XR_z(-\lambda)=\cos\lambda X+\sin\lambda Y\), and hence \(R_\phi(\theta)=R_z(\phi)R_x(\theta)R_z(-\phi)\). This identity explains why a calibrated change of later pulse phases can represent a Z-frame update without a separate physical Z pulse.

The state has not rotated just because a software variable changed. The later controls and the interpretation of phase-sensitive measurements must consistently use the new reference. In a multi-qubit operation, the interaction phase may also depend on that reference. Validate a frame update by an interference sequence, such as two π/2 pulses with a varied phase between them, rather than by a Z-basis population measurement that cannot see it.

Problem 3.3. Why is a successful zero-to-one pulse insufficient as a gate test?

Worked solution

Its phase action on superpositions may be wrong even when the output population is correct. An interference experiment is needed.

A single drive supplies rotations. Two-qubit gates require an interaction that cannot be written as separate local evolutions.

Worked model check. A drive has angular detuning twice its resonant Rabi strength. Can a longer pulse give 90% transfer in the isolated two-level model?

Worked solution

No. Its maximal transfer is \(\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+4\Omega^2)=1/5\). Duration changes the sine factor but cannot remove the detuning ceiling.
