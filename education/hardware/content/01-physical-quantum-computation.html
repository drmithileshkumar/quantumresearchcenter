1.1 Physical Requirements

A circuit says to rotate a qubit, couple it to another, and measure. A laboratory must supply a physical state, a timed interaction, and a detector. This course follows that chain and asks what must be true for the measured bit to mean what the algorithm predicts.

1.1.1 Encoding and initialization

Choose two physical states to represent zero and one, and a procedure that prepares a known starting state. The carrier usually has additional states, motion, and environmental couplings. A usable encoding must remain identifiable throughout the operation. Merely observing two distinguishable detector signals is not yet evidence of coherent control between the corresponding states.

1.1.2 Control and measurement

A gate is implemented by controlled evolution; readout converts a state-dependent response into a classical record. DiVincenzo’s framework organizes the requirements around identifiable scalable qubits, initialization, adequate coherence, universal control, and measurement, with additional requirements for communication. These are linked engineering tasks. Improving isolation may make control or measurement more difficult, so a device must be evaluated as a whole.

Problem 1.1. Does a long coherence time alone make a good processor?

Worked solution

No. Initialization, control, coupling, readout, and scalable operation must also work. A perfectly isolated but inaccessible state cannot execute a useful program.

1.2 Computational Resources

1.2.1 Physical and logical qubits

A physical qubit is a degree of freedom used to store a two-state system. A logical qubit is encoded across physical resources with a specified protection and control scheme. Counting physical devices does not determine how many reliable logical operations they support. Some encodings use several two-level systems; others use many states of an oscillator. Both require an explicit error model and recovery procedure.

1.2.2 Time, connectivity, and error

Consider an illustrative gate duration of 100 ns and an exponential coherence time of 100 μs. Their ratio is 0.001, but this is not a measured gate error. The relevant error also depends on which state is stored, the noise process, pulse imperfections, and leakage. For excited-state decay alone, the probability of a decay during that interval is \(1-e^{-t/T_1}\approx t/T_1=0.001\). A phase-sensitive superposition asks a different question.

Problem 1.2. What assumption permits the estimate t/T1?

Worked solution

An exponential relaxation model and a duration much shorter than T1. It estimates decay of excited-state population, not every contribution to gate infidelity.

1.3 Implementation Models

1.3.1 Gate and analog evolution

Gate computation uses a sequence of calibrated transformations. Analog simulation directly engineers an interacting generator and observes its evolution; annealing changes a generator along a path. These models share control and readout problems but require different correctness tests. A controllable analog device is not automatically a universal gate processor, and a circuit description alone does not specify its physical implementation.

1.3.2 A device-level specification

For each device, write an implementation contract: the encoded states, preparation error, control Hamiltonian and approximation, accessible interactions, readout rule, timing, and dominant failure modes. Compare the measured behavior with the ideal contract before discussing scaling. Numerical examples in this course are deliberately illustrative rather than current vendor specifications.

Problem 1.3. Why distinguish physical and logical qubit counts?

Worked solution

Encoding, syndrome measurement, routing, and auxiliary-state preparation consume physical resources. The mapping depends on the code and target logical error.

We begin by asking when a physical system with many states can be approximated by a qubit.
