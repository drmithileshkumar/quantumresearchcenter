14.1 Oscillator States

Instead of selecting only the lowest two oscillator levels, can we distribute a qubit across many levels so that common errors leave recognizable traces?

Predict before calculating. Does measuring parity always detect photon loss without revealing a logical bit?

Compare your reasoning

No. That depends on the encoding. Parity distinguishes even and odd cat codewords, whereas both binomial codewords used here have even parity.

14.1.1 Number states and coherent states

An oscillator’s number basis \(\ket n\) counts excitations. Its lowering operator satisfies \(a\ket n=\sqrt n\ket{n-1}\), and its Hamiltonian is \(\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+1/2)\). The square root is needed so \(a^\dagger a\ket n=n\ket n\).

Expand a coherent state as \(\ket\alpha=\sum_nc_n\ket n\). Imposing \(a\ket\alpha=\alpha\ket\alpha\) gives \(c_{n+1}=\alpha c_n/\sqrt{n+1}\). Iterating and normalizing yields \(\ket\alpha=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_n\alpha^n\ket n/\sqrt{n!}\). Its number probabilities are Poisson with mean \(|\alpha|^2\). These states describe one mode, which could be a cavity field or motion; a bosonic encoding is a use of that mode rather than a separate elementary particle platform.

14.1.2 Encoding within a mode

An encoding selects two orthogonal states in the larger oscillator space. Consider the illustrative binomial pair \(\ket{0_L}=(\ket0+\ket4)/\sqrt2\), \(\ket{1_L}=\ket2\). Each has mean occupation two. One loss gives \(a\ket{0_L}=\sqrt2\ket3\) and \(a\ket{1_L}=\sqrt2\ket1\).

Thus one loss on \(u\ket{0_L}+v\ket{1_L}\) yields \(\sqrt2(u\ket3+v\ket1)\). The same factor multiplies both logical amplitudes, and the error states remain orthogonal. This makes a syndrome and recovery possible without learning u or v. Full correction also needs the no-jump evolution, higher-order losses, and imperfect recovery to be analyzed. Merely storing a state across several oscillator levels does not establish protection.

Problem 14.1. What does one loss do to \(\ket4\)?

Worked solution

The unnormalized result is \(2\ket3\). Conditioned on a detected loss, the normalized state is \(\ket3\); the factor influences the event probability.

14.2 Protected Information

14.2.1 Cat states and parity

Expand \(\ket\alpha\pm\ket{-\alpha}\) in number states. The coefficient of n is proportional to \(\alpha^n[1\pm(-1)^n]\), so the plus state has only even n and the minus state only odd n. A loss lowers n by one and flips that parity.

If even and odd cats encode zero and one themselves, measuring parity reveals the logical value and destroys a general superposition. In the binomial example above, both logical states instead start even and both single-loss states are odd, so a parity check reveals the error sector without distinguishing the two logical alternatives. This contrast explains why the encoding must be specified before calling a measurement a syndrome. Preparation and logical control require nonlinear operations beyond freely evolving an ideal oscillator.

14.2.2 Grid-state encodings

Define dimensionless oscillator coordinates q,p with \([q,p]=i\). An ideal GKP zero state has peaks at \(q=2k\sqrt\pi\); logical one shifts those peaks by \(\sqrt\pi\). Measuring q modulo \(\sqrt\pi\) reveals a small displacement while leaving the even-versus-odd lattice label unresolved.

If the shift obeys \(|\delta q|<\sqrt\pi/2\), rounding to the nearest lattice position identifies its correction in the ideal model. Larger shifts can be mistaken for another logical value. Momentum requires a corresponding modular check because protecting only q does not protect an arbitrary qubit. Infinitely sharp, infinitely extended grids are nonnormalizable limits. Finite-width peaks and envelopes have finite energy and nonzero logical error. The full simultaneous correction conditions are supplied by the GKP theory, not proved here.

Problem 14.2. Why can parity measurement damage a superposition of an even and an odd cat?

Worked solution

The two logical alternatives have different parities. Measuring parity distinguishes them and removes their coherence instead of revealing only an error syndrome.

14.3 Control and Recovery

14.3.1 Auxiliary-mediated operations

A useful auxiliary interaction is \(H=\hbar\chi nZ_a/2\). Prepare the auxiliary in plus. On oscillator number n, evolution for t gives an auxiliary relative phase \(e^{i\chi nt}\). At \(\chi t=\pi\), this is \((-1)^n\): even n leaves plus and odd n gives minus, with an additional known number-dependent oscillator phase.

An X-basis auxiliary measurement therefore interrogates parity. Additional known oscillator phases from the sequence must be tracked or compensated. Whether the measurement preserves a logical qubit depends on its encoding, as the cat and binomial examples show. Auxiliary relaxation, reset, and control errors can propagate back to the stored state, so recovery must be evaluated as a physical circuit rather than a perfect projection.

14.3.2 Finite energy and error channels

For photon-loss rate κ per excitation, a number state n has short-time jump probability \(n\kappa dt\). Averaging over the state gives \(p_{\rm loss}\simeq\kappa\langle n\rangle t\) when this quantity is small. Larger mean occupation can improve separation in phase space but increases exposure to loss.

For the binomial code’s mean two, \(\kappa=100\) s⁻¹ and t=10 μs give first-order loss probability 0.002. Two-loss probabilities, finite-speed parity checks, and auxiliary faults determine how often the single-loss correction model fails. Compare corrected logical information through the full cycle with an appropriate unencoded reference; a long-lived cavity or a large cat separation alone is not a demonstration of error-corrected computation.

Problem 14.3. For \(\kappa=100\) s⁻¹, mean occupation 4, and \(t=10\) μs, estimate loss probability.

Worked solution

The short-time estimate is 100 × 4 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.004. Its validity requires this probability to be small.

Bosonic codes engineer redundancy in oscillator space. Topological approaches seek insensitivity to local disturbances through a different structure of physical states.

Worked model check. Apply one loss to u|0L〉+v|1L〉 in the binomial encoding. Why can parity reveal the loss without revealing u or v?

Worked solution

The result is \(\sqrt2(u\ket3+v\ket1)\). Both error states are odd, both code states are even, and the amplitudes have the same multiplicative factor. Full recovery still needs the no-jump and multiple-loss analysis.
