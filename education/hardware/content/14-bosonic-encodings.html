14.1 Oscillator States

Instead of selecting only the lowest two oscillator levels, can we distribute a qubit across many levels so that common errors leave recognizable traces?

14.1.1 Number states and coherent states

An oscillator has number states \(\ket n\), and photon loss acts through \(a\ket n=\sqrt n\ket{n-1}\). A coherent state satisfies \(a\ket\alpha=\alpha\ket\alpha\) and has mean occupation \(|\alpha|^2\). Its number distribution is not sharp. These states describe a mode; the mode may be a microwave cavity, optical field, or motion rather than a separate hardware platform.

14.1.2 Encoding within a mode

An encoding chooses a two-dimensional subspace of this larger state space. Protection requires specifying how likely physical errors move that subspace and how a measurement identifies the movement without identifying the logical amplitudes. Extra levels are useful resources only when the control and recovery operations can exploit them. High oscillator coherence alone does not establish a protected logical qubit.

Problem 14.1. What does one loss do to \(\ket4\)?

Worked solution

The unnormalized result is \(2\ket3\). Conditioned on a detected loss, the normalized state is \(\ket3\); the factor influences the event probability.

14.2 Protected Information

14.2.1 Cat states and parity

Even and odd cat states are normalized sums \(\ket\alpha\pm\ket{-\alpha}\). Expanding in number states leaves only even or odd terms, respectively. A single loss flips this parity. However, if those two cats themselves encode logical zero and one, measuring parity reads logical information. Correctable cat schemes need a specified encoding and stabilization or syndrome strategy; “measure parity” is not a universal repair instruction.

14.2.2 Grid-state encodings

Grid-state encodings distribute amplitude over a lattice in position and momentum. Small displacements can then be inferred modulo the lattice spacing, preserving logical information. Ideal infinitely sharp grids require infinite energy and are mathematical limits. Finite-width peaks and a finite envelope introduce additional error probabilities. The GKP construction supplies the formal correction conditions, which are stated here rather than proved.

Problem 14.2. Why can parity measurement damage a superposition of an even and an odd cat?

Worked solution

The two logical alternatives have different parities. Measuring parity distinguishes them and removes their coherence instead of revealing only an error syndrome.

14.3 Control and Recovery

14.3.1 Auxiliary-mediated operations

A two-level auxiliary system can interrogate an oscillator through occupation-dependent phase shifts. Suitable pulse sequences map a syndrome onto the auxiliary, which is measured and reset. The oscillator need not be directly measured. But auxiliary faults can propagate back to it, so syndrome extraction has its own error budget. Initialization of the encoded state and logical gates also need nonlinear control beyond a bare harmonic oscillator.

14.3.2 Finite energy and error channels

For a mode with photon-loss rate \(\kappa\), the short-time loss probability is approximately \(\kappa\langle n\rangle t\). Increasing state size can improve distinguishability or suppress one error mechanism while increasing exposure to loss. Compare corrected logical performance, recovery time, and hardware resources; neither photon number nor separation in phase space alone is a measure of protection.

Problem 14.3. For \(\kappa=100\) s⁻¹, mean occupation 4, and \(t=10\) μs, estimate loss probability.

Worked solution

The short-time estimate is 100 × 4 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.004. Its validity requires this probability to be small.

Bosonic codes engineer redundancy in oscillator space. Topological approaches seek insensitivity to local disturbances through a different structure of physical states.
