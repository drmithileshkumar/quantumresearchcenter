Noise and Decoherence

Which physical disturbances destroy a computation and how do we distinguish them?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 1–5; physical concepts and approximations are introduced as needed.

Anchor example: A decaying excited state and a Ramsey fringe.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 6.1 Relaxation
  2. 6.2 Dephasing
  3. 6.3 Error Models

6.1 Relaxation

An excited-state population decays, while a superposition can lose its interference even without losing energy. These two experiments motivate distinct timescales rather than one universal “qubit lifetime.”

6.1.1 Energy loss and T1

In a simple zero-temperature relaxation model, excited population obeys \(dp_1/dt=-p_1/T_1\), whose solution is \(p_1(t)=p_1(0)e^{-t/T_1}\). The same amplitude-damping process reduces off-diagonal density-matrix elements by \(e^{-t/(2T_1)}\). Population and coherence therefore decay at different rates even before additional phase noise is included.

6.1.2 Thermal excitation

With upward and downward transition rates Γup and Γdown, the population approaches \(p_{\rm eq}=\Gamma_{\rm up}/(\Gamma_{\rm up}+\Gamma_{\rm down})\). Its return rate is their sum. An observed nonzero late-time population can indicate temperature or other excitation processes; assuming it is zero can bias a fitted relaxation time. Leakage into an extra level needs a larger model.

Problem 6.1. Can T2 exceed 2T1 in the stated exponential model?

Worked solution

No. The pure-dephasing rate is nonnegative. An apparent violation suggests inconsistent fits, definitions, or a model outside these assumptions.

6.2 Dephasing

6.2.1 Frequency noise and T2

A fluctuating transition frequency accumulates an uncertain phase. Averaging \(e^{i\phi}\) over repetitions reduces the observed coherence even if every realization is pure. For independent exponential relaxation and Markovian pure dephasing, \(1/T_2=1/(2T_1)+1/T_\phi\). For T1=100 μs and Tφ=100 μs this gives T2≈66.7 μs. A single exponential need not fit slowly drifting or non-Markovian noise.

6.2.2 Echo and noise timescales

A π pulse midway through free evolution reverses the effect of a static frequency offset: the phase accumulated before the pulse is canceled by that after it. This spin-echo idea suppresses slowly varying dephasing, not energy decay or arbitrary rapidly changing noise. Ramsey T2* often includes shot-to-shot frequency variation; echo-based times probe a different filter of the noise spectrum.

Problem 6.2. Does echo reverse a spontaneous emission event?

Worked solution

No. Emission transfers energy and information to the environment. A mid-sequence π pulse does not recover the emitted quantum state.

6.3 Error Models

6.3.1 Leakage and correlated errors

A Pauli-error description can be convenient for circuit analysis, but coherent overrotation, leakage, loss, and correlated faults can behave differently. For a repeated overrotation by ε about the same axis, m repetitions accumulate an angle mε before randomization or other dynamics. Treating these as independent stochastic errors can give the wrong scaling.

6.3.2 From a model to a prediction

State the noise model, the sequence, and the observable before predicting an error probability. A relaxation measurement during idle does not directly determine error during a strong driven gate. Spectator operations can change noise and crosstalk. Useful models should explain more than the calibration experiment that supplied their parameters.

Problem 6.3. After ten identical overrotations by ε, what coherent angle error remains?

Worked solution

10ε if the rotations share the same axis and no other action changes the accumulation. A probability estimate requires applying that rotation to the specified input.

The next chapter constructs the experiments that infer these parameters and separates inference from proof of gate quality.
