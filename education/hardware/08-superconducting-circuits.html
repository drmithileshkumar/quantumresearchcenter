Superconducting Circuits

How can an electrical circuit support controllable quantum energy levels?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 1–7; physical concepts and approximations are introduced as needed.

Anchor example: A transmon coupled to a readout resonator.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  2. 8.2 Control and Coupling

8.1 Circuit Qubits

A capacitor and an inductor exchange electric and magnetic energy. Why can we not simply call their two lowest energy levels a qubit?

8.1.1 Oscillators and Josephson junctions

An ideal oscillator has equally spaced levels: \(E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)\). A drive resonant with the first transition also addresses higher transitions. A Josephson junction supplies a nonlinear circuit element: its energy depends on the superconducting phase difference as \(-E_J\cos\varphi\). The resulting unequal level spacings let a sufficiently weak, spectrally narrow drive select the lowest pair.

8.1.2 The transmon regime

The simplified transmon Hamiltonian is \(H=4E_C(n-n_g)^2-E_J\cos\varphi\), where \(n\) counts excess Cooper pairs and \(n_g\) is an offset charge. For \(E_J/E_C\gg1\), the approximate lowest transition is \(hf_{01}\simeq\sqrt{8E_JE_C}-E_C\), while \(f_{12}-f_{01}\simeq-E_C/h\). Increasing this ratio suppresses charge sensitivity but leaves a weakly anharmonic oscillator; higher levels remain physically present.

Problem 8.1. Let \(E_C/h=0.25\) GHz and \(E_J/h=20\) GHz. Estimate the lowest transition.

Worked solution

The estimate is \(\sqrt{8(20)(0.25)}-0.25=6.075\) GHz. The next transition is approximately 0.25 GHz lower. These are large-ratio approximations.

8.2 Control and Coupling

8.2.1 Microwave gates

A resonant microwave pulse rotates the lowest two levels, with its phase choosing an axis in the equatorial plane. A calibrated change of the drive reference implements a virtual Z rotation. Initialization combines a cold environment with waiting or active reset. Cooling alone is not a guarantee of the ground state: stray radiation and imperfect thermalization must be checked through population measurements.

8.2.2 Resonators and entangling gates

Capacitive or inductive coupling permits excitation exchange; detuning and couplers help select when it occurs. Coupled resonators can mediate interactions and provide access to measurement. Different gate designs exploit different effective interactions, so an abstract CZ symbol must come with a pulse duration, leakage estimate, and calibration procedure. Driving faster can increase unwanted occupation of the second excited state.

Problem 8.2. Why can a shorter pulse cause leakage?

Worked solution

Its spectrum is broader and its drive amplitude is usually larger. Both make an adjacent transition harder to ignore; the relevant comparison is with the anharmonicity, not the much larger carrier frequency.

8.3 Readout and Scaling

8.3.1 Dispersive measurement

In dispersive readout, the qubit shifts a resonator frequency. A probe acquires a state-dependent phase and amplitude, producing the two signal distributions studied in Chapter 5. In a simple two-level Jaynes–Cummings approximation, the shift scales as \(\chi\simeq g^2/\Delta\), with angular-frequency coupling \(g\), detuning \(\Delta\), and \(|g/\Delta|\ll1\). A transmon requires higher-level corrections; this formula is a mechanism, not a device calibration.

8.3.2 Materials, wiring, and crosstalk

Dielectric loss, fluctuating frequencies, stray excitations, residual coupling, and leakage produce different signatures in the earlier characterization experiments. Scaling also adds microwave lines, cryogenic heat, frequency collisions, and simultaneous-gate crosstalk. A promising isolated qubit therefore needs a second test inside a working array. Compare gate errors with neighbouring gates active and include the cost of retuning frequencies after fabrication.

Problem 8.3. If \(g/2\pi=10\) MHz and \(\Delta/2\pi=200\) MHz, what is the simple dispersive estimate?

Worked solution

\(\chi/2\pi=10^2/200=0.5\) MHz. The ratio is 0.05; multilevel effects and probe photon number still require checking.

An atomic qubit starts with naturally occurring levels instead of a fabricated nonlinear oscillator. The next chapter asks how motion supplies the missing coupling.
