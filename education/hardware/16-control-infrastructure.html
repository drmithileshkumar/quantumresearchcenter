Control Infrastructure

What equipment and signal paths must work together to execute a circuit?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 1–15; physical concepts and approximations are introduced as needed.

Anchor example: A gate command with a finite latency and heat budget.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  2. 16.2 Classical Control

16.1 Operating Conditions

A gate is a timed physical signal, and a measurement is a signal that must be interpreted. The classical equipment is part of the quantum computer.

16.1.1 Cryogenics and vacuum

Cooling reduces thermal occupation and supports superconducting operation where required. Vacuum reduces collisions in trapped-particle devices. Neither condition has a single universal specification: the relevant temperature or collision rate depends on the energy scale and experiment. A cold refrigerator plate does not prove that a qubit or resonator has the same effective temperature. Measure the populations or noise that matter to computation.

16.1.2 Optical and microwave delivery

Cables and optical paths carry control fields but also introduce attenuation, phase drift, distortion, and unwanted radiation. Filtering can suppress noise while limiting pulse bandwidth. Optical alignment and microwave impedance matching solve different delivery problems. Characterize the signal at the device through its response, since the waveform programmed at room temperature need not be the waveform experienced by the qubit.

Problem 16.1. A hypothetical cold stage has 10 μW available; each line adds 20 nW. What is the line-only upper bound?

Worked solution

The quotient is 500 lines. Other heat loads reduce that bound; it is not a complete refrigerator design.

16.2 Classical Control

16.2.1 Waveforms and synchronization

Waveform generators represent signals with finite sampling rates and resolution. Mixers, oscillators, and amplifiers translate these signals to the required frequencies. Shared clocks and phase references let separate pulses define a coherent sequence. Timing errors affect conditional operations, while reference-phase errors change rotation axes. Synchronization must include detectors and feedback channels as well as drive generation.

16.2.2 Feedback and decoding

Readout digitization, discrimination, and decoding take time. A feedback operation cannot depend on a result that has not arrived. Some Pauli corrections can be tracked in a classical frame and applied through later interpretation, but subsequent noncommuting operations may require resolved information. Distinguish processing latency from throughput: a decoder can keep up on average and still have problematic response-time tails.

Problem 16.2. A syndrome round takes 1 μs and decoding latency is 5 μs. Must every round wait 5 μs?

Worked solution

Not necessarily. A pipelined decoder can accept one round per microsecond while returning results five rounds later. Operations that require those results still face the latency, and sustained throughput must keep up.

16.3 System Budgets

16.3.1 Heat, bandwidth, and wiring

Every line introduces a heat and space cost. Amplifiers and classical electronics dissipate power at particular temperature stages, each with a separate cooling budget. Moving computation nearer the device can reduce wiring while increasing local heat. A useful budget lists static heat leaks, active dissipation, signal bandwidth, and channel count; a single room-temperature power total hides the most restrictive stage.

16.3.2 Calibration and stability

Calibration is an ongoing workload. Frequencies, amplitudes, readout thresholds, and interaction parameters can drift during an experiment. Track when each parameter was measured and validate it against representative circuits. Increasing processor size adds simultaneous-control contexts as well as parameters. A system that spends most of its time restoring calibrations has less useful availability than its gate duration suggests.

Problem 16.3. If calibration takes 15 minutes each hour, what fraction remains for data collection?

Worked solution

At most 45/60 = 75%, before other overhead. Useful execution estimates should include this duty cycle rather than reporting only pulse time.

Infrastructure limits which operations can happen together. Architecture translates these constraints into a schedule for an entire algorithm.
