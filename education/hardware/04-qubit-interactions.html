Qubit Interactions

How does a physical coupling produce an entangling gate?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 1–3; physical concepts and approximations are introduced as needed.

Anchor example: Two qubits coupled through a ZZ interaction.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 4.1 Interaction Models
  2. 4.2 Entangling Gates
  3. 4.3 Gate Performance

4.1 Interaction Models

Two qubits can each be well controlled yet remain computationally separate. To entangle them, the energy or transition of one must depend on the state of the other, or both must interact through a shared mediator.

4.1.1 Conditional energies

Consider \(H=\hbar J Z\otimes Z/2\). Equal-bit states have one energy sign and unequal-bit states the opposite. Evolution for time t gives \(U=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-iJt/2},e^{iJt/2},e^{iJt/2},e^{-iJt/2})\). The combination of phases \(\phi_{00}-\phi_{01}-\phi_{10}+\phi_{11}=-2Jt\) cannot in general be removed by separate single-qubit phases. It measures the nonlocal conditional phase.

4.1.2 Exchange interactions

An exchange-type model \(H=\hbar g(XX+YY)/2\) leaves 00 and 11 unchanged and couples 01 to 10. In that two-state subspace it is ℏgX, so \(\ket{01}\) evolves to \(\cos(gt)\ket{01}-i\sin(gt)\ket{10}\). At gt=π/4 it is an entangled state. The coupling’s units and factor-of-two convention must be fixed before translating its strength into a gate time.

Problem 4.1. For the exchange model, when is population fully transferred from 01 to 10?

Worked solution

At gt=π/2, the state is −i|10〉. Half that duration produces equal populations with a relative phase.

4.2 Entangling Gates

4.2.1 Controlled phases

For the ZZ model, Jt=π/2 gives a nonlocal phase of −π. Applying \(R_z(-\pi/2)\) on both qubits afterward makes the operation equal to controlled-Z up to a global phase. This follows by multiplying the four diagonal entries. Local phase corrections are part of the gate specification; saying “wait under ZZ” without them can implement a different circuit.

4.2.2 Mediated interactions

A resonator, a shared vibration, or an optical link can mediate coupling. A successful gate must usually return the mediator to a state independent of the logical input. Otherwise the mediator stores which-branch information and the data qubits alone undergo a noisy operation. A closed trajectory of a mediator can leave a useful conditional phase while removing that record.

Problem 4.2. Can separate Z rotations erase every ZZ conditional phase?

Worked solution

No. They change individual phases but leave the nonlocal combination unchanged modulo 2π.

4.3 Gate Performance

4.3.1 Residual entanglement

Check both populations and coherence after the interaction. If one branch leaves a mediator in |e0〉 and another in |e1〉, the data coherence is multiplied by their overlap 〈e1|e0〉 when the mediator is ignored. An overlap of zero completely distinguishes those branches even if the measured data populations look correct.

4.3.2 Speed and unwanted coupling

Stronger coupling can shorten a gate but increase leakage, sensitivity to control errors, or interactions with spectators. Always-on coupling requires refocusing or frame-aware compensation during nominally independent gates. The useful question is not the largest possible coupling but the fastest operation satisfying the required error and parallelism constraints.

Problem 4.3. Why must an auxiliary oscillator close its trajectory?

Worked solution

If its final state depends on the data, tracing it out reduces coherence. Closing removes the distinguishing record while allowing a geometric phase to remain.

We now have candidate gates. Initialization and measurement determine whether their inputs and outputs can be used reliably.
