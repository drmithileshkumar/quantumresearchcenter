Photonic Computation

How can light carry qubits and mediate computation when photons interact weakly?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 1–12; physical concepts and approximations are introduced as needed.

Anchor example: A dual-rail qubit passing through a balanced beam splitter.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 13.1 Optical Qubits
  2. 13.2 Photonic Gates
  3. 13.3 Detection and Architecture

13.1 Optical Qubits

A photon can take two paths coherently. A beam splitter mixes those paths, but making one photon control another requires an additional mechanism.

13.1.1 Encoding and sources

In dual-rail encoding, \(\ket{0_L}=\ket{1,0}\) and \(\ket{1_L}=\ket{0,1}\): one photon occupies two modes. Polarization and time bins supply other encodings. A source must deliver the required photon number, spectral profile, and timing. Vacuum and extra photons are outside the intended dual-rail subspace, so source quality cannot be reduced to a qubit rotation error.

13.1.2 Interference and phase control

A relative phase shifter rotates the phase between the rails, and a beam splitter mixes their amplitudes. Together they implement arbitrary single-qubit transformations in an ideal lossless two-mode model. The path lengths and optical phases must remain stable during computation. Initialization is therefore a source-and-mode preparation task, while passive optical elements provide much of the coherent one-qubit control.

Problem 13.1. What happens to a dual-rail qubit if its photon is lost?

Worked solution

The state leaves the one-photon subspace and becomes vacuum in the two rails. This is not the logical zero, which still contains one photon.

13.2 Photonic Gates

13.2.1 Two-photon interference

Choose the balanced beam-splitter convention \(a^\dagger\mapsto(c^\dagger+d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\), \(b^\dagger\mapsto(c^\dagger-d^\dagger)/\sqrt2\). One identical photon in each input produces \((\ket{2,0}-\ket{0,2})/\sqrt2\). The coincidence terms cancel. This Hong–Ou–Mandel effect requires indistinguishability in the unmeasured degrees of freedom; distinguishable photons do not exhibit the same cancellation.

13.2.2 Measurement-induced operations

Passive mode mixing alone does not provide a deterministic universal entangling gate on dual-rail qubits. Ancillary photons, interference, detection, and feed-forward can induce conditional operations. KLM establishes a route to scalable computation under idealized resource assumptions; its general construction is beyond this chapter. The central hardware question is how success probability, resource preparation, and loss affect the cost of an accepted logical operation.

Problem 13.2. What is the ideal coincidence probability for one identical photon in each balanced input?

Worked solution

Zero: expanding the creation operators cancels the term with one photon in each output. For fully distinguishable photons the probability is one half.

13.3 Detection and Architecture

13.3.1 Loss and heralding

Photon counters convert optical occupation to classical records, usually consuming the photons. Efficiency, false counts, timing resolution, and number resolution matter differently across protocols. Loss can erase a qubit before any intended measurement. Heralding can flag some failures, but discarding unsuccessful runs must be included in both the runtime and the interpretation of the reported output distribution.

13.3.2 Resource states and multiplexing

Large photonic computations often prepare entangled resource states and connect them through measurements. Multiplexing chooses successful source attempts from many parallel channels, at the price of switches, delay lines, and extra loss. Thus a device with excellent interferometers can still be limited by sources and detection. A complete design counts all photons and optical storage time, including discarded resources.

Problem 13.3. If independent sources succeed with probability 0.1, what is the chance that at least one of ten succeeds?

Worked solution

It is 1 − 0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.6513, before switching loss. Multiplexing improves availability but does not eliminate the cost of routing the successful photon.

A mode can hold more than a single photon. Using that larger space for encoding leads to bosonic error-protection strategies.

Sources and further study: Knill, Laflamme, and Milburn, linear optics.
