Physical Qubits

When can two states of a larger physical system be treated as a qubit?

Chapter direction

Starting point: Chapters 1–1; physical concepts and approximations are introduced as needed.

Anchor example: Three energy levels and a drive intended to address only two.

This lesson includes explanations, worked examples, and problems with solutions. Advanced results whose proofs are outside the chapter are identified explicitly.

  1. 2.1 Energy Levels
  2. 2.2 Qubit Encodings
  3. 2.3 Effective Dynamics

2.1 Energy Levels

A device has energy levels E0, E1, and E2. We want to rotate only the lowest two. The third level matters even when the circuit drawing has only one wire: a drive can accidentally put amplitude there.

2.1.1 Stationary states and frequencies

An energy eigenstate evolves by a phase \(e^{-iEt/\hbar}\), where ℏ is Planck’s constant divided by 2π. For a superposition of two levels, the relative phase advances at angular frequency \(\omega_{01}=(E_1-E_0)/\hbar\). Ordinary frequency is \(f_{01}=\omega_{01}/2\pi\). A common energy offset changes only a global phase. We will state whether frequencies are quoted in cycles per second or radians per second.

2.1.2 The two-level approximation

The qubit approximation keeps two chosen levels and neglects the rest. This requires both a preparation confined to that subspace and operations that approximately preserve it. A harmonic oscillator has equally spaced transitions; a drive resonant with its first transition can also excite the next. Anharmonicity, the difference between neighboring transition frequencies, can permit selective addressing.

Problem 2.1. If E1−E0=hf, what relative phase accumulates in time 1/(2f)?

Worked solution

The excited component gains exp(−iπ)=−1 relative to the ground component. This is half a cycle, not half a radian.

2.2 Qubit Encodings

2.2.1 Computational subspaces

An encoding need not use the two lowest energy states. Hyperfine spin states, two optical paths, or a protected multi-system subspace can serve as zero and one. The choice affects susceptibility to noise and which controls connect the states. A well-defined encoding specifies a basis, a phase reference, and the larger state space in which errors may occur.

2.2.2 Leakage and selection rules

Leakage is amplitude outside the computational subspace, not simply a wrong zero or one. A selection rule can suppress a transition because the control operator has zero matrix element between the relevant levels. Such protection is conditional on the symmetry and control fields. If an unwanted transition is detuned by Δ and coupled with strength Ω, a simple two-level model bounds its population by \(\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)\); this is a useful warning, not a universal multilevel leakage formula.

Problem 2.2. Does a zero computational-basis error guarantee no leakage?

Worked solution

No. If leaked outcomes are ignored or misclassified, the reported bit statistics can conceal population outside the chosen subspace.

2.3 Effective Dynamics

2.3.1 Hamiltonians and rotating frames

In our convention \(Z=\ket0\bra0-\ket1\bra1\), so a system with level one higher in energy has \(H_0=-\hbar\omega_{01}Z/2\) after removing a common offset. A Hamiltonian is the matrix generating evolution. Moving to a rotating coordinate frame removes the rapid reference phase and exposes the drive’s slow action. Frame changes do not physically rotate the state by themselves; they change its mathematical description.

2.3.2 Testing the approximation

Test the approximation by varying drive strength and detuning, checking population in extra levels, and comparing different pulse lengths. A good fit to one oscillation does not certify a closed two-level system over an entire computation. Keep the neglected coupling scales visible when moving from a physical model to a gate specification.

Problem 2.3. Why is an equally spaced oscillator not automatically a selective two-level qubit?

Worked solution

A resonant field can couple several neighboring transitions at the same frequency. A usable encoding needs additional nonlinearity, selection, or control structure.
