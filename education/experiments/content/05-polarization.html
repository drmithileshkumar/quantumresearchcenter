5.1 The question and the apparatus

Prepare horizontally polarized light and place a vertical analyzer in its path. An ideal analyzer transmits none. Now insert a third polarizer between them, at an angle θ from horizontal. Some light reaches the output. The extra filter changes the state reaching the final analyzer; it is not simply removing members of an unchanged collection.

Polarization is a useful bridge between classical waves and quantum states. Classical electric-field components obey the same two-component amplitude mathematics as a single photon's polarization. An intensity demonstration alone is therefore not evidence that the light consists of single photons. The photon version interprets normalized transmitted intensity as a transmission probability for identically prepared photons.

We assume ideal linear polarizers, a fixed propagation direction, and a detector that counts all transmitted light equally. Real polarizers have finite extinction ratios and insertion losses. Never point an unattenuated beam or a laser at an eye; the browser activity needs no physical optical equipment.

5.2 Build a prediction

Choose |H⟩=(1,0) and |V⟩=(0,1). The transmitted state of an analyzer at θ is |θ⟩=cosθ|H⟩+sinθ|V⟩. Its overlap with H is cosθ, giving Malus's law P=cos²θ. Unlike the spin-axis formula in Chapter 4, the angle here directly describes an optical polarization direction.

After transmission through the middle filter, the state is |θ⟩. Its probability of passing the vertical filter is sin²θ. Multiplying the conditional probabilities gives \(P_{H\to\theta\to V}=\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta=\tfrac14\sin^2(2\theta)\). The maximum is 1/4 at θ=45°.

The order is essential. Placing the angled filter after the crossed H and V pair cannot restore light already absorbed. Matrix multiplication captures the order: the output amplitude is PV Pθ|H⟩, where each P is a projection onto the transmitted state. Generally these projections do not commute.

5.3 What the evidence can establish

The experiment shows why a filter is both a selection and a preparation. It also gives an accessible example of measurement order. It does not establish consciousness-induced collapse or distinguish quantum theory from classical polarization optics. Stronger claims require additional observables, such as the counting statistics of Chapter 6.

Measure the input intensity after the preparation filter, not before it, if you want to compare directly with our probability. For initially unpolarized light, the first ideal polarizer transmits half the incident intensity, making the maximum transmission through all three filters 1/8 of the original.

Explore the experiment

Find the middle-filter angle giving maximum output. Then move it to 0° and 90° and explain the zero in each case. Follow both the intermediate and final transmission.

A worked example

Start with 1,000 H-polarized trials and set the middle filter to 30°. What is the mean final count?

The middle filter transmits 3/4. The final vertical filter transmits 1/4 of those, so the mean is 1,000×3/16=187.5. An actual count is an integer and fluctuates around that mean.

Try it yourself

Problem 5.1

What is the maximum transmission relative to an H-prepared input?

Hint

Maximize sin²(2θ).

Worked solution

1/4, reached at 45° within the 0°–90° range.

Problem 5.2

Does a classical laser demonstration prove photon quantization?

Hint

Ask which classical wave prediction differs.

Worked solution

No. Classical polarization optics predicts the same normalized intensities here.

Problem 5.3

Why does putting the angled filter after V fail?

Hint

Apply the crossed projections first.

Worked solution

PV|H⟩=0. Once the ideal crossed pair transmits no amplitude, a later passive filter has no transmitted field to act on.

What to carry forward

Track the state after each operation. A matching amplitude formula does not make every optical demonstration uniquely quantum.
