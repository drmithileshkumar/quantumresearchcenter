1.1 The question and the apparatus

Imagine a source that sends one system towards a two-outcome detector. A screen displays 53 results labelled A and 47 labelled B. Does this establish that A is more likely? Before calculating, ask what counted as a trial. Were missing events discarded? Was the source stable? Did the two detectors have equal efficiencies? A list of numbers becomes evidence only when these operational details are attached.

Separate preparation, transformation, and measurement. Preparation specifies what is sent in; transformation describes the controlled operation; measurement specifies the possible records. Repeat the same procedure to estimate probabilities. A quantum state is a tool for predicting those probabilities, not a photograph of the object between observations. Different explanations may fit the same finite dataset.

Our first apparatus is intentionally simple: a source, a selectable probability model, and two counters. In the browser, a seeded random-number generator supplies events. It has no quantum hardware behind it. Replaying the same seed reproduces the same sequence, which is useful for debugging and makes the distinction between a model and evidence concrete.

1.2 Build a prediction

For independent trials with a constant probability p of A, the count k follows a binomial distribution. Its mean is Np and its standard deviation is √(Np(1−p)). Dividing by N gives the estimated proportion and its approximate standard error: \(\hat p=k/N\), \(\mathrm{SE}\simeq\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/N}\). The square root is why four times as many trials roughly halves sampling uncertainty.

For 100 balanced trials, the count standard deviation is five. A difference of three from the expected 50 is ordinary fluctuation. A standard error is not a guarantee that the true value lies in a specified interval. Near zero or one, or for small samples, symmetric normal approximations can be misleading. A likelihood or a binomial interval is preferable.

Random uncertainty and systematic error behave differently. Repetition reduces the first, but unequal detection efficiencies can persist indefinitely. If A is detected with efficiency ηA and B with ηB, the observed A fraction among clicks is pηA/[pηA+(1−p)ηB]. Conditioning on detected events changes the population you are describing.

1.3 What the evidence can establish

A credible experiment reports a protocol, uncertainties, calibration, and a reason a competing model cannot explain the result equally well. A graph matching a curve is a beginning. It is stronger if the curve's parameters were calibrated independently or if the experiment predicts measurements not used in the fit.

Use an assumption ledger throughout this course: write down independence, stable preparation, fair sampling, and the measurement model when they are needed. Later Bell and observer experiments depend on more specific assumptions. Keeping those visible prevents a statement about one model from turning into a claim about every possible explanation.

The animation illustrates the convergence of sample frequencies, not the physical origin of randomness. Its statistics are generated by the probability that you choose. It cannot be used to establish the Born rule, certify a random-number generator, or demonstrate a violation of a classical bound.

Explore the experiment

Choose p=0.5, advance individual events, then run a longer sequence. Predict the scale of fluctuations before increasing N. Reset reproduces the sequence; changing p starts a new dataset.

A worked example

A fair source gives 530 A clicks in 1,000 trials. Is the difference from 500 large?

The count standard deviation under the fair model is √250 ≈ 15.8. The excess is 30/15.8 ≈ 1.90 standard deviations. This is not decisive evidence of bias, especially if many datasets were inspected. Check calibration and a prespecified statistical test before making a discovery claim.

Try it yourself

Problem 1.1

How many trials reduce the standard error from 0.05 to 0.01 at p=1/2?

Hint

Use SE=1/(2√N).

Worked solution

N=2,500. The original error corresponds to N=100, so five times the precision requires 25 times as many trials.

Problem 1.2

A fair source is detected with ηA=1 and ηB=1/2. What A fraction appears among clicks?

Hint

Keep the undetected B events in the denominator until conditioning.

Worked solution

The fraction is 0.5/(0.5+0.25)=2/3. The source need not be biased; the selected dataset is.

Problem 1.3

Would one million simulated clicks validate a detector?

Hint

Identify where the numbers came from.

Worked solution

No. They test the simulation and analysis under its assumptions. Detector validation requires measurements of the physical detector, including its response to independently controlled inputs.

What to carry forward

An experimental conclusion joins a preparation, a measurement model, data, and assumptions. More samples do not repair an incorrect measurement model.
