13.1 The question and the apparatus

Prepare an atom, ion, or effective qubit in its ground state. Apply an oscillating field near its transition frequency for a controlled duration, then measure the excited-state population. Repeating for different durations produces oscillations rather than a monotonic accumulation of excitation. The drive controls an amplitude, not merely an excitation rate.

Initialization, drive calibration, and readout are separate operations. A device can have excellent coherent control and poor measurement fidelity, or the reverse. Measure the ground and excited readout responses independently before attributing a small oscillation contrast entirely to the drive.

Our display uses a Bloch sphere: its north pole is |0⟩, its south pole |1⟩, and equatorial points are equal-population superpositions with different phases. The arrow represents a state, not the direction of a classical object's spin in ordinary space. Its rotation is a compact picture of the amplitude calculation.

13.2 Build a prediction

In a resonant rotating-frame approximation, the Hamiltonian is H=ℏΩX/2. Evolving |0⟩ for time t gives \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\cos(\Omega t/2)|0\rangle-i\sin(\Omega t/2)|1\rangle\). Squaring the second amplitude gives P1=sin²(Ωt/2). The pulse area θ=Ωt is the angle used in the animation.

A π pulse ideally transfers the population to |1⟩. A π/2 pulse creates equal populations, but its relative phase depends on the drive phase. This is why measuring only population after one pulse cannot completely characterize the prepared state.

With detuning Δ and a constant drive, the ideal excitation is [Ω²/(Ω²+Δ²)]sin²(√(Ω²+Δ²)t/2). Detuning changes both the amplitude and frequency. The explorer deliberately uses resonance; it does not silently interpret a detuned or decaying curve as the same model.

13.3 What the evidence can establish

Fit a duration scan with uncertainties, check the readout calibration, and repeat at several drive powers. A coherent-drive model predicts a systematic dependence of Ω on the field amplitude. A fit alone does not establish that leakage to higher levels or pulse distortion is negligible.

The rotating-wave and two-level approximations have a range of validity. Strong drives or nearby transitions can invalidate them. Calibration on isolated pulses may also fail in a longer sequence because of drift, heating, or interaction with other systems.

The animation traces a coherent rotation and plots its expected population. It does not estimate fidelity from a single oscillation. Process characterization and benchmarking require additional preparations and measurements; the course later introduces state tomography as a first step.

Explore the experiment

Advance the pulse area by small steps. Locate π/2, π, and 2π. Compare the arrow with the excited-state probability and explain why equal populations are not a unique state.

A worked example

A resonant Rabi frequency Ω/2π is 5 MHz. How long is a π pulse?

tπ=π/Ω=1/(2×5 MHz)=100 ns. The π/2 pulse takes 50 ns under the same constant drive.

Try it yourself

Problem 13.1

What is P1 after a 2π pulse?

Hint

Evaluate sin²π.

Worked solution

Zero. The state returns to the same physical ray, with an overall minus sign.

Problem 13.2

Can P1=1/2 certify a coherent superposition?

Hint

Compare a pure state with a balanced mixture.

Worked solution

No. Both can give half population. A measurement in another basis is needed to test coherence.

Problem 13.3

If the pulse area is π+ε, what is the leading population error?

Hint

Expand cos²(ε/2).

Worked solution

P1≈1−ε²/4, so the population error is approximately ε²/4 for small ε.

What to carry forward

Coherent control rotates amplitudes. Population scans calibrate part of that control but do not fully specify the prepared state.
