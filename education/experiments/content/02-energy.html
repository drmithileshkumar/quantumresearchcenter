2.1 The question and the apparatus

Illuminate a metal and collect the electrons it emits. Two knobs have different roles: light frequency changes the energy associated with each photon, while intensity changes the incident flux. A retarding voltage opposes the electrons. The stopping voltage is the point where even the most energetic emitted electrons fail to reach the collector.

In a second experiment, excite a dilute gas and resolve its emitted light with a spectrometer. The spectrum contains narrow features associated with transitions between energy levels. In the Franck–Hertz experiment, accelerated electrons lose energy to atoms in discrete excitation events, producing structure in the collected current. These are different apparatuses that constrain the same idea of allowed energy differences.

The visual focuses on the photoelectric energy balance. It draws a surface barrier and an emitted-electron energy gauge. It is not a microscopic animation of electron motion within a metal. Real surfaces have work-function variations, contact potentials, finite temperature, and distributions of initial electron energies.

2.2 Build a prediction

In the elementary photoelectric model, the maximum kinetic energy is \(K_{\max}=hf-\Phi\) when hf≥Φ. Here h is Planck's constant, f is frequency, and Φ is the work function. Below threshold this single-photon model predicts no photoemission from the occupied states represented by the model. The stopping potential satisfies eVs=Kmax.

Using electron-volts makes the arithmetic simple: an electron crossing one volt gains one electron-volt. For a work function of 2 eV and photon energy of 3 eV, the maximum kinetic energy is 1 eV and the stopping voltage is 1 V. Doubling intensity at fixed frequency increases the event rate in the unsaturated regime; it does not double that maximum energy.

For spectroscopy, photon energy is an energy difference: hf=Ei−Ef. A line measures the separation of levels, not their absolute position relative to an arbitrary zero. Repeated Franck–Hertz features require a transport model as well as excitation thresholds; one should not identify every dip with a new atomic level.

2.3 What the evidence can establish

A threshold and a linear stopping-voltage relation test a specific energy balance. They do not, by themselves, establish every feature of the quantum electromagnetic field. Some photoelectric observations admit descriptions with a classical field coupled to quantized matter. Chapter 6 introduces photon statistics that more directly distinguish nonclassical light.

For a reliable stopping-voltage measurement, subtract dark current, check voltage calibration, and vary frequency across several points. Estimate the slope and intercept with uncertainties instead of extracting a constant from a single point. For spectra, account for instrumental broadening before interpreting linewidth as a lifetime.

A worked example

Two spectral lines have photon energies 2 eV and 3 eV and share the same lower level. How far apart are their upper levels?

Their upper levels differ by 1 eV because the common lower energy cancels. This does not identify the absolute energies of any of the three levels.

Try it yourself

Problem 2.1

For Φ=2 eV and hf=3.5 eV, find Kmax and Vs.

Hint

Use the energy balance and the definition of an electron-volt.

Worked solution

Kmax=1.5 eV and Vs=1.5 V.

Problem 2.2

Does twice the light intensity imply twice the stopping voltage?

Hint

Separate rate from maximum energy.

Worked solution

No, in the single-photon unsaturated model the maximum energy is unchanged. More photons can produce more electrons per unit time.

Problem 2.3

Why does a spectrum not directly give absolute atomic energy levels?

Hint

Consider shifting every energy by the same constant.

Worked solution

Transition frequencies depend on differences. Adding a common energy offset changes no spectral frequency.

What to carry forward

Energy-resolved measurements constrain allowed energy exchanges. Separate the photon energy, the count rate, and the assumptions about the material.
