14.1 The question and the apparatus

Apply a π/2 pulse, wait for time t, then apply a second π/2 pulse and measure. During the wait, a frequency difference between the system and the reference drive accumulates a phase. The second pulse converts that phase into a population difference. This is Ramsey interferometry, with time playing the role of the separation between interferometer arms.

Repeated runs can lose contrast because individual systems acquire different phases or because uncontrolled environment interactions carry away coherence. A spin-echo pulse halfway through the wait can refocus static frequency offsets. It cannot generally reverse energy relaxation or arbitrary fluctuating noise.

The visual compares a damped Ramsey fringe with an idealized echo envelope. It fixes a phenomenological dephasing time and a chosen oscillation period. The echo comparison represents perfectly refocusable static spread without other decoherence; it is an upper-limit toy model, not a promise of perfect recovery in a laboratory.

14.2 Build a prediction

For a suitable final-pulse phase convention, P0(t)=[1+V(t)cos(Δt)]/2. An exponential model uses V(t)=e−t/T₂. T1 describes energy relaxation, while T2 describes decay of transverse coherence. In a simple Markovian qubit model, 1/T2=1/(2T1)+1/Tφ, where Tφ characterizes pure dephasing.

An ensemble of static detunings has visibility equal to the magnitude of its characteristic function ⟨eiΔt⟩. Gaussian detuning noise produces a Gaussian envelope, not necessarily an exponential. The fitted symbol T2* often includes this inhomogeneous broadening. State the envelope before quoting a coherence time.

An ideal π echo reverses the sign of phase accumulation from a constant detuning during the second half. The accumulated phases cancel, while irreversible loss to an inaccessible environment need not. The ability to recover a signal supplies information about the noise; it does not establish that all apparent decoherence is reversible.

14.3 What the evidence can establish

Fit relaxation and coherence experiments separately. Correct for imperfect pulse contrast, measure enough delays to resolve the envelope, and avoid using an unconstrained exponential fit when the data show revivals or oscillatory noise. Different pulse sequences probe different parts of a noise spectrum.

A diagonal reduced density matrix predicts local statistics but does not identify one definite global outcome. The cat chapter returns to that distinction. Here the operational task is narrower: measure how well a phase-sensitive signal survives and what an echo can recover.

A worked example

At t=T2 and Δt=0 modulo 2π, what is P0 for unit initial contrast?

P0=(1+e⁻¹)/2≈0.684. At the opposite phase it is about 0.316. The fringe contrast is e⁻¹, not either of these probabilities individually.

Try it yourself

Problem 14.1

For T1=100 μs and no pure dephasing, what is T2?

Hint

Use the stated Markovian relation.

Worked solution

T2=2T1=200 μs within that model.

Problem 14.2

Does an echo restore a particle already lost from the apparatus?

Hint

Identify what the pulse reverses.

Worked solution

No. Refocusing phase does not undo particle loss or ordinary irreversible relaxation.

Problem 14.3

Why can a Gaussian envelope arise?

Hint

Average exp(iΔt) over a Gaussian static detuning distribution.

Worked solution

The average is exp(−σ²t²/2), so static frequency spread gives a Gaussian contrast decay.

What to carry forward

Distinguish phase evolution, ensemble averaging, and irreversible noise. A coherence time belongs to a specified model and measurement sequence.
