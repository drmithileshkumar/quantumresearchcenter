7.1 The question and the apparatus

A Mach–Zehnder interferometer splits a beam into two paths, redirects them with mirrors, and recombines them at a second beam splitter. A phase plate changes the relative phase. With suitable alignment, all ideal single-photon trials can arrive at one output even though either path would separately feed both outputs.

Both paths must describe indistinguishable alternatives at recombination. Different polarization, frequency, arrival time, or an attached path record can reduce visibility. The apparatus is therefore a controlled comparison of alternatives, not merely a way of splitting an intensity in half twice.

The drawing represents the source, two beam splitters, a phase element, and two detectors. Glowing path segments show amplitude alternatives in the model. They should not be read as observations of a photon travelling along two measured trajectories.

7.2 Build a prediction

Use a real balanced beam splitter represented by the Hadamard matrix H. This phase convention is mathematically convenient; an optical implementation may distribute reflection phases differently. Starting in |0⟩, the first splitter prepares (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2. A phase element produces (|0⟩+eiφ|1⟩)/√2.

The second H gives output amplitudes (1+eiφ)/2 and (1−eiφ)/2. Thus \(P_0=(1+\cos\phi)/2\) and \(P_1=(1-\cos\phi)/2\). They sum to one. At φ=0, output 1 is dark; at φ=π, output 0 is dark. At φ=π/2 both outputs have probability 1/2.

If an uncontrolled process reduces the coherence by a real factor V, the same calculation gives P0=(1+V cosφ)/2. The contrast of the observed fringe is (Pmax−Pmin)/(Pmax+Pmin). A single phase setting cannot distinguish a phase error from reduced contrast; scan the phase.

7.3 What the evidence can establish

Classical coherent waves reproduce the intensity fringes. The single-photon version combines interference with indivisible detections, but the fringe shape alone is not a universal witness of quantization. The interpretation depends on how the source was characterized and what was recorded.

To measure visibility, stabilize path lengths and collect counts across a full phase period. Fit detector offsets and efficiency differences instead of automatically interpreting every imbalance as phase. A source drift during a sequential scan can mimic a distorted fringe; interleaving phase settings is one possible control.

This simple experiment will be reused in delayed choice, quantum erasure, and interaction-free measurement. Each modifies a different physical operation. Keeping the same amplitude convention makes those modifications easy to calculate without introducing stories about what the photon must have known.

Explore the experiment

Sweep φ through a full turn. Pause at 0°, 90°, and 180° and predict the detector probabilities. Watch the amplitude markers meet at recombination rather than interpreting them as particle tracks.

A worked example

What phase gives P0=3/4 in the ideal interferometer?

Solve (1+cosφ)/2=3/4, giving cosφ=1/2. Within one full turn the solutions are φ=60° and 300°. One probability alone therefore does not fix the phase uniquely.

Try it yourself

Problem 7.1

What happens if the second beam splitter is removed?

Hint

Measure the path-basis populations before H.

Worked solution

The two detectors each receive probability 1/2, independent of φ. The relative phase is not visible in path counts.

Problem 7.2

For V=0.6, find the maximum and minimum P0.

Hint

Set cosφ to ±1.

Worked solution

The extrema are 0.8 and 0.2, giving visibility (0.8−0.2)/(0.8+0.2)=0.6.

Problem 7.3

Why must both output probabilities sum to one?

Hint

Use a lossless two-mode model.

Worked solution

Unitary evolution preserves the norm. If measured probabilities do not sum to one relative to emitted trials, include loss or additional outcomes.

What to carry forward

A relative phase becomes a population difference only after a suitable recombination. State the beam-splitter convention and keep it consistent.
