9.1 The question and the apparatus

Start with the Mach–Zehnder interferometer. After the input has passed the first beam splitter, choose whether to insert the second. With it present, the paths recombine and the output depends on phase. With it absent, the output ports resolve the two path modes. Wheeler's question challenges the assumption that a photon must first decide to behave as a wave or a particle.

The sequence matters: preparation, first splitter, phase accumulation, setting choice, final detection. A delayed choice is still an ordinary choice of a later measurement operation. It does not replace an already recorded detection with another result. A convincing experimental implementation specifies the timing and separation of the setting choice and the photon's passage.

The display lets you switch between open and closed configurations and step through that sequence. Its moving indicator is a timing guide. It is not a hidden history showing where a photon really travelled between measurements.

9.2 Build a prediction

Immediately before the possible second splitter, write |ψ⟩=(|0⟩+eiφ|1⟩)/√2. For the open apparatus, the probabilities are the path populations, both 1/2. For the closed apparatus, applying H gives P0=(1+cosφ)/2 and P1=(1−cosφ)/2. The earlier state need not be recalculated backwards in time.

These are two different observables on the same incoming state. A state can give equal probabilities in one basis and a certain result in another. Calling the first result particle-like and the second wave-like is a description of the measurement statistics, not a dynamical law forcing an earlier identity switch.

The animation uses a balanced, lossless model. At φ=0, closing the interferometer changes the prediction from two equal output probabilities to one certain output. Choosing later changes which transformation is physically applied before detection. It leaves any earlier saved records unchanged.

9.3 What the evidence can establish

Laboratory delayed-choice experiments implement the relevant timing and compare the two configurations. They test interference and path-sensitive measurements under a late setting selection. They do not establish that the future sends a message into the past. The published apparatus must be examined to understand what information could propagate between events.

An interpretation can offer a different account of unobserved history while agreeing on these probabilities. This course distinguishes that interpretive freedom from the experimentally checkable prediction. A surprising narrative is not an extra observable.

When using the display, keep the detection setting and phase fixed for a batch of synthetic trials. Comparing batches demonstrates the different probability models. It does not reproduce the spacetime arrangement, random setting generator, source characterization, or detection calibration of a physical delayed-choice experiment.

Explore the experiment

Set φ=0 and compare the open and closed arrangements. Step through the timing guide and identify the operation that converts phase into an output imbalance.

A worked example

At φ=90°, open and closed arrangements both give equal detector counts. Are the arrangements equivalent?

No. Their predictions coincide at that particular phase. At φ=0 the open device gives 1/2,1/2 and the closed device gives 1,0. Scan a parameter before declaring two models experimentally equivalent.

What to carry forward

A late measurement choice changes the operation before detection. The quantum prediction requires no rewriting of earlier records.
