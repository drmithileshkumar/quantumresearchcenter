6.1 Hanbury Brown–Twiss beam-splitter correlations

6.1.1 Measuring delay-resolved coincidences across two outputs

Send a light beam onto a balanced \(50{:}50\) beam splitter and place single-photon detectors \(A\) and \(B\) at the two output ports, linked to a time-to-digital coincidence counter that records pairs of clicks separated by relative time delay \(\tau\). Rather than measuring only the mean count rate (brightness), this Hanbury Brown–Twiss interferometer measures how the conditional probability of registering a click at detector \(B\) at time \(t+\tau\) depends on having registered a click at detector \(A\) at time \(t\).

6.1.2 True single-photon states versus attenuated laser pulses

An ideal single-photon Fock state \(\lvert n=1\rangle\) cannot be split into two detector clicks at \(\tau=0\): it triggers either detector \(A\) or detector \(B\), never both simultaneously. Attenuating a classical laser beam with neutral-density filters behaves differently: although absorption reduces the mean photon number \(\langle n\rangle\ll 1\), a coherent state retains a Poisson photon-number distribution in which the two-photon probability scales as \(\langle n\rangle^2/2\), so the normalized coincidence ratio at \(\tau=0\) remains unity regardless of attenuation. A dim laser is therefore not a true single-photon source.

6.2 Second-order correlation and the antibunching dip

6.2.1 Factorial photon-number moments at zero delay

For a single optical mode with photon-number operator \(n\) and nonzero mean \(\langle n\rangle>0\), the zero-delay normalized second-order correlation function is defined by the normal-ordered factorial moment

\[g^{(2)}(0) = \frac{\langle n(n-1)\rangle}{\langle n\rangle^2}.\]

For an ideal one-photon state \(\lvert 1\rangle\), \(n(n-1)\lvert 1\rangle=0\), yielding \(g^{(2)}(0)=0\). More generally, an \(n\)-photon Fock state \(\lvert n\rangle\) (\(n\ge 1\)) gives \(g^{(2)}(0)=(n-1)/n\). A coherent state \(\lvert\alpha\rangle\) with Poisson statistics satisfies \(\langle n(n-1)\rangle=\langle n\rangle^2\) and thus \(g^{(2)}(0)=1\), whereas a single-mode thermal (chaotic) state obeys Bose–Einstein statistics with \(\langle n(n-1)\rangle=2\langle n\rangle^2\) and \(g^{(2)}(0)=2\).

6.2.2 Time-delay recovery for antibunched, coherent, and thermal light

As the delay \(\tau\) increases well beyond the source coherence or emitter reset time \(\tau_c\), events at \(t\) and \(t+\tau\) become statistically independent and \(g^{(2)}(\tau)\to 1\). Our interactive explorer illustrates three canonical delay profiles with characteristic scale \(\tau_c=1\): \(g^{(2)}(\tau)=1-e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}\) for an antibunched single emitter, \(g^{(2)}(\tau)=1\) for a coherent laser, and \(g^{(2)}(\tau)=1+e^{-|\tau|/\tau_c}\) for bunched thermal light.

6.2.3 Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for classical intensity fluctuations

Why does observing \(g^{(2)}(0)<1\) certify nonclassical light? In semiclassical photodetection theory, where clicks are generated by a nonnegative classical stochastic intensity \(I(t)\ge 0\), the zero-delay correlation is \(g^{(2)}_{\mathrm{cl}}(0)=\langle I^2\rangle/\langle I\rangle^2\). Because the variance of any real random variable is nonnegative, \(\langle(I-\langle I\rangle)^2\rangle=\langle I^2\rangle-\langle I\rangle^2\ge 0\), every classical intensity distribution must satisfy

\[g^{(2)}_{\mathrm{cl}}(0) = \frac{\langle I^2\rangle}{\langle I\rangle^2} \ge 1, \qquad g^{(2)}_{\mathrm{cl}}(0) \ge g^{(2)}_{\mathrm{cl}}(\tau).\]

Observing \(g^{(2)}(0)<1\) (sub-Poissonian statistics, commonly benchmarked below the single-emitter threshold \(g^{(2)}(0)<1/2\)) or \(g^{(2)}(0)<g^{(2)}(\tau)\) (photon antibunching) directly violates the classical intensity inequality.

6.3 Dead time, accidental subtraction, and source claims

6.3.1 Why two detectors are needed to bypass single-channel dead time

Why split the beam into two detectors \(A\) and \(B\) instead of recording time intervals on a single avalanche photodiode? Every physical photon counter experiences a recovery dead time \(\tau_{\mathrm{dead}}\) (typically tens of nanoseconds) after firing, during which it cannot register a second photon. A single detector therefore shows an artificial zero-delay dip for \(|\tau|<\tau_{\mathrm{dead}}\) even under a classical laser beam! Cross-correlating two independent outputs of a beam splitter eliminates single-channel dead time at \(\tau=0\).

6.3.2 Separating photon-number purity from mode indistinguishability

While a measured dip \(g^{(2)}(0)\ll 1\) establishes that multiphoton emissions are strongly suppressed, it does not by itself prove that successive photons are emitted into a pure polarization state or into indistinguishable spectral-temporal wave packets. Testing whether two photons from the source are quantum-mechanically indistinguishable requires the two-photon Hong–Ou–Mandel interference experiment of Chapter 16.

6.3.3 Reporting coincidence windows and background corrections

Credible single-photon characterization reports both the raw \(g^{(2)}(0)\) extracted from a specified coincidence time bin \(\Delta\tau\) and any background-subtracted value alongside the measured dark-count rate and timing jitter.

A worked example

A heralded single-photon source emits vacuum \(\lvert 0\rangle\) with probability \(0.80\) and a single photon \(\lvert 1\rangle\) with probability \(0.20\), with zero probability of \(n\ge 2\) photons. Calculate \(\langle n\rangle\) and \(g^{(2)}(0)\).

The mean photon number per pulse is \(\langle n\rangle = 0\times 0.80 + 1\times 0.20 = 0.20\). Because \(n(n-1)=0\) for both \(n=0\) and \(n=1\), the factorial numerator vanishes: \(\langle n(n-1)\rangle=0\), giving \(g^{(2)}(0)=0/(0.20)^2=0\). Notice that adding vacuum loss reduces the delivery efficiency \(\langle n\rangle\) without degrading the ideal single-photon purity \(g^{(2)}(0)=0\).

Try it yourself

Problem 6.1

What is \(g^{(2)}(0)\) for an ideal two-photon Fock state \(\lvert n=2\rangle\)?

Hint

Evaluate \(\langle n(n-1)\rangle/\langle n\rangle^2\) at \(n=2\).

Worked solution

For \(\lvert 2\rangle\), \(\langle n\rangle=2\) and \(\langle n(n-1)\rangle=2(2-1)=2\), so \(g^{(2)}(0)=2/2^2=1/2=0.5\). Because \(g^{(2)}(0)=1/2<1\) is nonclassical yet corresponds to two photons, certifying single-photon emission against \(n\ge 2\) Fock states requires \(g^{(2)}(0)<1/2\).

Problem 6.2

Why is a balanced beam splitter with two independent detectors used to measure \(g^{(2)}(0)\) instead of timing successive clicks on a single detector?

Hint

Consider what happens to a single detector immediately after it registers a click at \(\tau=0\).

Worked solution

A single detector is blind during its electronic recovery dead time \(\tau_{\mathrm{dead}}\) after a click, creating a false dip near \(\tau=0\) for any light source. Splitting the beam between two independent detectors allows simultaneous arrivals at \(\tau=0\) to be recorded.

Problem 6.3

Does measuring \(g^{(2)}(0)=0.01\) guarantee that the emitted photons have pure horizontal polarization \(\lvert H\rangle\)?

Hint

Ask whether photon-number counters resolve polarization.

Worked solution

No. A nonpolarizing Hanbury Brown–Twiss setup measures photon-number correlations and is insensitive to whether the polarization state is pure \(\lvert H\rangle\) or completely unpolarized; polarization tomography is required separately.

What to carry forward

Average intensity and photon-number statistics answer distinct physical questions: sub-Poissonian correlation \(g^{(2)}(0)<1\) rules out classical intensity mixtures, while \(g^{(2)}(0)<1/2\) bounds multiphoton contamination below the two-photon Fock level.
