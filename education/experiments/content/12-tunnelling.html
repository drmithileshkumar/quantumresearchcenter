12.1 The question and the apparatus

Consider a one-dimensional particle of energy E incident on a finite rectangular potential barrier of height V0>E and width a. Classical motion with that energy cannot enter and cross the forbidden region. Quantum scattering instead matches a wavefunction and its derivative across both interfaces, leaving a nonzero transmitted amplitude.

In scanning tunnelling microscopy, a conducting tip is brought close to a surface and a tunnelling current is measured under an applied bias. The current depends strongly on separation, allowing sensitive imaging. A real image also depends on electronic states, voltage, and tip structure; it is not a simple photograph of hard atomic spheres.

The visual shows an ideal barrier and a stationary amplitude envelope. It does not depict a particle borrowing energy or accelerating through a wall. The transmitted component has the same energy as the incident component in this time-independent elastic model.

12.2 Build a prediction

Inside the barrier, the time-independent Schrödinger equation gives exponential solutions with \(\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\). For an opaque barrier, the leading width dependence is T∝e−2κa. This approximation describes the sensitivity to width but omits a prefactor and should not be used at a=0.

For equal potentials on both sides, exact matching gives \(T=[1+V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)/(4E(V_0-E))]^{-1}\). The browser fixes E/V0=1/2, making T=sech²(κa). This correctly tends to one as the width goes to zero. Reflection has probability R=1−T for this lossless model.

An evanescent amplitude is not a negative probability, and a nonzero tail is not by itself a particle trajectory. Transmission is determined by probability current through the full matched solution. The drawn envelope is schematic; the displayed transmission uses the exact restricted formula above.

12.3 What the evidence can establish

A measured exponential dependence on distance supports tunnelling models, but extracting a barrier height requires assumptions about effective mass, geometry, and available electronic states. Temperature-dependent activation and leakage paths can complicate an interpretation. Compare alternative mechanisms across more than one control parameter.

The experiment does not violate energy conservation: a static barrier exchanges no net energy with the particle. Time-dependent barriers and inelastic scattering are different problems. Similarly, a stationary transmission coefficient is not a direct measurement of a unique time spent under the barrier.

Explore the experiment

Increase the dimensionless barrier width κa. Compare T and R and note the rapid suppression. Return to zero width to check the exact formula against its thin-barrier limit.

A worked example

For κa=1 at E/V0=1/2, estimate transmission.

cosh(1)≈1.543, so T=1/cosh²(1)≈0.420 and R≈0.580. The crude exponential alone, e⁻²≈0.135, misses the prefactor and is not accurate in this regime.

Try it yourself

Problem 12.1

What is T at zero width?

Hint

Take sinh(0)=0 in the exact expression.

Worked solution

T=1. No finite barrier remains.

Problem 12.2

Does a transmitted particle lose energy in this model?

Hint

The potential is time independent and equal on both sides.

Worked solution

No. Elastic transmission preserves its energy; the probability changes, not the outgoing energy.

Problem 12.3

What is the approximate change in T when a thick barrier grows by 1/κ?

Hint

Use the exponential dependence only in its valid regime.

Worked solution

T is multiplied by approximately e⁻²≈0.135, with other parameters fixed.

What to carry forward

Tunnelling is a boundary-matching prediction with measurable transmission. It does not require temporary violations of energy conservation.
