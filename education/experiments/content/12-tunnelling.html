12.1 Sub-barrier scattering and scanning tunnelling probes

12.1.1 Boundary continuity across a classically forbidden region

When a particle of total energy \(E\) approaches a one-dimensional rectangular potential barrier of height \(V_0>E\) and thickness \(a\), classical mechanics requires the particle to reflect at the front interface because kinetic energy \(E-V_0\) cannot be negative. In quantum mechanics, the stationary wavefunction \(\psi(x)\) and its spatial derivative \(d\psi/dx\) must remain continuous across both barrier boundaries \(x=0\) and \(x=a\). Because the evanescent wavefunction decays exponentially rather than vanishing instantly inside \(0<x<a\), a finite barrier thickness \(a\) leaves a nonzero transmitted wave for \(x>a\).

12.1.2 Tip–sample gap sensitivity in scanning tunnelling microscopy

In a scanning tunnelling microscope (STM), an atomically sharp conducting tip is held a few angstroms (\(a\sim 0.5\text{ nm}\)) above a conducting surface under a small bias voltage. Electrons tunnel across the vacuum work-function barrier between tip and sample, and because the transmission probability varies exponentially with the gap width \(a\), changing the tip–surface height by just \(0.1\text{ nm}\) typically alters the tunnelling current by an order of magnitude—enabling atomic-scale topographic and local density-of-states imaging.

12.1.3 Elastic stationary scattering without energy borrowing

In the apparatus schematic below, a stationary scattering wave at energy \(E=V_0/2\) impinges on a rectangular barrier of height \(V_0\) and width \(a\). Elastic tunnelling does not involve a particle "borrowing" extra energy \(\Delta E\ge V_0-E\) to hop over the top of the barrier: the transmitted wave emerges on the far side with the exact same energy \(E\) and wavelength as the incident wave, only with reduced amplitude.

12.2 Evanescent decay constant and exact rectangular transmission

12.2.1 Imaginary wavevector inside the barrier and exponential scaling

Inside the classically forbidden region \(0<x<a\) where \(V(x)=V_0>E\), the time-independent Schrödinger equation \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x)+V_0\psi(x)=E\psi(x)\) has real exponential solutions \(e^{\pm\kappa x}\) characterized by the decay constant

\[\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}.\]

When the barrier is thick (\(\kappa a\gg 1\)), the transmission coefficient is dominated by the exponential attenuation factor \(T\propto e^{-2\kappa a}\), explaining why small changes in \(a\), mass \(m\), or barrier excess \(V_0-E\) produce dramatic changes in transmission.

12.2.2 Exact boundary-matching formula and the thin-barrier limit

Matching \(\psi(x)\) and \(\psi'(x)\) at \(x=0\) and \(x=a\) for symmetric potentials \(V=0\) outside the barrier gives the exact transmission probability for all \(\kappa a\ge 0\):

\[T(\kappa a) = \left[1 + \frac{V_0^2\,\sinh^2(\kappa a)}{4E(V_0-E)}\right]^{-1}.\]

In our interactive model, the energy ratio is fixed at the midpoint \(E/V_0=1/2\), where \(V_0^2/[4E(V_0-E)]=1\) and the exact formula simplifies cleanly to

\[T(\kappa a) = \frac{1}{1+\sinh^2(\kappa a)} = \frac{1}{\cosh^2(\kappa a)} = \operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa a), \qquad R(\kappa a) = 1 - T(\kappa a) = \tanh^2(\kappa a).\]

Unlike the thick-barrier asymptotic approximation \(e^{-2\kappa a}\), the exact expression \(\operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa a)\) properly satisfies \(T(0)=1\) as the barrier width \(a\to 0\).

12.2.3 Probability current conservation across incident, reflected, and transmitted waves

Because the Hamiltonian is Hermitian and the potential \(V(x)\) is real, probability current is conserved across the barrier, enforcing unitarity \(T(\kappa a)+R(\kappa a)=1\) at every barrier width.

12.3 Separating elastic tunnelling from thermal activation

12.3.1 Testing distance and temperature dependence against competing models

In solid-state and molecular junctions, observing a small conductance across an insulating layer does not automatically prove coherent single-step elastic tunnelling: thermally activated hopping over the barrier (\(\propto e^{-(V_0-E)/(k_B T)}\)), defect-assisted multistep hopping, and pinhole shorts can also conduct current. Verifying elastic tunnelling requires checking that the conductance decays exponentially with barrier thickness \(a\) while remaining nearly independent of temperature \(T\) at low temperatures.

12.3.2 Energy conservation and the ambiguity of barrier traversal time

Because the stationary scattering eigenstates have definite energy \(E\), measuring the static transmission ratio \(T\) alone does not define a unique classical "tunnelling time" spent inside the forbidden region; wave-packet group delay, Larmor clock rotation, and modulated-barrier experiments measure distinct dynamical response functions.

12.3.3 From one-dimensional barrier models to real electrode interfaces

While the one-dimensional rectangular barrier captures the essential \(\kappa a\) scaling, quantitative STM spectroscopy also incorporates the three-dimensional tip curvature, image-charge rounding of the barrier, and the energy-dependent electronic density of states of both electrodes.

Explore the experiment

Sweep the dimensionless barrier thickness \(\kappa a\) from \(0\) to \(4\). Compare the exact transmission \(T=\operatorname{sech}^2(\kappa a)\) and reflection \(R=\tanh^2(\kappa a)\), and check both the zero-width limit \(\kappa a=0\) and the thick-barrier decay at \(\kappa a\ge 2\).

A worked example

At \(E/V_0=1/2\) and dimensionless barrier width \(\kappa a=1\), compute the exact transmission probability \(T\) and reflection probability \(R\), and compare \(T\) against the naive exponential factor \(e^{-2\kappa a}\).

At \(E/V_0=1/2\) and \(\kappa a=1\), \(\cosh(1)=(e+e^{-1})/2\approx 1.5431\), so the exact transmission is \(T=1/\cosh^2(1)\approx 0.41997\approx 0.420\) and the reflection probability is \(R=1-T\approx 0.580\). By contrast, the bare exponential \(e^{-2(1)}=e^{-2}\approx 0.1353\) omits the prefactor \(4\approx(2/\cosh 1)^2 e^2\) for \(E/V_0=1/2\) and underestimates \(T\) by more than a factor of three at \(\kappa a=1\).

Try it yourself

Problem 12.1

What does the exact transmission formula \(T=[1+V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)/(4E(V_0-E))]^{-1}\) give as the barrier width \(a\to 0\)?

Hint

Use \(\sinh(0)=0\).

Worked solution

Since \(\sinh(0)=0\), the denominator equals \(1+0=1\), giving \(T(0)=1\) and \(R(0)=0\) as required when the barrier disappears.

Problem 12.2

In elastic tunnelling through a static rectangular barrier, is the kinetic energy of a transmitted particle on the far side (\(x>a\)) smaller than its incident kinetic energy on the near side (\(x<0\))?

Hint

Compare the potential energy \(V(x)=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(x>a\) at fixed total energy \(E\).

Worked solution

No. Because the static barrier exchanges no energy with the particle and \(V(x)=0\) on both sides, a transmitted particle has the exact same kinetic energy \(E\) as an incident particle; the barrier reduces the fraction \(T\) of particles transmitted, not the energy of each transmitted particle.

Problem 12.3

In the thick-barrier regime (\(\kappa a\gg 1\)), by what factor does the transmission probability \(T\) change when the barrier width \(a\) increases by \(\Delta a=1/\kappa\)?

Hint

Evaluate \(e^{-2\kappa(a+1/\kappa)}/e^{-2\kappa a}\).

Worked solution

In the thick-barrier regime where \(\sinh(\kappa a)\approx\frac{1}{2}e^{\kappa a}\), increasing \(a\) by \(1/\kappa\) multiplies \(T\propto e^{-2\kappa a}\) by \(e^{-2}\approx 0.135\).

What to carry forward

Quantum tunnelling arises from boundary continuity of \(\psi(x)\) and \(\psi'(x)\) across a finite forbidden region, yielding exact transmission \(T=[1+V_0^2\sinh^2(\kappa a)/(4E(V_0-E))]^{-1}\) while conserving energy \(E\) and total probability \(T+R=1\).
