II · Paths, information, and time · Chapter 11

The Elitzur–Vaidman Bomb Tester

Can an object be detected in a trial in which it absorbs nothing?

In this chapter
  1. The question and the apparatus
  2. Build a prediction
  3. What the evidence can establish
  4. Interactive experiment
  5. Problems and solutions

11.1 The question and the apparatus

Balance an interferometer so output D1 is dark when both arms are open. Place an ideal absorber in one arm. In the original thought experiment the absorber is a sensitive bomb, but an opaque object suffices for the model. A photon hitting it is absorbed; a photon reaching the previously dark output can reveal its presence without an absorption in that successful trial.

The protocol has three outcomes: absorption, a bright-port detection that is inconclusive, and a dark-port detection that certifies the obstruction under ideal assumptions. Keeping all three is essential. The phrase interaction-free does not mean that every attempt avoids the object or that the entire arrangement has no physical interaction Hamiltonian.

Our drawing uses an absorber symbol and probability bars. It is entirely a browser model. The question concerns the logic of alternatives and null outcomes, not practical handling of dangerous devices.

11.2 Build a prediction

The first balanced splitter sends probability 1/2 towards the absorber. That branch is lost. Conditional on survival, only the other path remains; the second splitter sends half of the surviving probability to each detector. Thus \(P_{\rm absorb}=1/2\), \(P_{D0}=1/4\), and \(P_{D1}=1/4\).

With no object, both amplitudes reach the recombiner and D1 has probability zero. Therefore a D1 click distinguishes an obstruction from the empty ideal interferometer. A D0 click occurs in both cases and is inconclusive. The absence of interference is physically relevant even though the successful detector event did not deposit energy in the absorber.

Among nonabsorbed trials, half are successful certifications. Among all incident trials, only one quarter are. Another convention counts success divided by success plus absorption, giving 1/3 here. Always state the denominator before quoting an efficiency. Repeated weak interrogations can change the tradeoff but require a different protocol.

11.3 What the evidence can establish

An imperfectly balanced empty interferometer already has dark-port counts. A dark click then supplies evidence with a false-positive probability rather than logical certainty. Detector dark counts and partial transparency likewise change the inference. Characterize the empty-device baseline before introducing the object.

This protocol demonstrates that an unused alternative can affect observable probabilities. It does not establish the absence of every conceivable interaction in every interpretation. The operational claim is precise: in a successful ideal dark-port trial, an object is inferred without the modeled absorption event.

The simulator toggles an opaque absorber and compares the normalized probabilities of all outcomes. It does not simulate high-efficiency quantum-Zeno variants. Those extensions are a useful project only after the single-pass accounting is understood.

Explore the experiment

Compare the empty and obstructed devices. Follow all three outcome probabilities. Explain why a dark-port event is informative but a bright-port event is not decisive.

A worked example

For 400 incident photons with an ideal opaque object, what mean counts should be reported?

200 absorptions, 100 bright-port events, and 100 dark-port certifications. Reporting only the 200 surviving events would hide half the cost of the protocol.

Try it yourself

Problem 11.1

What is the unconditional certification probability?

Hint

Use all incoming trials.

Worked solution

1/4 for the balanced single-pass protocol.

Problem 11.2

Can a dark click prove an object is present if the empty baseline is nonzero?

Hint

Compare the two likelihoods.

Worked solution

It is evidence rather than certainty. A posterior probability also depends on the prior probability of an object and the calibrated false-positive rate.

Problem 11.3

Why does an opaque object not simply halve the original bright output?

Hint

Condition on the surviving path before recombination.

Worked solution

With one path removed, the cancellation at the dark port is absent. The surviving half is split between both outputs.

What to carry forward

A successful event and a successful protocol are different accounting questions. Include absorbed and inconclusive trials.
