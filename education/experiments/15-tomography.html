III · Control and reconstruction · Chapter 15

Reconstructing a Quantum State

How can several incompatible measurements describe one preparation?

In this chapter
  1. The question and the apparatus
  2. Build a prediction
  3. What the evidence can establish
  4. Interactive experiment
  5. Problems and solutions

15.1 The question and the apparatus

A z measurement alone cannot distinguish |+⟩ from a balanced mixture of |0⟩ and |1⟩. Both give equal z counts. Prepare many fresh copies of the same procedure and divide them among x, y, and z measurements. The extra bases reveal phase-sensitive information missing from the first dataset.

This is state tomography. You do not measure all three components on one untouched system. Each measurement consumes or changes its copy. The procedure assumes that the preparations across the three groups represent the same state and that the measurement axes are calibrated.

The explorer varies the polar angle and purity of a state in the xz plane. It shows a Bloch vector and the three predicted plus-outcome probabilities. The restriction y=0 is for legibility; the reconstruction formula itself includes all three components.

15.2 Build a prediction

Any qubit density matrix can be written \(\rho=(I+r_xX+r_yY+r_zZ)/2\), with a real vector r satisfying |r|≤1. The measured plus probabilities give rk=2P(+k)−1. Pure states lie on the unit sphere; mixed states lie inside.

The matrix is \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1+r_z&r_x-ir_y\\r_x+ir_y&1-r_z\end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenvalues are (1±|r|)/2, so |r|≤1 is the condition for nonnegative probabilities for every possible measurement. Purity is Trρ²=(1+|r|²)/2.

Finite independent samples can produce a linear estimate with |r|>1. This does not discover a new quantum state outside the sphere. It signals statistical or systematic error in an unconstrained estimate. Physical reconstruction methods impose positivity and should report uncertainties rather than silently clipping every component separately.

15.3 What the evidence can establish

Tomography is only as trustworthy as its preparation and measurement assumptions. If every axis is miscalibrated consistently, a reconstruction can look excellent and still describe the wrong physical operations. Self-consistent calibration and independent validation measurements address different parts of that problem.

Do not confuse an estimated density matrix with a unique preparation history. The maximally mixed state has many ensemble decompositions. Tomography predicts measurement statistics and cannot determine which decomposition was used without additional records.

The browser gives exact model probabilities, so the vector always remains physical. Use the exercises to examine noisy estimates. A practical tomography report should give the counts per setting, the measurement model, the fitting method, and a check against held-out settings.

Explore the experiment

Compare a pure equatorial state with a zero-length Bloch vector. Their z probabilities match, but their x probabilities differ. Rotate the pure state and predict all three measurement probabilities.

A worked example

Measured plus frequencies are Px=0.8, Py=0.5, Pz=0.9. Reconstruct the vector and check physicality.

r=(0.6,0,0.8), whose length is one. The reconstructed matrix is [[0.9,0.3],[0.3,0.1]]. Its determinant is zero and its trace one, so it is a pure state under the assumed calibration.

Try it yourself

Problem 15.1

What is the state if all three plus probabilities are 1/2?

Hint

Compute r component by component.

Worked solution

r=0 and ρ=I/2, the maximally mixed qubit state.

Problem 15.2

Why is Px=Py=Pz=0.9 not a physical exact qubit prediction?

Hint

Calculate |r|.

Worked solution

r=(0.8,0.8,0.8) has length √1.92>1, giving a negative eigenvalue in the linear reconstruction.

Problem 15.3

Can tomography distinguish different ensembles with the same density matrix?

Hint

Use the Born rule.

Worked solution

No system-only measurement distinguishes identical density matrices. Extra classical preparation records may distinguish their histories.

What to carry forward

Tomography reconstructs a statistical state from calibrated measurements on repeated preparations. Positivity and uncertainty are part of the inference.
