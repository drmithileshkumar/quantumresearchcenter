Tools / Two-qubit state

The state is
four arrows.

A two-qubit state is four complex numbers, and a complex number is a vector. So the whole state fits in one ordinary plane: four arrows whose lengths are the magnitudes and whose angles are the phases. Gates turn and add those arrows; the fifth, heavy arrow measures how entangled the pair is.

Why one plane is enough

A pure two-qubit state is four complex numbers, so it is four vectors in the plane — no projection, no loss. Two of the eight real numbers are not physical: the total length is fixed to one by normalization, and multiplying all four arrows by the same phase only rotates the whole figure, which no measurement can see. That leaves six, exactly the state's six parameters. This is the one common plot that holds everything, phases included.

Arrows that land on the same point are drawn side by side so that both stay visible; lengths and angles are untouched.

Gates act on these arrows in ways you can watch. Z and S turn the arrows whose qubit is |1⟩ and leave the others alone; H replaces a pair by their sum and difference (divided by √2), which is why it can move weight between arrows; X swaps pairs. A relative phase shows up as one arrow turning against the others, and that is the information a pair of ordinary Bloch spheres throws away: |Φ⁺⟩ and |Φ⁻⟩ have identical reduced states, but here one arrow is flipped through 180°.

The needle

Multiply the amplitudes that sit at opposite corners: c00c11 and c01c10. Each product is again a vector, with the phases added and the lengths multiplied. A state is a product of two single-qubit states exactly when those two vectors coincide. So draw the second from the tip of the first, and close the triangle: the closing arrow is 2·(c00c11 − c01c10). Its length is the concurrence, and it vanishes precisely when the state factorizes.

That gives entanglement a single number you can measure with a ruler, and a direction that records the relative phase. The length is basis-independent; the direction is a frame, and local gates rotate it without changing its length — which is the whole reason a local picture cannot see entanglement.

Things to try

  1. Press H on A from |00⟩. Two arrows appear along the real axis, each of length 0.707 — |00⟩ and |10⟩ — and the needle stays at zero: still a product state.
  2. Now press S on A. The |10⟩ arrow turns by 90° while |00⟩ stays put: a relative phase you can see, with the needle still at zero. Press T instead and it turns by 45°.
  3. Load Φ+ and Φ−. Both have the needle at full length 1.000; only its direction flips by 180°, and the |11⟩ arrow flips with it. This is the difference the Bloch spheres cannot show.
  4. Switch to the Bell basis. Each of the four Bell presets becomes a single arrow of length 1, while no product state can be: |00⟩ needs two arrows and |+0⟩ needs four. That is the sharpest way to see that Bell states are a different kind of object.
  5. Load |++⟩ and drag one arrow round the circle. Its length never changes, only its angle, and the needle grows from zero towards 0.707: a product state becoming entangled as the two products stop agreeing. Drag it back to 0° and the needle vanishes again.
  6. Load Partly entangled: the needle is 0.707 long, and the two starting arrows are 0.924 and 0.383 at the same angle.

This model follows pure states only, because unitary gates and projective measurements keep the state pure. A noisy two-qubit state needs a 4 × 4 density matrix and a mixed-state concurrence, and its arrows would need magnitudes that no longer add to one.
