What the sphere shows

A pure state of one qubit is a pair of complex numbers α and β with |α|² + |β|² = 1. An overall phase and an overall scale have no physical meaning, which leaves exactly two real parameters. This tool writes them as α = cos(θ/2) and β = eiφ sin(θ/2), and draws the arrow at (sin θ cos φ, sin θ sin φ, cos θ).

Because the arrow has length one, every pure state sits on the surface. Opposite points are orthogonal states: |0⟩ and |1⟩ on the vertical axis, |+⟩ and |−⟩ on x, |+i⟩ and |−i⟩ on y. A measurement along z returns 0 with probability |α|² and 1 with probability |β|², which is why moving the arrow around the equator leaves the probabilities alone: phase appears in interference, not in a single computational-basis readout.

The half angles are not decoration. Turning the qubit through 2π sends α and β to their negatives — a global phase, and the same point on the sphere. The arrow returns to its starting direction only after a 4π turn, and that sign has been measured with neutrons, not just argued.

The most general single-qubit gate

A gate must keep the total probability equal to one, so it is a 2 × 2 unitary matrix, \(U^\dagger U=I\). Every such matrix can be written with four real numbers, an overall phase \(\chi\) and three angles \(a\), \(b\), \(c\):

\[U=e^{i\chi}\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i(a+c)/2}\cos\frac{b}{2} & -e^{-i(a-c)/2}\sin\frac{b}{2}\\ e^{i(a-c)/2}\sin\frac{b}{2} & e^{i(a+c)/2}\cos\frac{b}{2}\end{pmatrix}=e^{i\chi}R_z(a)\,R_y(b)\,R_z(c),\]

where \(R_z(a)=e^{-iaZ/2}\) and \(R_y(b)=e^{-ibY/2}\) are rotations of the arrow about the \(z\) and \(y\) axes. So every gate is a turn about \(z\), a turn about \(y\) and another turn about \(z\). Equivalently, every gate is one rotation by an angle \(\omega\) about a single unit axis \(\hat n=(n_x,n_y,n_z)\):

\[U=e^{i\chi}\Bigl(\cos\tfrac{\omega}{2}\,I-i\sin\tfrac{\omega}{2}\,(n_xX+n_yY+n_zZ)\Bigr),\]

and its effect on the sphere is exactly that: the arrow turns by \(\omega\) about \(\hat n\). The phase \(e^{i\chi}\) multiplies \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) together and never moves the arrow, which is why the sphere needs only three of the four numbers. The half angles are the same ones as in the amplitudes: a full turn, \(\omega=2\pi\), gives \(-I\).

The six buttons above are special cases. X, Y and Z are half-turns, \(\omega=\pi\), about the \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) axes, each times a phase \(i\). H is \(i\) times a half-turn about the axis \((1,0,1)/\sqrt2\), halfway between \(x\) and \(z\), which is why it swaps |0⟩ and |+⟩. S and T are quarter- and eighth-turns about \(z\): \(S=e^{i\pi/4}R_z(\pi/2)\) and \(T=e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\pi/4)\).

Things to try

  1. Set θ = 30° and compare α with cos 15° = 0.966 and β with sin 15° = 0.259.
  2. From |0⟩ press H: the arrow lands on |+⟩ with probabilities 0.500 and 0.500. Press S: the arrow moves to |+i⟩ and P(0) does not move.
  3. From |0⟩ press X to reach |1⟩, then H to reach |−⟩. Both |+⟩ and |−⟩ measure 0 half the time, so the probabilities alone cannot tell them apart.
  4. Prepare |+⟩ and press Measure: the arrow jumps to |0⟩ or |1⟩ — the collapse — and the probabilities become certain. Press |+⟩ and Measure again a few times to watch the counts approach 50 / 50.
  5. Drag slowly near the north pole and quickly near the equator: the half angle compresses the whole sphere into the amplitudes.

This model shows pure states only. A noisy qubit sits inside the sphere, where the arrow is shorter, and needs a density-matrix view that this tool does not yet provide. Measurements here use your browser's random number generator, so a short run of outcomes can look surprising: that is statistics, not a bias.
